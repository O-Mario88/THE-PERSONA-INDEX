## 1. Faith and belief as identity: meaning, struggle, doubt, leaving, non-religious identities, and religious vs secular countries

### Takeaway
How much a religious identity "pays off" for self-esteem and life satisfaction depends heavily on whether the person's society values religion. People who are firmly religious or firmly non-religious tend to fare better than people who are uncertain or only weakly attached. That points to conviction, fit with one's surroundings, and community as the active ingredients, rather than the content of a belief. Leaving a faith tends to go with lower well-being mainly because it means losing a community. Spiritual struggle goes with worry and low mood.

### Cited Findings
- **Religiosity helps self-esteem only where religion is socially valued [moderate].** NEW. In 187,957 users of a European dating site in 11 countries, ranging from Sweden (least religious) to Poland (devoutly Catholic), more religious people overall had higher social self-esteem and better adjustment. These benefits appeared only in countries that value religiosity. For example, a very religious person might have high social self-esteem in Poland but not in Sweden. The authors' explanation: "you only pat yourself on the back for being religious if you live in a social system that values religiosity." Caveat: the sample came from a dating site and was European only. — [ScienceDaily on Gebauer, Sedikides & Neberich 2012, *Psychological Science*](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/01/120120010447.htm)
- **The same pattern replicated at very large scale worldwide [strong].** NEW. The "religiosity-as-social-value" hypothesis says religiosity boosts self-esteem in religious cultures but does little, or even reduces it, in secular ones. Three replication studies tested it:
  - self-reports in 65 countries (N = 2,195,301)
  - informant reports in 36 countries (N = 560,264)
  - self-reports from 1,932 urban areas in 243 federal states of 18 countries (N = 1,188,536)

  The authors tested seven alternative explanations and report that the results "fully and firmly replicated and extended prior evidence." The abstract I could open gives no effect sizes. Caveats: these are online convenience samples, not representative ones, but they cover many non-Western countries. The page lists the paper as accepted by *JPSP* in April 2016. — [Gebauer, Sedikides, Schönbrodt, Bleidorn, Rentfrow, Potter & Gosling, Southampton ePrints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/393348)
- **Being non-religious costs life satisfaction only in religious societies, and the link is not a straight line [moderate].** NEW. In World Values Survey data from 24 countries (N = 33,879), the authors accounted for how well each person's (non-)religiosity fit their country's norms. Once they did, highly religious people, non-religious people and self-identified atheists did not differ in life satisfaction. Weakly religious people were significantly less satisfied than highly religious people. Non-religious and atheist identity went with lower life satisfaction only in religious societies, and atheists' well-being depended on how many other secular people lived around them. The authors conclude that religiosity "does not universally benefit subjective well-being independent of societal context." — [Pöhls, Schlösser & Fetchenhauer 2020, *Journal of Happiness Studies* 21:2327–2353, Tilburg repository](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/nonreligious-identities-and-life-satisfaction-questioning-the-uni/); [University of Cologne summary](https://dss.uni-koeln.de/en/research/did-you-know-that/singlepage/wussten-sie-schon-dass-religioese-menschen-nicht-generell-zufriedener-mit-ihrem-leben-sind-als-nicht-religioese-1-1)
- **Certainty of worldview, whatever its content, tracks well-being in a U-shape [moderate, secondary summary; mostly ⚠W].** NEW, via an ISSEP research feature that summarises these studies:
  - Galen & Kloet (2011): comparing church members with organised non-believers, life satisfaction and emotional stability formed a curve across belief certainty, with both extremes doing better than uncertainty.
  - Galen et al. (2015): members of secular groups had positive outcomes indistinguishable from their religious peers.
  - Moore & Leach (2016, review): "dozens" of studies show a U-shaped link between how strongly people hold a worldview and well-being.
  - Park (2019): all four papers that tested for a curve found one.
  - Karim & Saroglou (2023): well-being was lowest among agnostics and "spiritual but not religious" people, and highest among atheists and the religious.

  The summary gives no sample sizes. — [ISSEP, "Nonreligious Worldviews and the Benefits of Existential Certainty"](https://www.issep.org/features/nonreligious-worldviews-and-existential-certainty)
- **Strongly held worldviews help the person but can cost relationships [early, secondary].** NEW. The same summary reports:
  - Womick et al. (2021): fixed values gave more sense of meaning but led people to devalue others with different views.
  - Van Tongeren (2023): strong worldview beliefs help personally but may hurt getting along with others.
  - Schnell & Keenan (2011): atheists committed to secular humanism did not show lower well-being or a crisis of meaning.
  - Charles et al. (2021): participants in Sunday Assembly, a secular congregation, reported more social communion and positive affect.

  — [ISSEP feature](https://www.issep.org/features/nonreligious-worldviews-and-existential-certainty)
- **Leaving one's religion goes with poorer well-being, explained by losing attendance [moderate; US only ⚠W].** NEW. In pooled US General Social Survey data from 1973 to 2012, people who left the religion they were raised in reported poorer health and lower well-being than both the consistently affiliated and the consistently unaffiliated. The gap was fully accounted for by their lower church attendance. The authors conclude that the social processes around leaving matter. — [Fenelon & Danielsen 2016, *Social Science Research* 57:49–62, Penn State repository](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/leaving-my-religion-understanding-the-relationship-between-religi/)
- **Spiritual struggle and doubt go with distress [strong].** LIB `cheng2023`, not re-opened. Across 42 studies in 24 countries early in the pandemic, people whose faith brought struggle and doubt reported more worry and low mood. Seeking comfort in faith showed no overall link. — [Cheng & Ying 2023](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1097598/text)
- **Context for "leaving" [moderate].** LIB `pew2025switching`, not re-opened. Across 36 countries, leaving one's childhood religion ranged from rare (95% or more stay in India, Israel, Nigeria and Thailand) to common (half of adults in South Korea). — [Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/03/26/around-the-world-many-people-are-leaving-their-childhood-religions/)
- **Practice and community, not label [moderate].** LIB `pew2019religion`, not re-opened. Across 26 countries, regular worship attenders were more often very happy than others; the library notes that simply identifying with a religion shows little link. — [Pew 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2019/01/31/religions-relationship-to-happiness-civic-engagement-and-health-around-the-world/)

### Inferences
- The same belief can feel like a source of security and belonging in one setting and a social cost in another (Gebauer 2012, 2016; Pöhls 2020). For self-reflection, the useful question is "how does holding this belief feel where I live and among my people?", not "is belief good for me?"
- Being "in between" (weakly religious, agnostic, spiritual but not religious, or in doubt) goes with lower well-being on average (Pöhls 2020; Karim & Saroglou via ISSEP; Cheng 2023). This is an average across people and does not show that being in between causes harm. It may often be a transition. An app must not frame doubt as a problem to fix, or push people toward certainty in either direction.
- Leaving a faith seems to hurt mainly through losing community (Fenelon 2016). That links the identity to the craving for belonging more than to belief itself.
- A strong worldview gives the person meaning but can reduce warmth toward people who think differently (Womick 2021; Van Tongeren 2023). That ties belief identity to the craving for faith in people.

### Gaps
- I could not open effect sizes for the 65-country Gebauer paper; PubMed and the University of Melbourne pages were blocked.
- I found no opened source on leaving a faith outside the US. Fenelon & Danielsen is GSS data. A European study on religious context and health appeared in search results (Huijts & Kraaykamp, Maastricht) but was not opened.
- The certainty findings come via a secondary summary with no sample sizes; most original samples are US or European.
- I found nothing opened on religious identity in Muslim-majority, Hindu, Buddhist or African contexts specifically, beyond the cross-national datasets above.

## 2. Political identity: affective polarisation, misperceiving the other side, news and mood, living with politics

### Takeaway
Political identity increasingly works as a social identity. Disliking the other side spills into friendships, family time and stress. Much of that dislike rests on exaggerated pictures of who the other side is and what they think of us. Correcting those pictures warms feelings a little. Avoiding the news is now common worldwide and is driven by feeling powerless and by toxic arguments.

### Cited Findings
- **Partisanship as social identity [strong as a review; US ⚠W].** NEW, abstract only. Ordinary Americans increasingly dislike and distrust members of the other party, describing them as hypocritical, selfish and closed-minded. They are unwilling to socialise across party lines, and this feeling shapes attitudes and behaviour "well outside the political sphere." The authors attribute it to partisanship operating as a social identity. — [Iyengar, Lelkes, Levendusky, Malhotra & Westwood 2019, *Annual Review of Political Science* 22:129–146](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-polisci-051117-073034)
- **This is not universal across rich democracies [moderate].** LIB `boxell2024`, not re-opened. Over four decades in 12 rich countries, dislike of the other side rose most in the US, rose less in five countries and fell in six. — [Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v106y2024i2p557-565.html)
- **We think the other side hates us about twice as much as it does [moderate; US].** NEW. The study used a representative US sample (n = 1,056) and a longitudinal sample (n = 2,707):
  - Democrats and Republicans dislike and dehumanise each other to about the same degree.
  - Each side believed the other's prejudice and dehumanisation were "approximately twice as strong" as they actually were.
  - The overestimate was largest among strong partisans.
  - These exaggerated beliefs independently predicted wanting social distance and supporting norm-breaking policies.

  The study is correlational. — [Moore-Berg, Ankori-Karlinsky, Hameiri & Bruneau 2020, *PNAS* 117(26):14864–72](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/exaggerated-meta-perceptions-predict-intergroup-hostility-between/)
- **Replicated beyond the US [strong].** LIB `ruggeri2021`, not re-opened. In 26 countries, people believed the other political side saw them far more negatively than it really did, and learning the other side's real views made them more accurate. — [Ruggeri et al. 2021](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-general-fault-in-our-fault-lines/)
- **"The parties in our heads" [moderate; US].** NEW. In a 2015 YouGov survey (n = 1,000), people hugely overestimated how many party supporters belong to stereotyped groups:

  | Group | Believed share | Actual share |
  |---|---|---|
  | Gay, lesbian or bisexual, among Democrats | 31.7% | 6.3% |
  | Atheist or agnostic, among Democrats | 28.7% | 8.7% |
  | Union members, among Democrats | 39.3% | 10.5% |
  | Earning over $250,000, among Republicans | 38.2% | 2.2% |
  | Aged 65 and over, among Republicans | 39.1% | 21.3% |

  The average overestimate was about 342%, larger for the other party. More interest in political news went with more bias for seven of the eight group–party pairs. In a California poll (1,815 partisans), more bias went with more social distance and seeing the other side as more extreme. Telling people the real makeup of the other party:
  - made them rate it 6.4 points warmer (out of 100)
  - reduced social distance by 2.5 points
  - cut errors in their estimates from 27.7 to 6.1 points

  The authors call these effects "relatively small." The PDF opened is the 2017 working version; a published version is listed on the University of Chicago Press journals site. — [Ahler & Sood, "The Parties in Our Heads" (PDF)](https://gsood.com/research/papers/partisanComposition.pdf)
- **People feel politics harms their lives [early; single survey, perceptions only; US].** NEW. In a 2017 YouGov survey (n = 800):
  - nearly 40% said politics stresses them out
  - about 1 in 5 had lost sleep or felt fatigue over it
  - 29.3% had lost their temper
  - about 20% said it had damaged friendships
  - 22.1% said they care too much about who wins
  - 4% reported suicidal thoughts linked to politics
  - 11.5% said it harmed their physical health

  The authors stress these are perceptions, not proven effects, from the first study of its kind. A 2022 follow-up exists, but its page was blocked. — [University of Nebraska news on Smith, Hibbing & Hibbing 2019, *PLOS ONE*](https://news.unl.edu/article/stressed-out-americans-making-themselves-sick-over-politics)
- **Relationship costs [moderate].**
  - LIB `chen2018`, not re-opened: phone location data showed Thanksgiving visits between homes in opposite-voting areas were 30–50 minutes shorter after 2016. — [Chen & Rohla 2018](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2018/05/31/researchers-document-another-cost-2016-election-shorter-thanksgiving-visits/)
  - LIB `pew2018politics`, not re-opened: 53% of Americans found political talk with people who disagree stressful, and 63% came away feeling they had less in common than they thought. — [Pew 2018](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2018/11/05/more-now-say-its-stressful-to-discuss-politics-with-people-they-disagree-with/)
  - LIB `pew2016social`, not re-opened: 37% of social media users felt worn out by political posts. — [Pew 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2016/10/25/the-political-environment-on-social-media/)
- **News avoidance is common worldwide and rising [moderate].** LIB `reuters2025news`, re-opened.
  - Across 48 markets, 40% sometimes or often avoid the news, up from 29% in 2017.
  - Highest: Bulgaria 63%, Turkey 61%, Croatia 61%, Greece 60%. Lowest: Japan 11%, Taiwan 21%.
  - Reasons named in the text: feeling powerless (about issues such as economic insecurity and climate change), news seeming irrelevant, news leading to toxic arguments, and feeling overwhelmed. The percentages are only in charts. Under-35s cite powerlessness, irrelevance and arguments more.
  - Trust in news is 40% overall, ranging from Finland 67% to Hungary and Greece 22%.
  - Outlets respond with explainers, constructive or hope-focused journalism, and filters. Svenska Dagbladet's Kompakt app lets users filter out difficult stories.

  — [Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary)
- **What reduces hostility.**
  - **[strong]** LIB `pettigrew2008`, not re-opened: across 515 studies in 38 nations, contact with other groups went with less prejudice. — [Pettigrew & Tropp 2008](https://courses.washington.edu/pbafhall/563/Readings/pettigrew.pdf)
  - **[moderate]** LIB `kalla2020`, not re-opened: non-judgemental exchange of personal stories reduced prejudice for months. — [Kalla & Broockman 2020](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps20-01)
  - **[strong]** LIB `nyhan2023`, not re-opened: cutting like-minded Facebook content by a third did not reduce polarisation. — [Nyhan et al. 2023](https://www.maxwell.syr.edu/research/article/like-minded-sources-on-facebook-are-prevalent-but-not-polarizing)

### Inferences
- Political identity hurts most through what people imagine about the other side. Both "they hate us" and "they're all extreme" are measurably exaggerated (Moore-Berg 2020; Ahler & Sood; Ruggeri 2021). A neutral, side-free form of self-knowledge is noticing when you are reacting to the imagined other side rather than a real person. That links directly to the craving for **faith in people**.
- Following the news more goes with more distorted pictures of the other side (Ahler & Sood). Together with the powerlessness and toxic-argument reasons for avoidance (Reuters 2025), this suggests a person's news *rhythm*, not their politics, is a legitimate and neutral thing to reflect on. That links to **freedom** (choosing one's intake), **security** and **contentment**.
- Correcting misperceptions has only small effects (Ahler & Sood). Insights should not promise that knowing better will heal divides.

### Gaps
- I did not open the full text of Iyengar 2019, so I have no figures on cross-party marriage, dating or family. The review's reference list names Huber & Malhotra 2017 and Iyengar, Konitzer & Tedin 2018, but I did not open them.
- The 2022 Smith & Hibbing follow-up was blocked, so whether political stress persisted after the 2020 change of president is unverified.
- Almost all the evidence on polarisation and well-being comes from the US. Outside the US I have only Ruggeri 2021 and Boxell 2024 (library). No opened source covers political identity and well-being in Africa, South Asia or Latin America.
- I found no study on how people successfully keep politics from taking over, such as compartmentalising or limiting it, beyond news avoidance and contact research.

## 3. Place and home: place attachment, belonging, rural vs urban, home as identity, displacement

### Takeaway
Feeling attached to a place (a home, a town, a landscape) works much like an attachment to a person. It offers security, belonging, memories, a sense of control and meaning. Even recalling an attached place can raise self-esteem, belonging and meaning in experiments. The benefit depends on social ties, and attachment can deepen distress when the place is threatened or changed. Evidence is thin and mostly North American.

### Cited Findings
- **What attached places give people [early; Canada ⚠W].** NEW. A three-study PhD:
  - Study 1 found 13 benefit categories in people's own descriptions of places they feel attached to: memories, belonging, relaxation, positive emotions, support for activities, comfort and security, self-growth, freedom and control, entertainment, connection to nature, practical benefits, privacy and aesthetics.
  - Study 2: after a threat, people became faster at recognising words about their attached place, suggesting a security function. This happened only for attached places.
  - Study 3: a visualisation of an attached place raised self-esteem, meaning, belonging and sense of control, but also negative feeling. It did so only for people without an avoidant style of place attachment.

  The abstract gives no sample sizes. — [Scannell 2013, PhD, University of Victoria](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/44cfd21e-60da-47ff-87a2-0bd4ecf6149b/full)
- **Attached places and meaning in life [early; university samples ⚠W].** NEW.
  - Two experiments (n = 200 and n = 274): recalling an attached place, rather than a neutral one, raised the sense that life makes sense (coherence) and the presence of meaning.
  - A one-week daily-tracking study (n = 348) found people were at home 53.5% of the time. Being in attached places went with more coherence and significance, but not more purpose.
  - A pre-post experiment (n = 472) confirmed higher meaning after recalling a strongly attached place, compared with a neutral or negatively attached one.

  The author suggests home works as a "safe haven" or "secure base" rather than a place for pursuing purpose. The feature also cites Scannell & Gifford (2017), where visualising an attached place raised meaning, belonging and self-esteem, and attached places gave security and the courage to explore. Caveat: this is a researcher's feature article, and its publication status is not stated. — [Krause 2023, ISSEP feature](https://www.issep.org/features/place-attachment)
- **Place attachment and well-being: mostly positive, but conditional [early; student thesis].** NEW. A bachelor's-thesis systematic review of 13 studies since 2015 (PRISMA method) found:
  - higher place attachment is mostly linked to more life satisfaction, better mental health and a stronger sense of belonging
  - but after disasters, pollution or urban transformation, attachment can deepen distress
  - social relationships, social capital and perceived support carry the link
  - attachment alone does not guarantee well-being

  — [Beautell Sánchez 2025, Universidad de La Laguna](https://riull.ull.es/xmlui/handle/915/42464?show=full)
- **The neighbourhood as a social place [moderate].**
  - LIB `kim2014`, not re-opened: US adults over 50 who saw their neighbourhood as close-knit and trusting were less likely to have a heart attack over 4 years. — [Kim, Hawes & Smith 2014](https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/bibcite/reference/12901)
  - LIB `sandstrom2014`, not re-opened: days with more chats with casual acquaintances brought more happiness and belonging. — [Sandstrom & Dunn 2014](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/15300/)
  - LIB `gallup2023social`, not re-opened: across 142 countries, 46% talked with a neighbour daily. — [Gallup & Meta 2023](https://gallup.com/file/analytics/513347/Gallup-Meta-Global%20State%20of%20Social%20Connections%20Report-2023.pdf)
  - LIB `cruwys2013`, not re-opened: belonging to more groups went with less later low mood. — [Cruwys et al. 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/socmed/v98y2013icp179-186.html)

### Inferences
- Place attachment maps onto several core cravings at once: **security** (safe haven, threat response), **belonging**, **freedom** (Scannell's "freedom–control" category and the "courage to explore") and **contentment** (relaxation, positive emotions). That makes "where do I feel most myself?" a rich and culturally neutral reflection theme.
- Because attachment can deepen distress when a place changes or is lost (Beautell 2025), insights for people who have moved, migrated or been displaced should acknowledge grief and not just praise rootedness.
- Calling to mind an attached place is a low-cost practice with early experimental support (Scannell 2013; Krause 2023). It is reasonable as an optional small step, but not as a claimed fix.

### Gaps
- **Displacement:** I opened no study on forced displacement, migration or the loss of home and well-being. A New Zealand refugee community report appeared in search results but was not opened. This needs another researcher or a later pass.
- **Rural vs urban identity:** no opened source.
- **Home as identity beyond place attachment:** no opened source.
- I opened no peer-reviewed meta-analysis of place attachment and well-being; the review I found is a bachelor's thesis. The evidence is mostly North American students.

## 4. Nature: nature connectedness and well-being

### Takeaway
Feeling that nature is part of who you are goes with modestly higher happiness, especially vitality. The size is r ≈ .19 across 8,523 people. The evidence is correlational and mostly Canadian and US. Separately, spending time in nature (about 2 hours a week) goes with better well-being.

### Cited Findings
- **Nature connectedness and happiness [strong as a meta-analysis; ⚠W].** NEW. The meta-analysis pooled 30 samples from 21 studies (N = 8,523) and found a small positive correlation:
  - overall: r = .19 (fixed effects, 95% CI .16–.21); r = .18 (random effects)
  - vitality: r = .24
  - positive feeling: r = .22
  - life satisfaction: r = .17
  - the "inclusion of nature in self" measure, which is closest to identity, showed the strongest link (r = .27)
  - publication status, year, age and gender did not moderate the link

  Samples came from Canada (46.7%), the US (20%), Europe (10%), Asia (3.3%) and mixed locations (10%), and all studies were in English. The authors caution that correlations do not show "if one variable causes the other." — [Capaldi, Dopko & Zelenski 2014, *Frontiers in Psychology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00976/full)
- **Time in nature [moderate].** LIB `white2019`, not re-opened. People who spent at least two hours a week in nature were more likely to report good health and well-being; the benefit levelled off at around 3–5 hours. — [White et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-44097-3)
- **Exercise in green space [moderate; UK ⚠W].** LIB `barton2010`, not re-opened. Across 10 UK studies (N = 1,252), exercising in green spaces lifted mood and self-esteem, most after short sessions. — [Barton & Pretty 2010](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/683/)

### Inferences
- Of all the measures, seeing nature as part of oneself shows the largest link (r = .27). So the identity angle ("I'm someone who notices and cares about the outdoors") may matter beyond simple exposure. That is my inference from the subgroup result, not a tested causal claim.
- Nature connects most to **contentment** and **happiness** (vitality, positive feeling) and, via Scannell's "connection to nature" place benefit, to **security**. Noticing nature needs no belief, politics or money, which suits a worldwide app.

### Gaps
- I opened no nature-connectedness evidence from Africa, South Asia or Latin America; only 3.3% of Capaldi's samples were Asian.
- I found no opened causal or intervention meta-analysis on nature-noticing exercises. There are later meta-analyses (for example on eudaimonic well-being), but they were not found within budget.

## 5. Leisure identities: hobbies, serious leisure, sport and fandom, music and arts

### Takeaway
Having a hobby goes with small but consistent gains in life satisfaction and fewer depressive symptoms in older adults across 16 countries, including China and Japan. Deep, effortful "serious" leisure goes with well-being largely because people find it satisfying. Identifying with a sports team goes with feeling more socially connected (r ≈ .33–.54), and a team's wins briefly lift fans' self-esteem. The same identification breeds bias against rival fans.

### Cited Findings
- **Hobbies across 16 countries [moderate; includes China and Japan].** LIB `mak2023`, re-opened. The study followed 93,263 adults aged 65 and over in 16 countries (Austria, Belgium, China, Czech Republic, Denmark, England, Estonia, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, USA) over three survey waves, about 4–8 years.
  - Hobby rates ranged from 96% in Denmark and 90% in Japan to 54% in Italy, 51% in Spain and 37.6% in China (where only social hobbies were measured).
  - Standardised fixed-effects estimates: depressive symptoms −0.10, self-reported health +0.06, happiness +0.09, life satisfaction +0.10.
  - A hobby at one wave predicted outcomes at the next to a similar degree.
  - Country explained less than 9% of the variance.
  - The authors state "causality cannot be established."

  — [Mak et al. 2023, *Nature Medicine*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-023-02506-1)
- **Serious leisure [early; China, single cross-sectional study].** NEW. In 447 Chinese marathon runners:
  - serious leisure correlated r = .46 with life satisfaction (β = .445)
  - satisfaction with the leisure itself fully carried the link
  - running-group membership strengthened the path from serious leisure to leisure satisfaction

  The serious-leisure scale used (SLIM) includes an "identity" dimension, but identity was not analysed separately. The sample was highly educated (84.4% college or above) and the design cross-sectional. Stebbins' original definition was not opened in this session. — [Tian et al. 2020, *Frontiers in Psychology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.581908/full)
- **Fan identity and belonging [moderate; undergraduates ⚠W].** NEW. The APS Observer reports Wann's 2006 studies (*Journal of Sport Behavior*) of hundreds of undergraduate fans:
  - higher team identification went with lower alienation and loneliness, and higher collective self-esteem and positive emotion (correlational)
  - group affiliation is a top reason for following a team
  - about 95% of fans attend games with friends

  — [APS Observer, "Sports Complex: The Science Behind Fanatic Behavior"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/sports-complex-the-science-behind-fanatic-behavior)
- **Does the team need to be local? [early].** NEW. Wann's model says fans gain social connectedness only from local teams. Two studies found no support for that:
  - team identification correlated with social connectedness at r = .334 (n = 291 undergraduates) and r = .542 (n = 432 online fans)
  - whether the team was local did not change the link

  The authors speculate that social media and streaming let distant fans connect. — [Schellenberg & Gaudreau 2021, *Journal of Exercise, Movement, and Sport*](https://scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2628)
- **The team as an extension of the self [moderate; classic experiment].** NEW, via the Observer. Highly identified basketball fans who watched their team win, compared with lose, rated their own future task performance and their self-esteem higher. The boost was similar to that from personal success; one of the researchers put it as "The team is an extension of the self." — [APS Observer on Hirt, Zillmann, Erickson & Kennedy 1992, *JPSP*](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/sports-complex-the-science-behind-fanatic-behavior)
- **Fandom divides [early-moderate].** NEW, via the Observer. Fans leaving a game (n = 148) rated the opposing fans' behaviour as worse than their own side's, especially winning fans: in-group bias. When teams lose, fans may see referees and opponents in a biased way. Rather than distancing themselves, fans "shift expectations." — [APS Observer on Wann & Grieve 2005, *Journal of Social Psychology*](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/sports-complex-the-science-behind-fanatic-behavior)
- **Playing sport [strong].** LIB `eime2013`, not re-opened. Across 30 studies, young people who played sport had higher self-esteem, better social skills and less low mood, with team sports apparently more helpful. — [Eime et al. 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1479-5868-10-98)
- **Enjoyment keeps people going [strong].** LIB `teixeira2012`, not re-opened. Across 66 studies, people who exercise because they enjoy it were more likely to keep it up long term. — [Teixeira et al. 2012](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8677w/exercise-physical-activity-and-self-determination-theory-a-systematic-review)
- **Music and arts [moderate].** All LIB, not re-opened.
  - `fancourt2019arts`: a WHO review of 3,000+ studies found arts participation supports health and well-being. — [Fancourt & Finn 2019](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789289054553)
  - `greenberg2022`: music taste lines up with personality similarly across 50+ countries (350,000+ people). — [Greenberg et al. 2022](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/musical-preferences-unite-personalities-worldwide)
  - `sachs2015`: many people find sad music comforting. — [Sachs et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00404/full)
- **Games [moderate].** LIB `przybylski2010` and `halbrook2019`, not re-opened. Games pull people in when they make players feel capable, free and connected, and whether gaming helps depends on why people play and whether they play with others. — [Przybylski et al. 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1037/a0019440); [Halbrook et al. 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691619863807)

### Inferences
- Leisure identities supply **success** (skill and mastery in serious leisure; reflected glory in fandom), **belonging** (fan communities, running groups, choirs), **contentment** and **happiness** (hobbies) and **freedom** (chosen, self-directed activity).
- Fandom shows in miniature how any group identity frees and divides at once. It brings connection and a lift after wins, and also bias against rivals and moods tied to results the fan does not control. Sport is a low-stakes, culturally universal way to reflect on group-identity dynamics without touching religion or politics. That is my inference.
- Satisfaction with the leisure carried the serious-leisure benefit (Tian 2020), and enjoyment predicts sticking with exercise (Teixeira 2012). Together they suggest reflecting on whether a hobby is still enjoyed, not just pursued.

### Gaps
- I opened no primary Stebbins source and no meta-analysis of serious leisure.
- I opened no large study of fandom and well-being outside the US and Canada, and no evidence on harms after losses (such as mood or violence) beyond in-group bias.
- I opened no new source on music or arts as an *identity* (for example "I am a musician") as opposed to participation.

## 6. Online identity: self-presentation, online communities, and online vs offline self

### Takeaway
Presenting oneself more authentically on social media goes with higher well-being. In a small two-week experiment, posting authentically lifted mood more than posting a polished version of oneself. Screen time itself explains very little of well-being. Online spaces can create friendships and fan connections, but I found no opened evidence on online communities and belonging specifically.

### Cited Findings
- **Authentic vs idealised self-presentation [moderate].** NEW.
  - Among 10,560 Facebook users (myPersonality app, 2007–2012), the smaller the gap between people's self-rated personality and the personality predicted from their likes and posts, the higher their life satisfaction.
  - In a within-person experiment (n = 90), after a week of posting authentically rather than a week of self-idealised posting, people reported higher well-being, mood and positive affect. Life satisfaction did not change.

  Caveats: the large study is correlational, self-idealising motives were not measured, it is unclear whether authentic posting beats not posting, and participants' countries were not stated. — [Bailey, Matz, Youyou & Iyengar 2020, *Nature Communications* (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/f0311e5d5abd44b8833222bc3580802f); [BPS Research Digest summary](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/being-more-authentic-social-media-could-improve-your-wellbeing)
- **Screen time is a weak signal [strong].** LIB `orben2019`, not re-opened. In data from 355,358 teens, screen use explained at most 0.4% of the differences in well-being. — [Orben & Przybylski 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1)
- **Stepping away [moderate].** LIB `allcott2020`, not re-opened. People who stopped using Facebook for four weeks felt a little happier, spent more time with family and friends, and were slightly less polarised, but knew less news. — [Allcott et al. 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20190658)
- **Seeing others' polished lives [moderate].** LIB `jordan2011`, not re-opened. People underestimate how often others feel bad; those who did so most felt lonelier and less satisfied. — [Jordan et al. 2011](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167210390822)
- **Online friendship [moderate; US].** LIB `pew2024teengames`, not re-opened. 47% of US teen game players had made a friend through a game. — [Pew 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/05/09/teens-and-video-games-today/)
- **Online fandom [early, authors' speculation].** NEW. Schellenberg & Gaudreau suggest social media and streaming let distant fans build connection. This was not tested. — [Schellenberg & Gaudreau 2021](https://scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2628)

### Inferences
- The gap between the online and offline self is a natural, neutral place for self-knowledge: "did what I shared today look like my actual day?" It links to **freedom** (being oneself), **contentment** and **belonging** (being known as one is).
- Because underestimating others' struggles goes with loneliness (Jordan 2011), noticing that others' feeds are curated may protect contentment. This combines two findings and has not been tested as an intervention.

### Gaps
- I opened no study on online communities as a source of belonging, for example for minorities, isolated people or diasporas.
- I opened no non-Western evidence on self-presentation and well-being; Bailey's participants' countries are not stated.
- I opened no evidence on anonymous or pseudonymous identities.

## 7. Cross-cutting: how these identities divide or free people, and the links to the core cravings

### Takeaway
Every identity in this set does two things at once. It gives meaning, security and belonging to the people inside it. It can also distort how they see people outside it, and its payoff depends on whether the surroundings value it. The most consistent, side-free levers for self-knowledge across the evidence are:
- **Fit:** how the identity feels where one lives.
- **Conviction vs ambivalence.**
- **The community the identity brings.**
- **Accuracy about the "other side".**

### Cited Findings
- **Fit with surroundings shapes the payoff.** Gebauer 2012 and 2016 (religiosity and self-esteem) and Pöhls 2020 (atheists' life satisfaction depends on how secular their country is). — [Gebauer 2016](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/393348); [Pöhls 2020](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/nonreligious-identities-and-life-satisfaction-questioning-the-uni/)
- **Community, more than label, carries the benefit.** Leaving religion hurts via lost attendance (Fenelon & Danielsen 2016). Place attachment's benefit runs through social ties (Beautell 2025). Fans gain connectedness, and about 95% attend with friends (Wann, via APS Observer). — [Fenelon](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/leaving-my-religion-understanding-the-relationship-between-religi/); [Beautell](https://riull.ull.es/xmlui/handle/915/42464?show=full); [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/sports-complex-the-science-behind-fanatic-behavior)
- **Identity can bias how we see others.**
  - Partisans think the other side's hostility is about double what it is (Moore-Berg 2020) and overestimate its stereotyped groups by about 342% (Ahler & Sood).
  - Fans judge rival fans more harshly (Wann & Grieve 2005).
  - Fixed values go with devaluing people who differ (Womick 2021, via ISSEP).

  — [Moore-Berg](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/exaggerated-meta-perceptions-predict-intergroup-hostility-between/); [Ahler & Sood](https://gsood.com/research/papers/partisanComposition.pdf); [ISSEP](https://www.issep.org/features/nonreligious-worldviews-and-existential-certainty)
- **Accurate information warms feelings a little.** Learning the other party's real makeup raised warmth by 6.4 points (Ahler & Sood), and learning real views improved accuracy in 26 countries (LIB `ruggeri2021`, not re-opened). — [Ahler & Sood](https://gsood.com/research/papers/partisanComposition.pdf); [Ruggeri 2021](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-general-fault-in-our-fault-lines/)
- **Hope.** LIB `counted2025`, not re-opened; hope is covered by another researcher. Across 22 countries, people attending worship more than weekly rated their hope about 8.7 out of 10, versus 7.8 for those who never attend. Separately, feeling powerless is a named reason people avoid the news (Reuters 2025, re-opened). — [Global Flourishing Study](https://globalflourishingstudy.com/paper/where-hope-lives-who-stays-hopeful-around-the-world); [Reuters 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary)

### Inferences
- A single, respectful framing works across all six domains: "What does this part of you give you, and what does it ask of you?" It is neutral about content and invites reflection on fit, community and how one sees outsiders.
- Insights about "the other side", whether political, religious or rival fans, are where self-knowledge most directly serves faith in people. The effects are small, so the wording should be modest.

### Gaps
- No opened study directly tests whether reflecting on one's own identities (self-knowledge) increases freedom, security, hope, connection or contentment. The links above are drawn from correlational and experimental findings about the identities themselves.

## 8. What this means for the app

### Takeaway
These identities can be reflected on safely and honestly if the app:
- asks about *everyday behaviour and feelings* around belief, news, place, outdoors, hobbies, teams and posting, never about *which* belief or party;
- frames insights around fit, community, conviction vs ambivalence, and how one pictures others;
- offers small, evidence-backed steps: recalling an attached place, noticing nature, a chosen news rhythm, a hobby touchpoint, posting as one is, and checking one's picture of "the other side".

### Everyday situations that reveal how someone holds these identities
All are concrete-choice questions, never rating scales or agree/disagree. Each sits next to the evidence it draws on.

**Faith and belief.** These avoid asking what the person believes.
- *Evening:* "Something worrying came up today. What did you do first?" Options: a prayer or quiet ritual / talked to someone / made a plan / went outside / distracted myself / nothing yet.
  - Rituals help many people feel steadier (LIB `hobson2018`). Any practice counts, religious or not.
- *Morning:* "A holiday or tradition from your upbringing is coming up. How are you marking it?" Options: joining fully / joining for family / my own way / not this year / it's not part of my life.
  - Fit and community carry the benefit (Gebauer; Pöhls; Fenelon).
- *Evening:* "Someone asked about your beliefs today, or you thought about them. How did it feel?" Options: easy / a bit guarded / I changed the subject / it didn't come up.
  - Fit with surroundings (Gebauer 2016; Pöhls 2020).

**Politics, with no side ever named.**
- *Morning:* "First thing today, did you check the news?" Options: before getting up / with breakfast / later on purpose / skipped it / it found me through notifications.
  - Reuters 2025 on avoidance; Ahler & Sood on news interest and misperception.
- *Evening:* "A relative or friend posted something political you disagree with. What did you do?" Options: replied / messaged them privately / muted / let it go / asked what they meant / talked about something else with them.
  - Pew 2016 and Chen 2018 (LIB); Kalla 2020 (LIB) on personal exchange.
- *Evening:* "When you pictured people on the other side today, who came to mind?" Options: someone I actually know / a public figure / a crowd online / I didn't think about it.
  - Moore-Berg 2020; Ahler & Sood.

**Place and home.**
- *Evening:* "Where today did you feel most like yourself?" Options: home / a street or café / outdoors / someone else's home / a place of worship or gathering / nowhere really.
  - Scannell 2013; Krause 2023.
- *Morning:* "Someone asks 'where are you from?' What do you say first?" Options: where I live / where I grew up / where my family is from / 'it's complicated'.
  - Home and origin as identity; open, with no judgement.

**Nature.**
- *Evening:* "Did anything outdoors catch your attention today?" Options: sky or weather / trees or plants / an animal or birds / water / nothing / I wasn't outside.
  - Capaldi 2014; White 2019 (LIB).

**Leisure and fandom.**
- *Evening:* "In your free hour today, what did you mostly do?" Options: something I'm getting better at / something with others / rested / scrolled / chores / had no free hour.
  - Mak 2023; Tian 2020.
- *Morning:* "Your team or favourite player lost last night. This morning you…" Options: avoided the results / went over it with other fans / felt it all morning / shrugged it off / don't follow a team.
  - Hirt 1992; Wann & Grieve 2005; Schellenberg 2021.

**Online.**
- *Evening:* "You shared something online today. How close was it to how your day really was?" Options: very close / a nicer version / only the highlight / I didn't share anything.
  - Bailey 2020.

### What an insight can honestly say
Each insight is one sentence, about the person's own pattern plus a general finding, never a comparison with others.
- "You turned to a set practice when things got tense. Rituals, religious or everyday, help many people feel more in control." Drawn from `hobson2018` (LIB).
- "Whether a belief feels like support or strain often depends on whether the people around you share it. That's about fit, not about you being right or wrong." Drawn from Gebauer 2016 and Pöhls 2020.
- "Feeling in between, unsure, or changing in what you believe is something many people go through, and it can feel unsettling. Being unsure isn't the same as being lost." Drawn from Cheng 2023 (LIB) and ISSEP. Stated gently. Never say that certainty is the goal.
- "People on every side tend to think the other side dislikes them about twice as much as it really does." Drawn from Moore-Berg 2020 and Ruggeri 2021 (LIB). It is symmetrical, so it takes no side.
- "You checked the news before getting up three mornings this week. Many people now choose when news fits into their day." Drawn from Reuters 2025. Describe the pattern and the choice; don't label the person an avoider.
- "The place you named shows up often when you feel most yourself. Places we're attached to tend to give a sense of security and belonging." Drawn from Scannell 2013 and Krause 2023 (early evidence; keep it tentative).
- "You noticed something outdoors on most evenings. Feeling connected to nature goes with feeling more energetic." Drawn from Capaldi 2014: vitality, r = .24, correlational.
- "Your free hours often went to something you're getting better at. Hobbies are linked with more life satisfaction in many countries." Drawn from Mak 2023.
- "Following a team links you to other people. Those links tend to matter more than the score." Drawn from Schellenberg 2021 and Wann 2006.
- "On days you posted closer to your real day, you also chose brighter evening answers." Use this only if the person's own data show it; Bailey 2020 is the background.

### Small steps the evidence supports
Each step is optional, small and concrete.
- **Recall an attached place.** Spend one minute picturing a place where you feel at home. Early experimental support (Scannell 2013; Krause 2023). Skip it for people whose answers suggest a lost or painful home, since attachment can deepen distress (Beautell 2025).
- **Two minutes noticing nature,** on the way somewhere or from a window. Correlational support (Capaldi 2014); exposure of about 2 hours a week (White 2019, LIB).
- **Pick a news window.** Choose when news enters the day, for example not before getting up, and turn off one alert. Grounded in avoidance being common and news interest going with misperception (Reuters 2025; Ahler & Sood). It is a choice, never a prescription to avoid.
- **Check the picture of the other side.** Before replying to a political post, think of one real person you know who sees it differently. Grounded in Ahler & Sood, Moore-Berg 2020 and contact research (Pettigrew 2008, LIB). Effects are small.
- **Ask, don't argue.** Ask someone what an issue means to them personally. Grounded in Kalla & Broockman 2020 (LIB).
- **One touchpoint with a hobby or group,** with others if possible. Grounded in Mak 2023 and Cruwys 2013 (LIB). Enjoyment, not obligation (Teixeira 2012, LIB).
- **Post one thing as it is,** or simply notice the gap. Grounded in Bailey 2020.
- **Mark a tradition your own way,** if the person signals ambivalence about one. Grounded in fit and community findings (Pöhls 2020; Fenelon 2016). The app should never suggest joining or leaving a religion.

### What to avoid
- **Taking sides or ranking beliefs.** Never imply that religious, spiritual, atheist or any political identity is happier, healthier or more correct. The evidence says context, fit and community matter, not content (Gebauer 2016; Pöhls 2020). Never ask which party, candidate or religion.
- **Preaching or "fixing" doubt.** Spiritual struggle goes with distress (Cheng 2023, LIB), but the app is not therapy. Acknowledge it, and do not push toward certainty, leaving or returning.
- **Mocking belief or unbelief,** including in jokes or emoji. Remember that being non-religious carries real social cost in religious societies, and religiosity can be devalued in secular ones (Gebauer 2016; Pöhls 2020).
- **Comparing with others.** Use general findings as "this is common" context only, never "you're more X than most."
- **Overclaiming.** Most findings here are correlational, and correction effects are small (Ahler & Sood). Use words like "often", "tends to" and "for many people."
- **Treating news avoidance as virtue or vice.** Present it as a chosen rhythm (Reuters 2025).
- **Romanticising rootedness.** People who moved, migrated or were displaced may feel loss; attachment can deepen distress (Beautell 2025). Evidence on displacement is a gap.
- **Feeding fandom hostility.** No "rival" framing in sport insights (Wann & Grieve 2005).
- **Inference, not evidence:** religion and political views are highly sensitive personal data in many jurisdictions. Questions should stay behaviour-based, and the app should not store or infer a person's specific belief or party. This needs legal review outside this research scope.

### Cited Findings
See sections 1–7. Every claim in this section draws on sources already cited above; no new sources were introduced here.

### Inferences
- The safest and most useful design pattern is behaviour-first questions, such as what you did, where you were or what you checked, with insights about fit, community and perception. That keeps the app neutral in every culture while still touching the identities that give people meaning.

### Gaps
Evidence gaps still open for this domain:
- displacement and migration
- rural vs urban identity
- online community belonging
- non-Western political identity and well-being
- non-Western nature connectedness
- music or arts as identity
- effect sizes for Gebauer 2016
- full text of Iyengar 2019
- whether the political-stress findings held in the 2022 follow-up
