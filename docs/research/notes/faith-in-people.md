# Restoring faith in other people: misjudged goodness, the cost of cynicism, and rebuilding trust, connection and goodwill

Research pass of 2026-10-24. Grades: **strong** = meta-analysis or widely replicated; **moderate** = solid single studies, or very large but correlational surveys; **early** = small, new or fragile. Source tags: **[LIB:id]** means the source is already in the evidence library (that id is reused) and was re-opened in this session; **[NEW]** means the source is not in the library. Library sources that were *not* re-opened this session are only listed as cross-references under Gaps and are marked as such. **WEIRD** flags findings that rest only on Western, educated, rich samples. Budget used: 21 searches and 40 fetches. PubMed and PMC pages came back empty or behind a captcha, so some exact numbers are missing; these are listed under Gaps.

## 1. How much do people underestimate others' goodness?

### Takeaway
People are consistently too pessimistic about strangers' honesty, kindness and liking, and about how much a small kindness, thank-you or compliment will mean to the person who receives it. The underestimation is large: lost wallets come back roughly 1.8 to 3 or more times as often as people expect. It appears worldwide in the wallet data, but most of the "kindness is undervalued" experiments are WEIRD (mostly US). A key mechanism is that distrust is never tested, so it is never corrected.

### Cited Findings
**Honesty of strangers: lost wallets (worldwide)**
- **Cohn, Maréchal, Tannenbaum & Zünd 2019 (Science) [LIB:cohn2019], moderate: one very large multi-country field experiment.** 17,303 "lost" wallets were handed in at banks, museums, post offices, police stations and similar sites in 355 cities across 40 countries (about 400 per country). Return rates were 40% with no money, 51% with $13.45 and 72% with $94.15. In 38 of 40 countries, wallets with money came back more often than wallets without. 279 economists and experts, and about 300 US laypeople, predicted the opposite: that more money would mean fewer returns. In a follow-up survey of over 2,500 people in the UK, US and Poland, keeping a wallet felt like stealing, more so when it held more money ("aversion to viewing oneself as a thief") — [U-M News](https://news.umich.edu/moral-concerns-override-desire-to-profit-from-finding-a-lost-wallet). Caveat: wallets were handed to staff in institutions, not left on the street. Rates varied a lot between countries, and wallets left at the Vatican and at two anti-corruption bureaus were never returned (same source).
- **World Happiness Report 2025, ch. 2 (Helliwell and colleagues) [LIB:whr2025, new chapter URL], moderate.** Actual wallet return in Cohn et al. was **1.8 times** the expected rate, and expected and actual return correlated at r = 0.62 across countries. In Helliwell & Wang's 2011 Toronto drop, people expected 23% to be returned and **over 80%** were. In Nordic countries 81% came back against 47% elsewhere; expected stranger return was 47% in the Nordics against 28% elsewhere. In a 2019 drop of 200 wallets in 20 North American cities, two-thirds came back, about double what US (34%) and Canadian (39%) respondents expected. In Reader's Digest drops, expected return predicted actual return at r = 0.71 in 1996 but only r = 0.19 in 2018. The authors say people are "far too pessimistic" about others' benevolence — [WHR 2025 ch. 2 PDF](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf); press summary: "much too pessimistic about the kindness of their communities" — [UN SDSN](https://www.unsdsn.org/news/world-happiness-report-2025-people-are-much-kinder-than-we-expect-research-shows/).

**Why distrust does not self-correct**
- **Fetchenhauer & Dunning 2010, "Why so cynical?" (Psychological Science 21(2)) [NEW], moderate: experimental test of a mechanism.** People "grossly underestimate" others' trustworthiness in an economic trust game, even when they are paid to be accurate. The proposed reason is asymmetric feedback: when you trust, you learn whether you were wrong, but when you withhold trust, you never find out. In an experiment, people given feedback whether or not they had trusted (symmetric feedback) became less cynical than people given no feedback, or feedback only after trusting — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797609358586/). Sample sizes and expected-vs-actual percentages were not on the open page (see Gaps).

**Overestimating others' selfishness ("norm of self-interest")**
- **Miller & Ratner 1998; Brick et al. 2021 (Collabra: Psychology 7(1)) [NEW], strong for the effect (original plus two preregistered replications), but WEIRD.** In preregistered replications with US MTurk (N = 800) and UK Prolific (N = 799) samples, people again overestimated how much self-interest drives others. Effect sizes were Cohen's d = 0.57–0.59 for how much payment would change others' willingness to give blood, and d = 0.75–0.84 for how much smokers' own smoking status shapes their views on smoking policy. Communal orientation did not moderate the effect — [UvA DARE record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/6af3fc3c-0445-48ce-99bc-2a6724b0f0d6).

**Underestimating the impact of our own kindness, thanks and compliments**
- **Kumar & Epley 2018, "Undervaluing gratitude" (Psychological Science) [LIB:kumar2018], moderate: 4 experiments, US.** People writing thank-you letters underestimated how happy recipients would feel and how surprised they would be by the content, and overestimated how awkward recipients would feel. They also underestimated how warm, and especially how competent, recipients would find them. These predictions shaped who they chose to write to, so the misjudgment acts as a barrier — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/uncategorized/people-underestimate-the-power-of-saying-thanks.html).
- **Kumar & Epley 2022, random acts of kindness (Journal of Experimental Psychology: General) [NEW], moderate: multiple experiments, US.** Givers underestimated how much happier recipients would feel in every experiment. In the hot chocolate study at a Chicago ice rink, 75 of 84 people gave their drink away. Givers predicted recipients' mood at 2.7 on a −5 to +5 scale; recipients reported 3.5. A cupcake field study had 200 participants. In a lab study, people who received a gift through someone's kindness then gave an average of **$48.02 of $100** to a stranger, against $41.20 in the comparison condition. Estimators did not predict this pay-it-forward effect — [Neuroscience News press release](https://neurosciencenews.com/kindness-outcome-21263/); [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/do_you_underestimate_the_impact_of_being_kind). The effect was not explained by surprise alone: unexpected items not framed as kindness did not produce it (Greater Good).
- **Zhao & Epley 2021, "Insufficiently complimentary" (JPSP 121(2), 239–256) [NEW], moderate: multiple experiments, replicated within the paper, US.** People giving compliments underestimated how good the compliment made the recipient feel. They overestimated how awkward the recipient would feel and strongly underestimated how well-phrased the recipient would find it, because givers focus on wording while recipients care about the warmth behind it. People told to focus on warmth were more willing to give compliments. A control condition suggested the gap is specific to compliments — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/za/blog/ulterior-motives/202110/you-probably-don-t-compliment-other-people-often-enough). Separate work by Boothby, Zhao & Bohns found the same underestimation in pairs of friends and pairs of strangers. Givers were "overly concerned about their ability to convey praise skillfully", and only about half of the people who wrote a compliment for a colleague actually sent it — [Nextgov/The Conversation](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2021/03/we-underestimate-the-value-of-complimenting-others/249722). This line of work overlaps the library's boothby2021.

**The liking gap**
- **Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark 2018 [LIB:boothby2018], moderate: 5 studies, US plus one UK community study.** After talking with a stranger, people underestimated how much their partner liked them. In Study 1a, actual liking was 5.82 and perceived liking 5.17 on a 7-point scale. Gaps were b = −0.36 to −0.65 across studies, and appeared in short, medium and long conversations. In a UK "How to Talk to Strangers" workshop (n = 100 analysed), the gap was about 0.68 on a 5-point scale. Among first-year university suitemates (n = 102), the gap lasted from September to February and was gone by May. Shyer people showed bigger gaps (about 1.14 points for high shyness against 0.25 for low) — [Essex repository PDF](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/23025/1/The%20Liking%20Gap%20in%20Conversations.pdf).

**Talking to strangers and weak ties**
- **Sandstrom & Dunn 2014 (PSPB 40(7), 910–922) [LIB:sandstrom2014], moderate: 3 studies; Study 1 used students' day-to-day reports.** Students felt happier and had a stronger sense of belonging on days when they talked with more classmates than usual. Interactions with weak ties (acquaintances) were related to social and emotional well-being across all daily interactions — [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/15300/).
- Worldwide context: 50% of people across 142 countries had no interaction with a stranger in the past week, and only 16% had one daily (Bangladesh 3% to Liberia 42%) — [Gallup/Meta 2023](https://gallup.com/file/analytics/513347/Gallup-Meta-Global%20State%20of%20Social%20Connections%20Report-2023.pdf) [LIB:gallup2023social].

**News exposure and a "mean world" picture**
- **Nature Human Behaviour 2023, 7:812–822 (Upworthy headline experiments; LMU Munich co-authors) [NEW], moderate: large field RCTs.** Negative words in headlines increased news consumption. The data cover about 105,000 story variations, about 8 million clicks and over 530 million impressions, with a preliminary randomised sample of N = 22,743 — [LMU repository record](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/94951/). This shows that news *supply* is pulled toward negativity. It does not directly test whether exposure changes beliefs about people.
- The WHR authors note that expected and actual wallet return lined up much less closely in 2018 (r = 0.19) than in 1996 (r = 0.71). Perceptions may have drifted away from reality — [WHR 2025 ch. 2](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf). This is a correlation across two waves of a magazine experiment, not proof that media caused it.

### Inferences
- The most robust single message is that **other people are, on average, more honest and kinder than we predict, and our own small kindnesses land better than we predict.** The worldwide wallet evidence (40 countries plus Gallup expectation data) is the strongest anchor. The thanks, compliment and kindness studies are consistent with it but mostly US-based.
- The asymmetric-feedback mechanism (Fetchenhauer & Dunning) explains why pessimism persists in individuals: withheld trust produces no disconfirming evidence. Small, low-risk tests ("symmetric feedback") are the way such a belief can update.
- The barriers to kindness are **social-evaluation worries** (awkwardness, saying it badly, seeming incompetent), not lack of goodwill. This points to steps that lower the cost of acting rather than steps that lecture about kindness.
- Underestimation is not a claim that everyone is trustworthy. In Cohn et al., return rates varied widely by country and by institution, and 28–60% of wallets were *not* returned, depending on the money condition.

### Gaps
- Exact expected-vs-actual trustworthiness percentages and sample sizes for Fetchenhauer & Dunning 2010 were behind the APS login.
- The number of participants and experiments in Zhao & Epley 2021 and Kumar & Epley 2022 (beyond the field studies above) were not stated on the pages opened.
- No cross-cultural (non-WEIRD) replication of the gratitude, compliment or liking-gap effects was found or opened in this session. Treat these as WEIRD until shown otherwise.
- A direct test of "mean world" beliefs (Gerbner-style cultivation research, or news exposure → lower generalised trust) was not found within budget. The headline study above concerns what gets clicked, not what people come to believe.
- Library cross-references **not re-opened this session** (verified in the 2026-10-15 library pass): epley2014, Epley & Schroeder commuter studies, where people expected talking to a stranger to be less pleasant than it was ([library URL](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2019/07/26/744267015/want-to-feel-happier-today-try-talking-to-a-stranger); the re-fetch was blocked by robots.txt); kardas2021, 12 experiments with over 1,800 people in which deep talks with strangers were less awkward than expected; liu2022, unexpected reach-outs appreciated more than expected; flynn2008 / bohns2016, help-seekers underestimated how many strangers would say yes by about 48%; jordan2011, people underestimate others' negative emotions; pew2025neighbors, 76% of Americans would bring in a neighbour's mail but only 52% expect a neighbour to do it for them; reuters2025news, 40% of people across 48 countries sometimes or often avoid the news.

## 2. What does cynicism cost?

### Takeaway
Cynical beliefs about human nature predict lower later income in US and German panels, except where cynicism is realistic, such as societies with low prosociality or high homicide. Contrary to the popular image of the "cynical genius", cynics on average score *lower* on cognitive and academic tests. A Finnish cohort links late-life cynical distrust to higher dementia risk, but that finding is fragile. Cynicism is costly mainly when it is unwarranted, which supports calibration, not blanket trust.

### Cited Findings
- **Stavrova & Ehlebracht 2016, "Cynical beliefs about human nature and income" (JPSP 110(1), 116–132) [NEW], moderate: 5 studies, national panels plus 41 countries.** Cynicism at baseline predicted lower income 9 years later (Study 1) and 2 years later (Study 2) in nationally representative US longitudinal surveys. In a nationally representative German panel, it predicted a smaller income increase over 9 years (Study 3). The effect held independently of Big Five traits (Study 4). In survey data from 41 countries, the income penalty was **weaker** where prosocial behaviour was low, homicide rates were high and societal cynicism was high (Study 5). The authors conclude that cynical beliefs have "negative economic outcomes unless such beliefs hold true." The proposed mechanism is that cynics avoid cooperation and over-invest in self-protection — [Tilburg repository](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/aa10b12e-a926-4fe6-8e93-b6be81684b60).
- **Stavrova & Ehlebracht 2019, "The cynical genius illusion" (PSPB 45(2), 254–269) [NEW], moderate: 4 studies, about 200,000 people in 30 countries.** Laypeople believe cynical people are cognitively superior. In the data, cynical people generally performed **worse** on cognitive ability and academic competency tasks. More competent people endorsed cynicism only where their sociocultural environment warranted it, while less competent people endorsed it unconditionally. The authors suggest this may be an "adaptive default" against exploitation — [Tilburg repository](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/098985fd-e45e-4389-9cde-6646333d3b26).
- **Neuvonen, Tolppanen and colleagues 2014 (Neurology) [NEW], early-to-moderate: one Finnish cohort, fragile adjustment.** 1,449 older adults (mean age 71.3, 62% women) were followed for 8.4 years for dementia and 10.4 years for mortality. People with the highest cynical distrust had 2.54 times the dementia risk of those with low distrust after adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors. Adjusting only for age and sex showed **no** association — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20140609/high-cynical-distrust-increased-dementia-risk). 622 participants were tested for dementia at follow-up, and the press coverage reported about three times as many diagnoses — [ScienceNordic](https://sciencenordic.com/cynical-people-are-more-likely-develop-dementia). The brief cites the study as Neuvonen et al.; Healio names Anna-Maija Tolppanen as lead author, and ScienceNordic says the study was led by Tolppanen. Author order was not checked against the journal page.
- Well-being cost of distrust (country and individual level): expecting a lost wallet to be returned ("very likely" vs not) goes with life evaluations **more than three-quarters of a point higher** on a 0–10 scale (Gallup World Poll 2019). The authors report this as larger than the effect of doubling income and almost twice that of unemployment — [WHR 2025 ch. 2](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf). This is correlational.

### Inferences
- Read together, the evidence says that **cynicism is a calibration problem**. Distrust is useful where exploitation is common, and expensive (in money, relationships and possibly health) where it is not. In most everyday settings people's estimates are too low (Section 1), so most people pay a cost for miscalibrated distrust.
- The "cynical genius" finding helps an app: it can honestly say that doubting everyone is not a sign of being smarter. It should not imply the user is less intelligent.
- The health link should be presented cautiously, if at all. It comes from one cohort and depends on which covariates are adjusted for.

### Gaps
- Sample sizes for the US and German panels in Stavrova & Ehlebracht 2016, and effect sizes for both Stavrova papers, were not on the repository pages.
- Mortality results for Neuvonen et al. 2014 were not reported on the pages opened, and the PubMed abstract could not be retrieved.
- No independent replication of either Stavrova paper was found within budget.
- Broader cynicism and health literature (e.g., hostility and cardiovascular meta-analyses) was not covered.

## 3. Generalised trust: patterns, well-being, repair after betrayal, and forgiveness

### Takeaway
Saying "most people can be trusted" ranges from about 2% to 74% across 118 countries. Most of the world sits below 30%; the Nordics, and in the survey data China, are at the top. Trust strongly tracks well-being and buffers people against hardship. After a betrayal, repair depends on what kind of violation it was. Apologies work for competence lapses but integrity violations are harder to repair. Forgiveness therapies produce moderate gains in forgiveness and small-to-moderate drops in depression in randomised trials.

### Cited Findings
**Cross-country levels (Integrated Values Surveys = World Values Survey + European Values Study, wave ending 2022) [NEW]**
- The question is: "most people can be trusted" or "you need to be very careful in dealing with people". Latest values (mostly the 2017–2022 wave) cover 118 countries and territories. **Highest:** Denmark 73.9%, Norway 72.1%, Finland 68.4%, China 63.5%, Sweden 62.8%, Iceland 62.3%, Switzerland 58.5%, Netherlands 57.0%, New Zealand 56.6%. **Lowest:** Zimbabwe 2.1%, Albania 2.8%, Peru 4.2%, Nicaragua 4.3%, Colombia 4.5%, Indonesia 4.6%, Philippines 5.3%. **Large countries:** Brazil 6.5%, Egypt 7.3%, Mexico 10.5%, Nigeria 13.0%, India 16.9%, Russia 22.9%, Pakistan 23.3%, Japan 33.7%, USA 37.0%, Germany 41.6%, UK 43.3% — [Our World in Data table](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/self-reported-trust-attitudes?tab=table) and [CSV](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/self-reported-trust-attitudes.csv). Strength: moderate (large standard surveys). The single binary item may be read differently across cultures (see Inferences).
- Low generalised trust does not mean low kindness. **Indonesia** has 4.6% generalised trust in the IVS, yet ranks **1st in the world for both donating and volunteering** — [WHR 2025 ch. 2](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf). In Sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America, helping strangers is the dominant form of kindness rather than donating (same source).
- Trends: the WHR 2025 press release links declining happiness and **social trust in the US and parts of Europe** to polarisation and anti-system votes — [UN SDSN](https://www.unsdsn.org/news/world-happiness-report-2025-people-are-much-kinder-than-we-expect-research-shows/). No time series was opened this session (see Gaps).

**Trust and well-being (Helliwell and colleagues)**
- Living in a country where people are judged fair and helpful goes with about **four-fifths of a point** higher life evaluation on a 0–10 scale. In European Social Survey data from 2002 to 2022/23, people who judge most others to be fair and kind suffer **materially less** well-being loss from unemployment, ill health, discrimination or unsafe streets. Trust works as a buffer — [WHR 2025 ch. 2](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf). Strength: moderate (very large, correlational).

**Trust repair after betrayal (Kim, Ferrin, Dirks, Cooper)**
- **Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks 2004 (Journal of Applied Psychology) [NEW], moderate: 2 experiments, business students, hypothetical hiring.** For the same tax-return error framed either as a competence lapse or as an integrity lapse, an **apology repaired trust better than a denial for competence violations**, while a **denial worked better for integrity violations**. Later evidence mattered: repair was best when the evidence matched what the accused had said (an apology followed by evidence of guilt, or a denial followed by evidence of innocence) — [WashU Source](https://source.washu.edu/2004/09/removing-the-shadow-of-suspicion/).
- **Kim, Cooper, Dirks & Ferrin 2013 (OBHDP 120(1), 1–14) [NEW], moderate: 570 undergraduates.** A response matched to the type of violation worked for both individuals and groups, while mismatched responses lowered trust. Groups were generally less trusting than individuals, especially after ineffective responses. Persuasive arguments against the accused, rather than social pressure, partly explained this. The paper cites Schweitzer et al. 2006 as showing that promises can help, but did not isolate it — [SMU repository PDF](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/lkcsb_research/article/4230/viewcontent/Repairing_trust_with_individuals_afv.pdf). WEIRD: student samples, new relationships, hypothetical scenarios.

**Forgiveness interventions**
- **Wade, Hoyt, Kidwell & Worthington 2014, "Efficacy of psychotherapeutic interventions to promote forgiveness: a meta-analysis" (J Consult Clin Psychol 82(1), 154–170; PMID 24364794) [NEW].** The citation and aim were verified: the meta-analysis covered interventions to help people forgive a specific hurt, plus moderators — [PubMed search result](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Wade+Hoyt+Kidwell+Worthington+psychotherapeutic+interventions+forgiveness); [author site listing](https://www.evworthington-forgiveness.com/journal-articles-forgiveness-and-reconciliation/efficacy-of-psychotherapeutic-interventions-to-promote-forgiveness-pdf). **Its effect sizes could not be retrieved this session** (see Gaps).
- **Akhtar & Barlow (2016; Trauma, Violence & Abuse), "Forgiveness therapy for the promotion of wellbeing: a systematic review and meta-analysis" [NEW], strong: meta-analysis of RCTs.** The trials involved adolescents and adults who had experienced interpersonal hurt or violence. Forgiveness therapy improved state forgiveness (SMD 0.55, 95% CI 0.21–0.88) and trait forgiveness (SMD 0.43, 0.20–0.67). It reduced depression (SMD 0.37, 0.07–0.68) and increased positive affect (SMD 0.29, 0.06–0.52). The signs are reported as negative in the abstract because of how the scale was coded — [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:c4f1e260-c0e9-4ff1-b858-030826eeefbd). The number of trials and countries was not on the page.

### Inferences
- Trust levels are a feature of **places and histories**, not just personalities. A person from a context where 5% say "most people can be trusted" may be accurately reading real risks, and Stavrova 2016 shows cynicism costs less there. The app should not treat low trust as a flaw.
- The China figure (63.5%) is likely affected by how "most people" is interpreted and by survey conditions. The report should not present it as the world's highest-trust large society without that caveat. This is my inference; it was not tested in the sources opened.
- The Indonesia contrast (very low generalised trust, top-ranked generosity) is a useful reminder that **trusting strangers in the abstract and being kind in practice are different things**. People who have "lost faith in people" may still be living generously.
- For personal betrayals, the trust-repair work suggests two practical points. First, whether a let-down feels like a *competence* lapse ("they messed up") or an *integrity* lapse ("they don't care, they lied") changes what can repair it. Second, an apology is most credible for mistakes, while integrity breaches need evidence over time. These are lab scenarios with strangers, so the extension to close relationships is an inference.
- Forgiveness therapy has meta-analytic support for moderate effects in RCTs. But it was delivered as a structured intervention for real hurts, and forgiving is not the same as reconciling or trusting again (the library already notes this).

### Gaps
- Wade et al. 2014 effect sizes (number of studies, participants, vs no-treatment and vs alternative treatments, dose-response) could not be retrieved: the PubMed page returned empty. The report writer should not quote numbers for it from memory.
- No trust-repair *meta-analysis* was opened. Evidence above is from the Kim, Ferrin, Dirks and Cooper experimental programme only, with WEIRD student samples.
- No time-series trend data (e.g., US General Social Survey, WVS waves over time, OECD Trust Survey 2024) was opened within budget. Trend claims rest only on the WHR press statement.
- Library cross-references **not re-opened this session**: fehr2010 (175 studies with 26,006 people: forgiveness is more likely with empathy, an apology and a sense that the harm was unintended); davis2015 (self-forgiveness and well-being, 65 samples); lewicki2016 (admitting fault is the most important part of an apology).

## 4. Kindness and prosocial behaviour

### Takeaway
Doing kind things reliably gives the giver a small-to-medium well-being boost. This is shown by meta-analyses (Curry 2018: δ = 0.28 across 27 studies; Hui 2020: 201 studies with 198,213 people), and the link appears across cultures for spending on others (120 of 136 countries). Informal, everyday helping is linked to well-being more than formal volunteering is. Helping strangers rose worldwide after 2020. Evidence that *witnessing* kindness inspires more kindness (moral elevation) is plausible but rests on smaller single studies.

### Cited Findings
- **Curry, Rowland, Van Lissa, Zlotowitz, McAlaney & Whitehouse 2018, "Happy to help?" (JESP 76, 320–329) [LIB:curry2018], strong.** The meta-analysis covered 24 articles (27 studies, 52 effect sizes) with a total N of 4,045. Acts of kindness raised the actor's well-being by **δ = 0.28** (small-to-medium). The effect was not moderated by sex, age, participant type, intervention, control condition or outcome measure, and no sign of publication bias was found. The authors call for more theory-specific, better-designed studies — [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:4701365b-5760-4ece-a390-857a5f7b3c0c).
- **Hui et al. 2020, "Rewards of kindness?" (Psychological Bulletin) [NEW], strong.** 201 independent studies with 198,213 participants found a modest but real link between prosocial behaviour and well-being. The link was stronger for eudaimonic well-being (meaning, growth) than for hedonic well-being. **Informal helping (e.g., helping a neighbour with groceries) was more strongly linked to well-being than formal volunteering**. Younger givers showed more psychological benefit, and older givers better physical health. Links were stronger in women. Ethnicity and social class were largely unexamined — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2020/09/doing-good-boosts-health).
- **Aknin, Barrington-Leigh, Dunn, Helliwell et al. 2013 (JPSP) [LIB:aknin2013], strong: worldwide.** In Gallup World Poll data (2006–2008; 234,917 people in 136 countries), spending on others was linked to well-being in **120 of 136 countries** and in every world region, after controlling for income, social support, freedom and perceived corruption. In experiments, recalling spending on others raised happiness in Canada and Uganda (n = 820) and in India (n = 101). Buying a goody bag for a sick child rather than for oneself raised well-being in Canada and South Africa (n = 207) — [APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2013/02/people-giving).
- **Global trends (WHR 2025 ch. 2) [LIB:whr2025], moderate.** In 2024, donating, volunteering and helping strangers fell from 2023 but stayed **more than 10% above 2017–19 levels**. Helping strangers was higher than in 2017–19 in **every** region, by a global average of 18%. North America/Australia/New Zealand was the only region where combined prosociality was not significantly higher than pre-pandemic. Balanced giving and receiving went with higher well-being and fewer depressive symptoms, and gifts from closer ties went with more happiness — [WHR 2025 ch. 2](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf).
- **Pay-it-forward.** Receiving a kindness made recipients more generous to a third person ($48.02 vs $41.20 of $100), which givers did not anticipate — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/kindness-outcome-21263/) (Kumar & Epley 2022) [NEW], moderate.
- **Moral elevation (witnessing kindness): early-to-moderate.** Thomson & Siegel 2013 (Journal of Positive Psychology 8(1), 50–64), 4 studies, found that witnessing a moral act produces "elevation" consistent with Haidt's account, with links to donation behaviour. The effect was moderated by the recipient's character, by exposure to an immoral act and by how much effort the moral act took — [Institute of Coaching abstract](https://instituteofcoaching.org/resources/moral-act-elevation-and-prosocial-behavior-moderators-morality) [NEW]. Rullo, Lalot & Heering 2022 (Routledge journal 17(4), 545–560), 5 studies, found that elevation after witnessing uncommon virtue mediated effects on prosocial intentions and, in Study 4, behaviour — [Basel edoc](https://edoc.unibas.ch/88391) [NEW].

### Inferences
- Kindness steps are well supported as a **modest, reliable** mood and meaning boost, not a cure. Everyday, spontaneous help (Hui) and small gifts or spending on others (Aknin) are the best-evidenced forms. The cross-cultural base for spending on others is unusually strong.
- Kindness and generalised trust feed each other. Receiving kindness raises one's own generosity (pay-it-forward), and noticing real kindness corrects pessimistic estimates (Section 1). That makes "notice one kindness" a reasonable, low-cost step, though the elevation evidence is weaker than the evidence for doing kindness.
- Balanced giving and receiving matters (WHR). Steps should include *accepting* help or *asking* for small help, not only giving, especially for people who over-give.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of moral elevation was found or opened. Elevation findings are single multi-study papers with unknown sample sizes and mostly Western samples.
- Hui et al.'s pooled r was not shown on the page opened.
- Library cross-references **not re-opened this session**: jenkinson2013 (systematic review of volunteering, 40 papers: better well-being and lower mortality in observational data, but trials show no clear health gains); caf2024 (World Giving Index); korndorfer2015 (higher-status people donate and help more in large surveys, contrary to small lab claims); algoe2010 (gratitude in couples).

## 5. Faith and community: hope, trust and well-being worldwide

### Takeaway
Religious involvement goes with higher well-being mostly **where life is hard and religion is the norm**, and mostly through **active participation and community** rather than belief or affiliation alone. People who affiliate but don't take part are no happier than the unaffiliated in 24 of 26 countries. How people use faith in hard times matters. Positive religious coping goes with better adjustment, while spiritual struggle ("negative religious coping") consistently goes with more anxiety and depression, including in a 24-country pandemic meta-analysis. All of this evidence is correlational. These findings describe patterns, not a recommendation for or against any belief.

### Cited Findings
- **Diener, Tay & Myers 2011, "The religion paradox: If religion makes people happy, why are so many dropping out?" (JPSP 101, 1278–1290) [NEW], moderate: huge but correlational.** Gallup data from 2005–2009 cover over 455,000 people in 154 countries. Religious people report slightly higher well-being overall, but the link depends on the society. In wealthy, secure countries (low unemployment, good education), religiosity is lower and its link with well-being is weak. In poorer countries with difficult living conditions, religiosity is much higher and religious people report higher well-being, plausibly because religion provides **social support, respect and meaning** that are otherwise hard to get — [University of Mannheim article](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/forschung-erleben/artikel/du-hast-doch-alles-brauchst-du-dann-noch-einen-gott/); citation verified at [World Database of Happiness](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/the-religion-paradox-if-religion-makes-people-happy-why-are-so-many-dropping-out-11065/). The Mannheim page is a secondary summary and gives no effect sizes.
- **Pew Research Center 2019 [LIB:pew2019religion], moderate: 26 countries, mostly Christian-majority.** Actively religious people were "very happy" significantly more often than the unaffiliated in 12 of 25 countries, and "no country" showed them less happy. In the US the figures were 36% against about 25%; in Australia 45% against 33%. **Inactive affiliates and the unaffiliated were about equally happy in 24 of 26 countries.** Actives more often joined non-religious voluntary groups: in the US, 58% of actives, 51% of inactives and 39% of the unaffiliated. They were more often regular voters, and less often smokers (in 17 of 19 countries). They were **not** clearly healthier on self-rated health (23 of 25 countries showed no difference after controls). Pew stresses that the data are correlational, that reverse causation is possible ("crisis religiosity") and that the countries are mostly Christian-majority nations in Europe and the Americas. Congregational social connection is one possible driver — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2019/01/31/religions-relationship-to-happiness-civic-engagement-and-health-around-the-world/).
- **Ano & Vasconcelles 2005, religious coping meta-analysis (Journal of Clinical Psychology 61(4), 461–480), the Pargament framework [NEW], strong for the association, but Christian- and US-heavy.** 49 studies with 13,512 participants. **Positive religious coping** (benevolent reappraisal, working with God as a partner, seeking support from clergy or members, spiritual connection, religious helping) went with better adjustment (r = .33) and slightly less distress (r = −.12). **Negative religious coping** (spiritual discontent, seeing the problem as punishment from God, passively waiting for God to fix things, pleading for direct intervention) went with more distress (r = .22) and was unrelated to positive adjustment (r = .02). Of studies reporting affiliation, participants were about 57% Protestant, 28% Catholic and 13% other faiths, and about 65% were White/European American. The coping measures were built on Judeo-Christian traditions — [UNED PDF](https://www.uned.es/universidad/inicio/dam/jcr:fd37f522-5523-47c7-9f9f-821180678483/ANOYVA~1.PDF).
- **Cheng & Ying 2023 (Frontiers in Psychiatry 14) [LIB:cheng2023], strong: meta-analysis, 24 countries in 7 regions.** 42 studies with 25,438 participants (58% female) in the first wave of COVID-19. Negative religious coping was the only significant overall correlate of anxiety and depressive symptoms (r = .29). Positive religious coping showed no overall link (r = −.02). Among younger people and women, who were more distressed, positive coping was linked to *more* symptoms. The data are cross-sectional and the authors suggest religious coping may work best combined with other strategies — [Frontiers](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1097598/text).

### Inferences
- The common ingredient across these findings is **belonging and meaning shared with others**. Participation, not affiliation, carries the association (Pew), and the advantage is concentrated where other sources of support are scarce (Diener et al.). An app can honour this without taking sides: for a person of faith, their congregation can be one place where trust is restored; for a person without faith, the comparable ingredients are regular, face-to-face community and shared purpose. That equivalence is an inference; it is not tested in these sources.
- **Spiritual struggle** (feeling abandoned or punished, losing faith in God) is a consistent marker of distress across cultures. For some users, "losing faith in people" may come together with losing religious faith. The app should reflect this gently and neutrally, never argue for or against belief, and treat it as a reason for care rather than a problem to fix.
- Positive religious coping is not a guaranteed buffer in an acute crisis (Cheng & Ying). An insight should not imply that "faith will fix it".

### Gaps
- The Diener et al. 2011 abstract and effect sizes (including the US state-level analyses) could not be opened directly: the PubMed link failed.
- Non-Christian and non-theistic traditions are under-represented in the coping meta-analysis (Ano & Vasconcelles). The Pew happiness data are mostly Christian-majority countries.
- Library cross-references **not re-opened this session**: counted2025 (Global Flourishing Study, 202,898 people in 22 countries: hope 8.7 among those attending worship more than weekly vs 7.8 among those who never attend); lim2010 (having close friends in one's congregation, not attendance alone, predicted life satisfaction); pew2016everyday.

## 6. Loneliness and social connection worldwide

### Takeaway
About 1 in 6 people worldwide are lonely (WHO), or about 1 in 4 on Gallup/Meta's "very/fairly lonely" measure. Loneliness is twice as common in low-income countries as in high-income ones and highest among the young. WHO links it to about 871,000 deaths a year. Daily contact with strangers is rare (16%), and half of people had none in a week. The WHO's individual-level recommendations (check in on friends, greet neighbours, join groups, volunteer, put phones away) match the small-step evidence above.

### Cited Findings
- **Gallup & Meta 2023, Global State of Social Connections [LIB:gallup2023social], moderate: 142 countries, June 2022 to February 2023, about 1,000 people aged 15+ per country, China not surveyed.** 72% felt very or fairly connected (Mongolia 95%, Dominican Republic 47%). **24% felt very or fairly lonely**, over 1 billion people (Lesotho 58%, Vietnam 5%). Loneliness by age was 25% at 15–18, 27% at 19–29, 25% at 30–44, 22% at 45–64 and 17% at 65+. Men and women were both at 24%. Of the 29 countries where at least a third of people were lonely, 22 were in Africa, 4 in the Middle East and 3 in South Asia. 16% interacted with a stranger daily and 50% not at all in the past week — [Gallup PDF](https://gallup.com/file/analytics/513347/Gallup-Meta-Global%20State%20of%20Social%20Connections%20Report-2023.pdf).
- **WHO Commission on Social Connection 2025 [LIB:who2025 / who2025connection], moderate: global synthesis.** About **1 in 6** people are affected by loneliness, linked to an estimated **871,000 deaths a year** (about 100 an hour). Loneliness is 17–21% at ages 13–29 (highest among teenagers), about 24% in low-income countries and 11% in high-income countries. Up to 1 in 3 older adults and 1 in 4 adolescents are socially isolated. Lonely people are about twice as likely to become depressed, and lonely teens are 22% more likely to get lower grades. Causes include poor health, low income, living alone, weak community infrastructure and digital technologies, such as excessive screen time and negative online interactions. Individual actions recommended: check in on friends, put phones away in conversation, greet neighbours, join local groups, volunteer, and seek support services when loneliness is severe — [WHO news release](https://who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death).
- **WHR 2025 [LIB:whr2025].** In 2023, **19% of young adults worldwide said they had no one to count on**, a 39% increase since 2006. Americans dining alone rose 53% over two decades — [UN SDSN](https://www.unsdsn.org/news/world-happiness-report-2025-people-are-much-kinder-than-we-expect-research-shows/).

### Inferences
- The two global loneliness estimates (24% vs about 16%) use different measures and samples. The report should present them as a range, not a contradiction.
- Loneliness and distrust likely reinforce each other. Isolated people get fewer chances to test their expectations of others (the asymmetric-feedback logic from Section 1), and the liking gap is largest in shy people. This chain is an inference; no single study opened here tested it.
- Because loneliness is highest in low-income countries and among the young, a worldwide app should not assume a Western older-adult picture of loneliness.

### Gaps
- No intervention meta-analysis for loneliness was opened in this pass.
- Country-level loneliness data for China is absent from Gallup/Meta.

## What this means for the app

### Everyday situations that reveal how someone sees other people
Each of these could be a morning or evening question with concrete options and no rating scales. Each maps onto a well-measured misjudgment:
- **Lost item (honesty expectations; Cohn 2019, WHR 2025).** "You realise you left your wallet or phone at a café or bus stop. What's your first thought?" Options: *Someone will hand it in* / *It'll come back but the cash will be gone* / *It's gone for good* / *I'd go back but not expect much*.
- **A stranger's offer (trust in good intentions; Fetchenhauer & Dunning 2010).** "Someone you don't know offers to help with a heavy bag or directions." Options: *Accept and thank them* / *Accept but stay alert* / *Politely decline* / *Wonder what they want*.
- **The unsent thank-you (Kumar & Epley 2018).** "You think of someone who helped you a while back." Options: *Message them now* / *Mean to, but it'd feel awkward* / *Assume they know* / *Tell someone else about it instead*.
- **The unspoken compliment (Zhao & Epley 2021).** "A colleague, classmate or relative did something well today." Options: *Tell them* / *Hold back in case it sounds odd* / *Mention it later if it comes up* / *Assume others already told them*.
- **After meeting someone new (liking gap; Boothby 2018).** "You've just had a first chat with someone. How do you think it went for them?" Options: *They enjoyed it* / *Probably fine* / *I likely seemed dull or awkward* / *I'm replaying what I said*.
- **Queue or commute (talking to strangers; Sandstrom & Dunn 2014).** "You're waiting next to someone for ten minutes." Options: *Start a small chat* / *Smile, no words* / *Phone out* / *Hope they don't talk to me*.
- **Being let down (attributing the violation; Kim, Ferrin et al.).** "A friend forgets something they promised." Options: *They're stretched thin* / *They made a mistake* / *They don't really care* / *This always happens with people*.
- **After an apology (forgiveness vs reconciliation; Akhtar & Barlow; library fehr2010).** Options: *Accept and move on* / *Accept, but watch for change* / *Need them to put it right first* / *Not ready yet*.
- **After the news (news negativity; LMU/NHB 2023).** "After reading or scrolling the news, how do people around you seem?" Options: *No different* / *A bit more on guard* / *Worse than I thought* / *I skipped the news today*. Keep the options politically neutral.
- **Noticing kindness (elevation; WHR helping strangers).** "Did you see anyone do something kind today?" Options: *Yes, for me* / *Yes, for someone else* / *Didn't notice any* / *I did something kind*.
- **Asking for small help (balanced giving; WHR 2025; library flynn2008).** "You need a small favour." Options: *Ask straight away* / *Ask only someone close* / *Do it myself rather than ask* / *Put it off*.

Weekly and monthly "mirrors" can show the person's *own* patterns. For example: how often they chose "it'll come back" versus "it's gone"; how many thanks or compliments they meant to give against how many they gave; whether let-downs were read as mistakes or as not caring. Never show comparisons with other users or population averages.

### What an honest insight could say to someone who has lost faith in people
These are grounded in the evidence and written to avoid comparing the person with others. Each can carry a short "why" with the source.
- "Being cautious makes sense when people have let you down. But caution that's never tested can't be updated. When we don't trust, we never find out when we would have been right to (Fetchenhauer & Dunning 2010)."
- "In a 40-country experiment, most lost wallets came back, and wallets with money in them came back *more* often than wallets with none. Experts predicted the opposite (Cohn et al. 2019)."
- "People who hold back a thank-you or compliment usually worry it'll feel awkward. Recipients tend to feel better, and less awkward, than the giver expects. The warmth matters more than finding the right words (Kumar & Epley 2018; Zhao & Epley 2021)."
- "After a first conversation, people tend to underestimate how much the other person liked them. It's normal to replay what you said (Boothby et al. 2018)."
- "Doubting everyone isn't the same as seeing clearly. Studies find people who stay cynical regardless of circumstances don't tend to be the sharper judges. What helps is matching trust to the situation (Stavrova & Ehlebracht 2019)."
- After a betrayal: "There's a difference between someone making a mistake and someone not caring. Apologies tend to repair mistakes. Broken honesty usually takes consistent actions over time (Kim, Ferrin et al. 2004)." Avoid claiming that trust *always* comes back.
- Tone: phrase as "research suggests" or "many people find". Present it as one view of what people are like, never a verdict on the user.

### Small steps the evidence supports
All steps are optional, low-risk and reversible:
1. **Send one specific thank-you** to someone who helped you. Strong practical support: gratitude underestimation (moderate, WEIRD) plus the kindness meta-analyses (strong).
2. **Give one compliment, and focus on what you appreciate, not how you word it.** Moderate evidence, WEIRD.
3. **Do one small, informal kindness**, such as carrying, holding a door or a short favour. Strong evidence (Curry 2018; Hui 2020), with a modest effect size.
4. **Spend a small amount on someone else** (a coffee, a snack). Strong evidence, worldwide (Aknin 2013).
5. **Have one short chat with a stranger or acquaintance** (barista, neighbour, someone in a queue). Moderate evidence (Sandstrom & Dunn 2014; library epley2014). It also matches the WHO's "greet neighbours" advice.
6. **A low-stakes trust test with a check-back.** Ask a small favour or lend something minor, then note what actually happened. This creates the "symmetric feedback" that corrects miscalibrated distrust (Fetchenhauer & Dunning 2010, moderate). Keep it trivially low-risk.
7. **Write down one kindness you saw today** (early evidence on elevation; it also counters negativity in what we notice).
8. **Check in on one person** or put the phone away during one conversation (WHO 2025 recommendations).
9. **Let someone help you** (balanced giving and receiving; WHR 2025).
10. For a past hurt, and only if the person chooses, **reflect on whether the harm was a mistake or deliberate**, and on what, if anything, would need to change for trust to return. Forgiveness therapy has RCT support (strong), but the app is not therapy. It should present this as reflection, not treatment.

### What to avoid
- **Naivety about real danger, abuse or exploitation.** Cynicism is costly mainly where it is unwarranted (Stavrova 2016). Where exploitation is common, caution is rational. Never nudge someone to trust, forgive or reconcile with a person who has harmed them. Separate *forgiving* (an inner process) from *reconciling* or *trusting again*. If an answer suggests abuse, coercion or danger, the app should not offer "trust more" steps. Instead, it should acknowledge the situation and point neutrally to appropriate support, without diagnosing.
- **Turning trust into a personality flaw.** Low trust often reflects real environments: generalised trust ranges from 2% to 74% across countries. Avoid language like "you're too cynical". Never compare the person with others or with national averages, and avoid country stereotypes.
- **Preaching kindness or optimism.** Present steps as small experiments, not moral duties. The evidence shows modest effects (δ ≈ 0.28), not transformation.
- **Taking sides on religion or politics.** Treat faith as one possible source of community and meaning, and do the same for secular community. Do not suggest that becoming (or stopping being) religious will help. If someone expresses spiritual struggle, respond with neutral care: it is linked to distress across cultures, so don't argue or reassure theologically. For news, reflect only on how the person feels, never on outlets or positions.
- **Over-claiming.** Do not cite the dementia link as fact (it depends on adjustment), and do not quote Wade 2014 numbers (they were not retrieved). Flag that the gratitude, compliment and liking-gap findings come mostly from US and UK samples.
- **Forcing social contact on people who are anxious.** The liking gap is biggest in shy people. Frame stranger-talk as an option with an easy "smile only" version.
