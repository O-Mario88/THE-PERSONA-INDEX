## 1. Hope theory (Snyder: agency + pathways): outcomes and interventions

### Takeaway
Hope means having goals, believing you can act on them (agency) and seeing routes to reach them (pathways). It goes with better well-being and work performance, but deliberate hope interventions give only small gains in hope (d ~ .2), tiny gains in life satisfaction, and no clear drop in distress. Published studies also look better than unpublished ones.

### Cited Findings
- **Hope interventions, meta-analysis (Weis & Speridakos, 2011; Psychology of Well-Being).** 27 studies, N = 2,154: 17 clinic-referred or at-risk samples, 10 school, college or community samples; 21 adult and 6 child or adolescent samples; 12 randomised. Programmes included Snyder's hope therapy (goal-setting, planning pathways, building agency through stories) and Herth's Hope Intervention Program. Effects: hope **d = .22** (small, with significant heterogeneity), life satisfaction **d = .16**, psychological distress **d = .04 (not significant)**. Published studies showed d = .23 against d = .09 for unpublished dissertations, a sign of publication bias. Single-session lab studies did better than multi-session clinical ones, and clinic samples did a little worse than community samples (p = .09). The authors call this "only modest evidence" and found "no consistent evidence that hope enhancement strategies can alleviate psychological distress." **[STRONG]** that effects are small; countries not stated on the page. **[new: weis2011]** — [Weis & Speridakos 2011](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/2211-1522-1-5)
- **Hope at work, meta-analysis (Reichard, Avey, Lopez & Dollwet, 2013; Journal of Positive Psychology).** k = 45 studies, 133 effect sizes, N = 11,139 employees. Hope was significantly and positively linked to work performance and to employee well-being; the abstract gives no numeric r. Effects were **stronger for women and for US-based studies**. **[STRONG]**, partly **[WEIRD]** (larger effects in US samples). **[new: reichard2013]** — [Reichard et al. 2013](https://digitalcommons.cwu.edu/cobfac/252)
- **Hope across 22 countries (Global Flourishing Study).** N = 202,898; one item, 0–10: "Despite challenges, I always remain hopeful about the future." Highest: **Indonesia 9.17**, then Mexico and Argentina. Lowest: **Japan 5.95**, with Sweden and Hong Kong also near the bottom. Weekly-plus worship attenders scored 8.68 and never-attenders 7.82, the largest demographic gap. Self-employed scored 8.27 and unemployed 7.68. Married people were higher than single people, though in Nigeria widowed people were higher than married. Older people were slightly higher overall, but younger people were higher in some countries. More education went with slightly higher hope overall, but not in Mexico. Spread of hope within Japan (Gini 0.21) was about 3x that within Indonesia (0.07). Cross-sectional, "a snapshot in time." **[MODERATE]**, cross-cultural. **[library: counted2025]** — [Global Flourishing Study: Where hope lives](https://globalflourishingstudy.com/paper/where-hope-lives-who-stays-hopeful-around-the-world)
- **Hope is more than optimism.** Lybbert & Wydick, building on Snyder, define hope as goals (aspirations) + agency (self-efficacy, locus of control) + pathways (routes you can see). They note that hope "includes a clear direction, agency, and realistic pathways," whereas optimism is a general expectation that things will go well. — [Lybbert & Wydick, NBER chapter](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c13832.pdf) **[new: lybbertwydick]**

### Inferences
- The two working parts of hope, "I can act" and "I can see a way," fit concrete everyday questions better than general cheer. They are the parts an app can honestly reflect back from someone's own answers.
- Because hope programmes barely shift distress (d = .04), a product should not promise that hope exercises will relieve suffering. At most they nudge goal-directed thinking a little.
- The GFS ranking (Indonesia and Latin America high; Japan, Sweden and Hong Kong low) shows that hope is not simply a matter of national wealth. How people talk about hope, and their norms for saying it out loud, differ across cultures.

### Gaps
- I did not open a meta-analysis linking trait hope to academic achievement or physical health, so I cannot give pooled r values for those. The search returned only single studies.
- Reichard et al.'s numeric correlations were not on the page opened.
- I did not check measurement validity across cultures, such as whether the Adult Hope Scale or Children's Hope Scale works the same way in different countries.

## 2. Optimism (Scheier & Carver), health, realistic vs unrealistic optimism, learned optimism

### Takeaway
Dispositional optimism (expecting good outcomes) has a small but consistent link with better physical health, including fewer cardiovascular events, in many cohorts both inside and outside the US. The evidence is observational. Optimism can be raised a little by brief exercises, but the gains are measured right afterwards and may not last.

### Cited Findings
- **Optimism and physical health, meta-analysis (Rasmussen, Scheier & Greenhouse, 2009; Annals of Behavioral Medicine 37(3):239–256).** 83 studies, 108 effect sizes, random effects. Mean effect size **0.17** (p < .001), which is small. Optimism predicted every outcome group: mortality, survival, cardiovascular outcomes, physiological and immune markers, cancer, pregnancy outcomes, symptoms and pain. Effects were **larger for subjective than objective health measures**. **[STRONG]**; samples' countries not given in the abstract. **[new: rasmussen2009]** — [KU ScholarWorks record](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/3408d811-f9c3-4069-97ff-26a20cedf277)
- **Optimism, cardiovascular events and mortality, meta-analysis (Rozanski et al., 2019; JAMA Network Open).** 15 mostly community-based studies, about 230,000 participants. Optimists had about **35% lower risk of cardiovascular events** than pessimists. Optimism was also linked to lower all-cause mortality, though the press release gives no effect size. Results held after adjusting for depression, physical activity, gender and education. There was a dose-response pattern, and **results were similar in US and non-US studies**. The authors say it is unknown whether *trained* optimism brings the same benefit as naturally occurring optimism. **[STRONG]** as an association, not as proof of cause. Opened as the Mount Sinai press release, not the paper. **[new: rozanski2019]** — [Mount Sinai press release](https://www.mountsinai.org/about/newsroom/2019/optimism-linked-to-lower-risk-of-cardiovascular-events-and-death)
- **Can optimism be increased? Meta-analysis (Malouff & Schutte, 2017; Journal of Positive Psychology 12(6):594–604).** 29 RCTs, N = 3,319. Pooled **g = .41**. **Best Possible Self (BPS) studies had significantly larger effects** than other interventions, and in-person delivery beat other formats. Effects were also larger when measured **within one day** of the intervention, in completer rather than intention-to-treat analyses, and with active controls. No longer-term follow-up was reported. **[STRONG]**, but the effects are measured immediately and are probably inflated. **[new: malouff2017]** — [UNE repository](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/21628)

### Inferences
- The optimism–health link is real but small and observational. An app must not claim that "thinking positively will make you healthier."
- Because effects were largest one day after the exercise and in completer analyses, short optimism exercises probably give a passing lift, not a lasting trait change. This matches the BPS decay data in section 3.

### Gaps
- **Realistic vs unrealistic optimism** (Weinstein's unrealistic optimism, optimism bias) and **learned optimism** (Seligman's explanatory style, the Penn Resiliency Program) were not opened this session; the budget ran out. I make no evidence claims about them. The Oettingen work in section 4 is the closest evidence available here on when optimism-like thinking backfires.
- No trial was found that shows induced optimism changes hard health outcomes.

## 3. The Best Possible Self (BPS) exercise

### Takeaway
Writing or imagining your "best possible self" in the future reliably lifts positive feelings and optimism by a small-to-moderate amount. The effects are largest straight away and seem to fade within about a week. Whether BPS reduces negative feelings or depression is contested between the two meta-analyses.

### Cited Findings
- **Carrillo et al., 2019 (PLoS ONE 14(9): e0222386).** 29 studies, N = 2,909, BPS against controls. Positive affect **d+ = .511**, optimism **d+ = .334**, well-being **d+ = .325**. Negative affect and depressive symptoms showed small effects; exact values were not in the abstract. Against gratitude exercises, BPS was better for positive affect (d+ = .326) and negative affect (d+ = .485). Trend-level moderators: larger effects in **older participants** and with **shorter total practice time**. **[STRONG]**; countries and risk of bias not reported on the page. **[new: carrillo2019]** — [UJI repository](https://repositori.uji.es/items/b1903fd5-5ff4-4010-8593-ef1f46e34e66/full)
- **Heekerens (2020 doctoral thesis, FU Berlin; Study 3 is a BPS meta-analysis that matches the Heekerens & Eid journal paper).** 34 RCTs. **Small effects on positive affect and optimism.** Effects were larger when measured immediately and "**appeared to vanish about one week after the intervention**." **No significant effects on negative affect, life satisfaction, depressive symptoms or happiness.** Effects were larger for momentary affect, and for optimism measured as specific positive expectations rather than a general life orientation. **Online delivery showed no significant effect on positive affect.** **[STRONG]**. **[new: heekerens2020]** — [FU Berlin Refubium](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/handle/fub188/27309?show=full)
- **Conflict:** Carrillo reports small benefits for negative affect and depressive symptoms; Heekerens finds none. Carrillo reports d+ = .325 for well-being; Heekerens finds no effect on life satisfaction. The two used different inclusion rules (Heekerens: RCTs only). — [Carrillo 2019](https://repositori.uji.es/items/b1903fd5-5ff4-4010-8593-ef1f46e34e66/full) vs [Heekerens 2020](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/handle/fub188/27309?show=full)
- BPS also had the largest effects among optimism interventions in Malouff & Schutte's meta-analysis. — [Malouff & Schutte 2017](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/21628)

### Inferences
- BPS is a *mood and expectation lift*, not a cure. Any lift probably needs to be renewed, so a regular rhythm suits it better than a one-off.
- Heekerens found no effect for online BPS on positive affect. An app version may do less than the lab versions, so it should be tested rather than assumed to work.
- Taken alone, BPS is a positive future fantasy. Section 4 suggests pairing it with obstacles and if-then plans.

### Gaps
- I did not open the Heekerens & Eid 2021 journal article itself, so numeric pooled effects from that version are missing.
- Neither meta-analysis gave country breakdowns on the pages opened. Cross-cultural evidence for BPS is unknown here.

## 4. Why positive fantasy alone backfires: Oettingen, mental contrasting, WOOP/MCII, and the risks of forced cheerfulness

### Takeaway
Dreaming about a good future feels good now but predicts *less* effort and *more* depressive symptoms later, unless the dream is paired with the obstacle that stands in the way and an if-then plan. This is mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII, or WOOP), which has a small-to-medium effect on reaching goals. Telling people to repeat positive statements or to avoid "negative" feelings can backfire, especially for people who already feel low.

### Cited Findings
- **Positive fantasies, "pleasure now, pain later" (Oettingen, Mayer & Portnow, 2016; Psychological Science).** Four studies: N = 88 students with 4-week follow-up; N = 109 schoolchildren aged 9–10 with 7 months; N = 78 students with 6 months, diary method; N = 148 students with 2 months. At each point in time, more positive fantasies went with **fewer** depressive symptoms (r = −.29 to −.43). Over time, more positive fantasies predicted a relative **increase** in symptoms (r = .22 to .45). In Study 4, fantasies predicted lower study effort (β = −.16) and lower academic success (β = −.23), and lower success partly explained the later rise in symptoms. Limitations: correlational, mostly female samples, substantial attrition, and an effort index with low reliability (α = .59). The authors suggest mental contrasting could make fantasies protective. **[MODERATE]** (four small correlational studies, students and children) **[WEIRD]**. **[new: oettingen2016]** — [Oettingen, Mayer & Portnow 2016 (author PDF)](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/oettingen-mayer-portnow-2016-published.pdf)
- **MCII/WOOP meta-analysis (Wang, Wang & Gai, 2021; Frontiers in Psychology).** 24 effect sizes, N = 15,907, **g = 0.336** (small to medium) on goal attainment. Egger's test was significant, and trim-and-fill reduced the estimate to **g = 0.242**. Age was not a moderator: children, college students, adults and patients were similar. **Delivery was the only significant moderator: experimenter-led g = .465 vs document-based g = .277.** Goal domain was not significant, with academic goals smallest at g = .255. One MOOC study made up about 87% of the sample. **"Most MCII studies were conducted in Western cultures."** **[STRONG]** **[WEIRD]**. **[library: wang2021]** — [Wang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full)
- **Positive self-statements can hurt (Wood, Perunovic & Lee, 2009; Psychological Science 20:860–866).** A survey found people often use affirmations and believe in them. In two experiments, **people with low self-esteem who repeated "I'm a lovable person" felt worse** than those who did not, while those with high self-esteem felt slightly better. Conclusion: such statements "backfire for those who most need them." **[MODERATE]**; sample sizes were not on the page opened. **[new: wood2009]** — [Wood et al. 2009 summary](https://contextualscience.org/publications/wood_perunovic_lee_2009)
- **Accepting negative emotions protects mental health (Ford, John, Lam et al.; JPSP vol. 115, listed as 2017 online).** Study 1, N = 1,003: habitually accepting one's thoughts and feelings predicted higher well-being and life satisfaction and fewer depressive and anxiety symptoms, beyond reappraisal and rumination. Study 2, N = 156, lab stressor: acceptance predicted less negative emotion, **without dampening positive emotion**. Study 3, N = 222: acceptance predicted less negative emotion during daily stressors, which in turn predicted better psychological health 6 months later. The benefit came from accepting *mental experiences*, not from accepting bad *situations*. **[MODERATE]**. **[new: ford2018]** — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2jb8x65t)
- **Aspirations in poverty: goals work when grounded.** Lybbert & Wydick describe "aspirations failure," where effort is held back by limited aspirations rather than by outer limits alone. They insist that hope "must rest on real opportunities" and that easing inner limits complements, but does not replace, easing outer ones. In Colombia (Cassar et al., cited in the chapter), **simply stating a goal** had the largest effect on enterprise outcomes of the treatments tested. — [Lybbert & Wydick, NBER chapter](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c13832.pdf)

### Inferences
- The difference between "empty positivity" and "grounded hope" has a fairly direct test in this research: **wish + named obstacle + if-then plan** beats wish alone. A self-reflection app can build this structure into its questions and steps.
- "Toxic positivity" as a named construct had no empirical source in this session. The evidence for avoiding forced cheer is Wood (2009: affirmations hurt people with low self-esteem) and Ford (2018: accepting, not fighting, negative feelings goes with better mental health). The people most likely to open a hope app on a bad day are the ones most at risk from upbeat affirmations.
- Experimenter-led MCII beat document-based MCII, so a static text prompt is probably the weaker form. Interactive, personalised prompts may sit somewhere between the two. This is untested.

### Gaps
- I did not open the newer WOOP trials in non-Western settings, nor Bastian et al. on social pressure not to feel sad.
- No source was opened on "toxic positivity" in workplaces or apps specifically.

## 5. Temporal landmarks and the fresh-start effect

### Takeaway
New beginnings such as a new week, month, year, birthday or term make people more likely to start aspirational behaviours. The boost is real but strongest at the landmark itself and then fades. The evidence comes from US field data and shows when people *start*, not whether they *keep going*.

### Cited Findings
- **Dai, Milkman & Riis, 2014 (Management Science).** Google searches for "diet" in the US, 2004–2012: **+14.4%** at the start of a week, +3.7% at the start of a month, **+82.1%** at the start of a year, +10.2% after federal holidays. University gym visits: **+33.4%** at the start of a week, +14.4% month, +11.6% year, **+47.1%** semester, +24.3% after breaks, +7.5% after birthdays other than the 21st. stickK commitment contracts: +62.9% week, +23.6% month, **+145.3%** year, +55.1% after holidays. Proposed mechanisms: landmarks open a **new mental accounting period** that leaves past failings in the "old" period; they create **distance from a past, imperfect self**; and they prompt **big-picture thinking**. The field data **did not directly test these mechanisms**. Motivation peaks at the landmark and fades, fastest after holidays. **Negative landmarks such as divorce or bereavement were not studied.** **[MODERATE]** **[WEIRD]** (all US data, Gregorian calendar). **[library: dai2014]** — [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014 (PDF)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)

### Inferences
- Weekly and monthly mirrors fall naturally on fresh-start points. A Monday or first-of-month mirror can invite one forward-looking intention while motivation is highest. Because the spike fades, pairing it with an if-then plan (section 4) is the logical complement.
- The "distance from the past self" mechanism links to hope: a mirror can mark a clean page ("that was last month's pattern") without erasing what the person learned. This has not been tested in an app.
- The authors note other calendars as future work. Landmarks such as the Lunar New Year, Nowruz, Ramadan or Diwali may matter more for many users worldwide.

### Gaps
- No source opened on persistence after fresh starts, failed fresh starts, or fresh starts outside the US.
- Dai et al.'s 2015 lab work on "putting imperfections behind you" was not opened.

## 6. Narrative identity and beliefs that change is possible (implicit theories, growth mindset)

### Takeaway
How people tell their own story matters. In therapy, clients' stories became more agentic ("I can affect what happens to me"), and that shift came *before* their mental health improved. Teaching people that abilities and personality can change ("growth mindset") has small, highly variable effects: close to zero on average for grades in the most rigorous analyses, somewhat larger for mental health and for disadvantaged groups. These are contested findings.

### Cited Findings
- **Agency in life stories precedes recovery (Adler, 2012; JPSP 102(2):367–389).** 47 adults in outpatient psychotherapy in Chicago, about 70% female and 70% white. They wrote narratives before therapy and after each session, up to 12 times (nearly 600 narratives). **Agency themes increased over therapy, and increases in agency preceded improvements in mental health by one to two sessions; the reverse was not significant.** Agency–mental health r = .29. Coherence did not change and was not related to mental health. Non-experimental, small sample. **[MODERATE]**, small **[WEIRD]**. **[new: adler2012]** — [Adler 2012 (PDF)](https://cdn2.psychologytoday.com/assets/2023-10/Adler%20%282012%29%20JPSP.pdf)
- **Growth mindset, two meta-analyses (Sisk, Burgoyne et al., 2018; Psychological Science).** (1) Mindset and achievement: 129 studies, N = 365,915, **r = .10**, I² = 96%, studies from over 20 countries. (2) Mindset interventions: 29 studies, N = 57,155, **d = 0.08**. For low-SES students **d = 0.34** (only 7 effect sizes, N = 577); for others d = 0.03. Conclusion: weak overall effects, "most were null," and resources might be better spent elsewhere. **[STRONG]**. **[new: sisk2018]** — [Sisk et al. 2018 (PDF)](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf)
- **National preregistered trial (Yeager et al., 2019; Nature).** 12,490 ninth-graders in 65 nationally representative US public high schools. Two online sessions of about 25 minutes teaching that abilities can be developed "through effort, new strategies, and appropriate help." Lower achievers' GPA rose by **0.10 grade points (0.11 SD)**. Enrolment in advanced maths rose from 33% to 36% (exploratory). Effects were larger where **peer norms supported challenge-seeking**. US public schools only. **[MODERATE]** (one large, preregistered study) **[WEIRD]**. **[library: yeager2019]** — [Yeager et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1466-y)
- **Psychological Bulletin 2023 meta-analysis, "For whom, how, and why" (doi 10.1037/bul0000368; authors not shown on the page opened).** 53 independent samples, randomised studies from 2002–2020. Academic achievement **d = 0.14** for targeted subsamples with high fidelity, with a prediction interval of −0.08 to 0.35 that **includes zero**. **Mental health d = 0.32** (95% CI .10–.54; prediction interval .07–.57). Social functioning d = 0.36 with a very wide prediction interval (−.50 to 1.22). The authors stress that effects vary widely. **[STRONG]** with high heterogeneity. **[new: psybull2023mindset]** — [IFP summary of bul0000368](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2023/meta-analyses-systematic-reviews/bul0000368/)
- **The critics' reply (Macnamara & Burgoyne, 2023; Psychological Bulletin 149:242–258).** Their meta-analysis of 63 studies found academic effect **d = 0.05**, and Tipton et al.'s reanalysis found d = 0.07. Among studies meeting best-practice standards the effect was **d = 0.02 (not significant)**. Authors with financial incentives were "two and a half times as likely" to report positive effects; Tipton et al. dispute that moderator. Macnamara and Burgoyne conclude that apparent effects "may be attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias." Sisk et al. had already found larger effects in studies that *failed* to change mindsets, which raises the question of whether mindset is the active ingredient. **[STRONG]** but contested. **[new: macnamara2023]** — [Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023 reply (PDF)](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/2023-90931-004.pdf)

### Inferences
- The narrative finding supports mirrors that reflect a person's own **moments of agency**, times they chose, acted or tried another way, rather than general uplift. Adler's finding is about timing and does not prove cause.
- "You can change" messages on their own carry little proven weight. The more defensible version is concrete: "you used a new strategy / asked for help and it moved something." That matches the content of Yeager's lesson (effort + strategies + help) and the agency and pathways parts of hope.
- The somewhat larger growth-mindset effects on mental health (d = 0.32) are encouraging for a reflection app. The wide variability means it should not be promised.

### Gaps
- **Redemptive life stories (McAdams)**, including their cultural specificity (the "redemptive self" as an American story) and the redemption-and-sobriety findings, were **not opened**. I make no claims about them. The PSPR review on the incremental validity of narrative identity (Adler et al.) was blocked by CAPTCHA pages.
- Single-session growth-mindset-of-personality interventions for adolescent depression (Schleider and colleagues) were not opened.

## 7. Hope in hardship worldwide; hopelessness and suicide; what a product should do

### Takeaway
In poverty, raising aspirations through role models and goal-setting can change behaviour and even long-run investment, but only where real opportunities exist. Hopelessness is one of the stronger risk factors for suicide relative to others, yet it is weak in absolute terms (odds ratios of about 2). A product cannot detect who is at risk by spotting hopelessness. The evidence-based response is to ask directly when answers are heavy (asking does not raise risk), point to human help, and draw on safety-planning ideas. The product should not pose as the intervention.

### Cited Findings
**Hope and aspirations in low- and middle-income countries**
- **Oaxaca Hope Project, Mexico (Lybbert & Wydick).** 601 microfinance borrowers in 52 community banks, randomised in matched pairs. The programme was a documentary, a goal-setting magnet and a four-week "hope curriculum" with **no business training**. At one month: aspirations index **+0.24 SD** (p < .01); agency +0.054 SD and pathways +0.036 SD, neither significant; Hope-7 index +0.17 SD. Sales +17.7%, profits +19.1% and savings +14.2%, **all with confidence intervals including zero**. Short-term only; Hawthorne effects possible; "where external constraints bind, psychological interventions may have little impact." **[EARLY]**, LMIC. — [Lybbert & Wydick, NBER chapter](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c13832.pdf)
- **Other LMIC evidence cited in the same chapter, not opened separately.** In India, female village leaders narrowed the gender aspiration gap by about 25% for parents and 32% for adolescents (8,453 adolescents, 495 villages; Beaman et al., 2012). In Jakarta slums, child sponsorship raised children's hope by **0.66 SD** and self-efficacy by 0.29 SD (526 children; Glewwe, Ross & Wydick). Across six countries, sponsorship raised schooling by about 1–1.5 years (10,144 adults). — [Lybbert & Wydick, NBER chapter](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c13832.pdf)
- **Role-model documentaries, rural Ethiopia (Bernard, Dercon, Orkin, Schinaia & Seyoum Taffesse).** RCT in which households watched documentaries about people from similar communities who escaped poverty through their own efforts. **Five years later, treated households had higher aspirations, especially lifetime aspirations, and had increased future-oriented investment in agriculture and children's education.** The authors' claim is that "a light-touch behavioural intervention can have persistent economic effects." Abstract only, so no sample size or effect sizes; one region. Lybbert & Wydick note the earlier short-run results raised aspirations across 64 villages but *did not* directly raise school enrolment. **[MODERATE]**, LMIC. **[new: bernard_futureinmind]** — [Oxford Economics](https://www.economics.ox.ac.uk/node/3545781)

**Suicide and hopelessness**
- **Global burden (WHO fact sheet, dated 28 Aug 2026).** More than 720,000 suicide deaths a year (727,000 given in the overview). **About 73% occurred in low- and middle-income countries in 2021.** Suicide was the **third leading cause of death among 15–29-year-olds**. Risk factors listed: mental disorders, especially depression and alcohol use; previous attempt; acting impulsively in a crisis over money, relationships or pain; conflict, disaster, abuse, loss, isolation; and vulnerable groups. WHO's LIVE LIFE approach: limit access to means, responsible media reporting, socio-emotional skills for adolescents, and **early identification, assessment, management and follow-up**. The fact sheet does not list hopelessness by name. **[STRONG]** (official statistics). **[new: who2026suicide]** — [WHO suicide fact sheet](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/suicide)
- **Hopelessness as a predictor, meta-analysis (Ribeiro, Huang, Fox & Franklin, 2018; British Journal of Psychiatry).** 166 longitudinal studies, 357 prediction cases, mean follow-up of about 9 years. Hopelessness predicted **ideation OR 2.19, attempts OR 1.95, death OR 1.98**. Depressive symptoms predicted ideation (OR 1.57) and attempts (OR 1.30) but **not death**. **No overall estimate exceeded OR 2.0**, and correcting for publication bias shrank them further. Hopelessness is "strong relative to other risk factors but still weak in absolute terms." The authors advise re-evaluating risk detection that relies on depression or hopelessness alone. **[STRONG]**. **[new: ribeiro2018]** — [Ribeiro et al. 2018 (Cambridge Core reader)](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/product/44413C7251A6471522724814003D813A/core-reader)
- **Asking about suicide does not raise ideation (Dazzi, Gribble, Wessely & Fear, 2014; editorial review published by Cambridge University Press, journal not named on the page opened).** 13 studies, 2001–2013, covering adolescents and adults in general and at-risk groups. **None found a significant increase in suicidal ideation from being asked.** Several found possible reductions in distress, or benefits from follow-up contact. Caveats: a narrative review without formal quality appraisal; some session-level increases appeared in one study (Reynolds et al.: 16.4% of sessions showed increases, 17.5% decreases). **[MODERATE]**. **[new: dazzi2014]** — [Dazzi et al. 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FCAEE9E5BC840D76CF10AEBECD921AC9)
- **Safety planning, meta-analysis (Nuij et al., 2021; British Journal of Psychiatry 219(2):419–426).** 6 studies, N = 3,536. Safety planning-type interventions cut suicidal behaviour: **RR 0.570** (95% CI 0.408–0.795), **number needed to treat 16**. There was **no significant effect on suicidal ideation**. **[STRONG]**, but only 6 studies. **[new: nuij2021]** — [Nuij et al. 2021 (Glasgow eprints)](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/238354)
- **Self-guided digital suicide prevention, meta-analysis (Torok et al., 2020; Lancet Digital Health).** 16 randomised comparisons, N = 4,398. Suicide-focused digital self-help reduced ideation after the intervention, **g = −0.23**. Depression-focused programmes had a non-significant effect (g = −0.12). **At follow-up the combined effect was g = −0.01, which is none.** Plans and attempts: OR 0.74, not significant. Trials came from Australia, the US, Belgium, the Netherlands and Germany, with high attrition, low adherence and some risk of bias; **all authors were employed by the Black Dog Institute, which develops such tools.** The authors call for trials in LMICs. **[STRONG]** that effects are small and short-lived **[WEIRD]**. **[new: torok2020]** — [Torok et al. 2020 summary (Centre for Suicide Prevention)](https://www.suicideinfo.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Suicide-prevention-using-self-guided-digital-interventions.pdf)

### Inferences
- Since hopelessness predicts suicide only weakly, a product should **not** try to "score" risk from mood patterns and stay quiet below a threshold. Every heavy answer should get the same low-threshold, respectful pointer to human help. Asking directly in that flow is supported by the evidence (Dazzi).
- Safety planning reduced suicidal *behaviour* (NNT 16) without reducing *ideation*. Helping someone know their own warning signs, coping steps and people to call is valuable even if their thoughts persist. In a non-therapy app, this means signposting to such a plan or to a helpline, not running a clinical intervention.
- Self-guided digital tools give at best a small, short-lived drop in suicidal thinking. A reflection app should treat human help, not its own content, as the response to hopelessness.
- With 73% of suicides in LMICs, helpline pointers must be localised by country and language and must work offline. A US-only number fails most of the world.
- In poverty, the LMIC studies suggest hope interventions work by **broadening the sense of what is possible** (aspirations) and **naming a goal**. Their effect is limited when outer barriers are binding. Grounded hope must not imply that individual effort alone can overcome structural hardship.

### Gaps
- Effectiveness of **crisis helplines** themselves, and safe messaging guidelines for apps (for example, WHO or Samaritans guidance), were not opened. The budget ran out.
- No source opened on Beck's original hopelessness studies or Franklin et al. 2017. Ribeiro et al. come from the same research group and cover the main point.
- No source opened on how hopelessness is expressed in different cultures, or on stigma around helplines in LMICs.

## 8. Post-traumatic growth (PTG) and its critiques

### Takeaway
Most people say they grew after adversity, but these are retrospective self-reports. When growth is measured before and after events, real gains are modest, appear only in some areas (self-esteem, relationships, mastery), are not found for meaning or spirituality, and **appear after positive events too**. Suffering is not required for growth, and self-reported growth may partly be a way of coping.

### Cited Findings
- **Prospective meta-analysis (Mangelsdorf, Eid & Luhmann; Psychological Bulletin, online Dec 2018).** 122 longitudinal studies, 154 samples, 364 effect sizes, **N = 98,436**. Only measures repeated over time were included; retrospective self-perceived growth was excluded. **Self-esteem, positive relationships and mastery** showed positive trends after **both negative and positive events**. Relationships first dipped and then rose above pre-event levels. **Meaning in life and spirituality showed no genuine growth.** There was no general support for negative events producing more growth than positive ones, so "**growth does not require suffering**," though few studies looked at positive events. Of the 25 studies with control groups, most found **no significant difference between the event group and controls**. Prior work had found that 58–83% of people *report* PTG. Frazier et al. (2009), cited in the paper, found that perceived and actual change agreed only for spirituality and that people overestimated their growth. Self-reported growth has been linked to anxiety, intrusive rumination and PTSD. **54% of studies were from the US; mostly Western samples.** **[STRONG]** **[WEIRD]**. **[new: mangelsdorf2019]** — [Mangelsdorf, Eid & Luhmann (PDF)](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2019/01/Mangelsforf-Eid-Luhmann-2018.pdf)
- **Consensus critique and agenda (Jayawickreme, Infurna et al., 2021; Journal of Personality 89(1):145–165; 25 authors).** Critics have pointed to "significant methodological limitations" in PTG research. The authors propose that PTG "can be more accurately captured in terms of personality change" and recommend more rigorous, open-science designs to establish how common, durable and causally driven such changes are. Only the abstract was available. **[MODERATE]** (expert review). **[new: jayawickreme2021]** — [Utrecht research portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/post-traumatic-growth-as-positive-personality-change-challenges-o/)

### Inferences
- An app should **never** promise that hardship will make someone stronger, and should not frame suffering as the price of growth. The best prospective data show that growth also follows positive events, and that event groups often do not differ from controls.
- When someone says "I've grown from this," the respectful response is to accept it as their meaning-making, without amplifying it into a promise or treating its absence as failure. Some self-reported growth goes with ongoing distress.

### Gaps
- I did not open the meta-analytic prevalence of moderate-to-high PTG, nor cross-cultural PTG data such as collectivist or religious meaning-making in LMICs.

## What this means for the app

### Takeaway
Grounded hope comes from three things a reflection app can actually provide: evidence from the person's **own** answers that they can act (agency) and find ways (pathways); honest naming of **obstacles** turned into **if-then steps**; and **renewal** at natural fresh starts. An insight can honestly promise more clarity, more visible options and small steps that raise the odds. It cannot promise that "everything will be all right." It must never replace that honesty with affirmations or cheer, and must never let a heavy answer pass without a pointer to human help.

### Cited Findings (design-relevant evidence, all cited above)
- Hope interventions: hope d = .22, distress d = .04 (not significant), so promise small shifts and not relief — [Weis & Speridakos 2011](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/2211-1522-1-5)
- BPS and optimism lifts fade within about a week, and online BPS showed no effect on positive affect, so renewal is needed and app effects must be tested — [Heekerens 2020](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/handle/fub188/27309?show=full); [Malouff & Schutte 2017](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/21628)
- Positive fantasies predict less effort and more depressive symptoms later; wish + obstacle + plan has g ≈ .24–.34 — [Oettingen et al. 2016](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/oettingen-mayer-portnow-2016-published.pdf); [Wang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full)
- Affirmations backfire for people with low self-esteem; accepting negative feelings protects — [Wood et al. 2009](https://contextualscience.org/publications/wood_perunovic_lee_2009); [Ford et al.](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2jb8x65t)
- Agentic self-narratives preceded recovery in therapy — [Adler 2012](https://cdn2.psychologytoday.com/assets/2023-10/Adler%20%282012%29%20JPSP.pdf)
- "You can change" messages have small, variable effects — [Sisk et al. 2018](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf); [Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/2023-90931-004.pdf)
- Fresh starts lift starting, not keeping going — [Dai et al. 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)
- Hopelessness predicts suicide weakly (OR ~2); asking does not raise ideation; safety planning cuts suicidal behaviour (RR 0.57); digital self-help effects are small and short-lived — [Ribeiro et al. 2018](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/product/44413C7251A6471522724814003D813A/core-reader); [Dazzi et al. 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FCAEE9E5BC840D76CF10AEBECD921AC9); [Nuij et al. 2021](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/238354); [Torok et al. 2020](https://www.suicideinfo.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Suicide-prevention-using-self-guided-digital-interventions.pdf)
- 73% of suicides occur in LMICs — [WHO](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/suicide)
- Growth after adversity is modest, follows good events too, and self-reports overstate it — [Mangelsdorf et al.](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2019/01/Mangelsforf-Eid-Luhmann-2018.pdf)

### Inferences (design guidance)

**Questions (morning and evening, concrete options, no scales)**
- Ask about **what people did**, not how hopeful they feel. Example (pathways): "Last time a plan fell through, what did you do next? Tried another way / Asked someone / Waited it out / Let it go." Example (agency): "This morning, which of these did you decide yourself? What to eat / When to start work / Who to message / None today." This builds a record of agency and pathways from behaviour, which section 1 shows are the active parts of hope. Any mapping onto "hope" is an unvalidated proxy and must never be shown as a score.
- Include **obstacle questions** right after any wish question (mental contrasting): "If tomorrow goes to plan, what's most likely to get in the way? Tiredness / Other people's needs / My phone / Worry / Something else." A wish question without an obstacle question copies the pattern Oettingen links to lower effort.
- Allow and normalise heavy answers. Every question should have honest negative options ("Nothing went right today"), consistent with the acceptance evidence. Do not design out the low end.

**Insights (after each answer)**
- **What an insight can honestly promise:** that the person has shown a capability before ("On Tuesday you found another way when the bus didn't come; that's a skill, and skills can be used again"); that naming an obstacle plus a plan makes follow-through more likely ("A when-then plan tends to help people act on what they intend"); and that patterns are information, not verdicts. Phrase effects as possibilities ("often helps," "can make it easier"), never outcomes ("will change your life").
- **Do not:** use affirmations ("You are strong, you are lovable"), which backfire for people with low self-esteem; tell people to look on the bright side; say "everything happens for a reason"; promise that hardship builds strength; or claim health benefits from optimism.
- **Do:** validate first, then point to agency. ("That sounds like a heavy day. You still chose to [X], which counts.") Acceptance of feelings and agency can sit together.
- Growth messages should be **concrete and strategy-based** ("trying a different approach or asking for help moved something last week"), not abstract "you can change anything." The abstract version has weak and contested evidence.
- For grounded "my life is going to turn around" feelings, tie forward-looking insights to **evidence the person produced**: "Three times this month you did [X] when [Y]. If that keeps up, [small, realistic consequence]." Hope then rests on the person's own record, which is honest and involves no comparison with others.

**Small steps (optional)**
- Present steps in **WOOP / if-then form**: "If [the obstacle you named] happens, then I'll [tiny action]." This is the best-evidenced format here (g ≈ .24–.34 after bias correction). Interactive, personalised prompts are probably better than static text, since experimenter-led MCII beat document-based.
- Steps must be **feasible without money, time or privileges** the person may not have. The LMIC evidence says hope interventions fail where outer limits bind, and that implying effort alone is enough would be false.
- A BPS-style prompt ("imagine things going well a year from now") should **always be followed** by "what's in the way?" and "what's one if-then for that?" Never use BPS alone, and never after a heavy answer.

**Weekly and monthly mirrors**
- Schedule mirrors at **fresh starts** (start of week, first of the month, optionally the user's own cultural new years). Frame them as a new chapter: "last month's pattern was ___; this month's page is blank." Invite one if-then intention while motivation is highest, and expect it to fade.
- Make mirrors **agency narratives**: highlight moments the person chose, tried, adapted or asked for help (from Adler, where agentic stories preceded recovery). Show change over time **within the person only**, never against others.
- Repeat the renewal. Hope and optimism lifts fade within about a week, so the daily-and-weekly rhythm suits this better than one-off "big insight" moments.
- Avoid redemption pressure. Do not turn every hard month into a growth story. If a month was simply hard, say so plainly and point to one thing the person did.

**When someone seems hopeless (heavy answers)**
- Do not try to predict risk from patterns and stay silent below a threshold. Hopelessness is a weak predictor (OR ~2), so **every** heavy answer should get a calm, human pointer to help, localised by country and language, given that 73% of suicides happen in LMICs.
- It is safe and appropriate to **ask directly and gently** in that flow ("Are you having thoughts of ending your life?"). Asking did not raise ideation in the studies reviewed.
- **Pause all positive-future content** after a heavy answer: no BPS, no fresh-start pep, no "things will get better" promises. Offer acceptance plus connection: a helpline, a trusted person, and optionally the parts of a personal safety plan (warning signs, what helps, who to contact). Safety planning cut suicidal behaviour even though suicidal thoughts persisted.
- The app should **not present itself as the intervention**. Digital self-help effects on suicidal thinking are small and gone by follow-up, so the job is to connect people to humans.

**What to avoid, summarised**
1. False promises ("everything will be all right," "your life will turn around") with no basis in the person's own data.
2. Forced cheer: affirmations, silver linings, "good vibes only," growth-from-suffering stories.
3. Fantasy without obstacles: dream prompts that are not followed by "what's in the way" and an if-then.
4. Implying effort alone overcomes poverty or structural barriers.
5. Missing hopelessness: relying on a risk score, sending upbeat content after heavy answers, offering US-only helplines.
6. Overselling "you can change" or the science. Every effect in this area is small to moderate.
