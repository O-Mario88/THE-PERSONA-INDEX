# Self-knowledge and freedom: economic (money, work, debt) and social (judgement, authenticity)

Conventions used in these notes:
- Grade: **[strong]** = meta-analysis or widely replicated; **[moderate]** = solid single study / large survey / RCT; **[early]** = small, new, pilot or fragile.
- Source status: **(lib: id)** = already in the project evidence library and re-opened this session; **(new: id)** = new source, with a suggested id. Library entries I could not re-open are only cross-referenced by id, never used for claims.
- **WEIRD** = rests only on Western, educated, rich samples. **LMIC** = low- and middle-income country evidence.
- Budget used: 25 web searches and 39 page fetches. Four fetches failed (Europe PMC and NCBI E-utilities were blocked by the proxy; the PubMed page for Gilovich 2000 returned a CAPTCHA twice; a Dartmouth PDF errored).

## 1. Which traits and habits predict financial well-being?

### Takeaway
How people actually handle money day to day (skill, follow-through, self-control, confidence they can reach a goal) predicts financial well-being far better than money knowledge does. Present bias ("now over later"), materialistic values and money-avoidant beliefs go with worse outcomes. But income and objective circumstances remain the biggest single driver, and almost all of this evidence is correlational and mostly from the US, UK or New Zealand.

### Cited Findings
- **Skill and confidence beat knowledge (US, cross-sectional).** In the CFPB's nationally representative 2016 survey (N = 6,394 in the model), financial skill was strongly linked to financial behaviour (standardised coefficient 0.398). Financial knowledge added almost nothing (0.038; the model worked as well without it). Financial self-efficacy (confidence in reaching financial goals) was also strongly linked to behaviour (0.324) and had a small direct link to well-being (0.093). Self-control → behaviour was 0.139. Behaviour → objective financial situation was 0.546, and situation → financial well-being was 0.820. Income had the largest link with financial situation (0.400). The model explained more than two-thirds of the variance. Cross-sectional, so associations only. US only. [moderate] (new: cfpb2018pathways) — [CFPB research brief (gflec copy)](https://gflec.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/bcfp_financial-well-being_pathways-role-financial-capability_research-brief.pdf?x98192); [CFPB summary page](https://consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/pathways-financial-well-being)
- **The behaviour measure was made of everyday habits:** paying bills on time, staying within budget, paying cards in full, checking statements, consulting a budget, setting goals and saving habits. — [CFPB brief](https://gflec.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/bcfp_financial-well-being_pathways-role-financial-capability_research-brief.pdf?x98192)
- **Childhood self-control predicts adult money outcomes (conscientiousness-type trait).** In the Dunedin cohort (n = 1,037, born 1972–73, New Zealand; self-control rated at ages 3–11 by teachers, parents, observers and self; 96% followed to age 32), lower childhood self-control predicted more difficulty with savings, home ownership, investments and retirement planning, plus more bankruptcy, missed payments, credit-card problems and living paycheck to paycheck. This held after accounting for IQ and family social class. A sibling comparison of 500 British twin pairs found that the twin with lower self-control at age 5 did worse at 12 (smoking, school, antisocial behaviour; not financial outcomes). Children whose self-control improved did better as adults than their early scores predicted. The factsheet gives no effect sizes. WEIRD (NZ/UK). [moderate] (new: moffitt2011) — [Moffitt et al. 2011 PNAS factsheet, Duke](https://moffittcaspi.trinity.duke.edu/sites/moffittcaspi.trinity.duke.edu/files/Moffitt_PNAS_factsheet.pdf)
- **Present bias goes with credit-card debt.** Among 541 low- and moderate-income US tax-help clients, those whose incentivised choices showed present bias had higher revolving credit-card balances (p < .01). This held after controlling for socio-demographics, credit limits and FICO scores, and also for balances one year later. The slides do not give the size of the effect. US only. [moderate] (lib: meier2010) — [Meier & Sprenger slides, AEJ: Applied 2010](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/banking/MeierSlides_NYU.pdf)
- **Materialism goes with lower well-being and riskier consumer behaviour.** A meta-analysis of 753 effect sizes from 259 samples found r = −.19 for multifaceted materialism measures and r = −.16 for the relative importance of materialist goals. Links were largest for risky health and consumer behaviours and for negative self-appraisals (r −.28 to −.44) and smallest for life satisfaction (r −.13 to −.15). Moderators included age, gender, settings that endorse materialism, cultures emphasising "affective autonomy", and economic growth. The authors suggest poorly met psychological needs may explain the link. Correlational. [strong] (new: dittmar2014) — [Dittmar, Bond, Hurst & Kasser 2014, JPSP (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/)
- **Compulsive buying is about 1 in 20 adults, but the estimate depends on the measure.** A meta-analysis of 40 studies (49 estimates, 16 countries, about 32,000 people) found pooled prevalence of 4.9% (95% CI 3.4–6.9%) in representative adult samples, 8.3% in university students, 12.3% in non-representative adult samples and 16.2% in shopping-specific samples. It was higher in younger people and women. Prevalence did not differ between US and non-US samples. Most of the heterogeneity came from the screening tool and the time frame used. Non-clinical samples only. [strong] (new: maraz2016; complements lib: koran2006) — [Maraz, Griffiths & Demetrovics 2016, Addiction (NTU repository)](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/27158/)
- **Money beliefs ("money scripts") are a pilot-level measure.** 422 web respondents were a convenience sample, mostly married, highly educated, middle-aged White women (82% Caucasian). The study found four scripts: *money avoidance* ("Rich people are greedy"), *money worship* ("More money will make you happier"), *money status* ("Your self-worth equals your net worth") and *money vigilance* ("You should not tell others how much money you have or make"). Avoidance, worship and status went with lower income and net worth. Worship went with carrying revolving card balances, and vigilance with not carrying card debt. Respondents who did not know their own net worth scored highest on avoidance. Compulsive buying was hypothesised but not measured. The authors call it a pilot and cannot say whether the beliefs come before the outcomes. WEIRD. [early] (new: klontz2011) — [Klontz et al. 2011, Journal of Financial Therapy (USU PDF)](https://qanr.usu.edu/fcse/files/money-beliefs-and-financial-behaviors-development-the-klontz-money-script-inventory-jft-2011.pdf)
- **Psychological traits account for part of the literacy–behaviour link.** In Fernandes et al.'s three empirical studies, the apparent effect of financial literacy on behaviour shrank substantially once psychological traits were controlled for or an instrumental-variable approach was used. — [Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer 2014, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2333898)

### Inferences
- The most "knowable" self-traits that matter are behavioural (do I follow through? do I avoid looking? do I prefer now?) and confidence-related (do I believe I can manage this?), not "how much do I know about money."
- In the CFPB model, income → situation (0.400) is large. Self-knowledge can shift behaviour at the margin but cannot substitute for income. An app should not imply that money difficulty is a personality failure.
- Avoidance, such as not knowing your own net worth or not opening statements, recurs as a marker (Klontz). The library's olafsson2017 (people check accounts less when in debt) points the same way, but I did not re-open it.

### Gaps
- I did not find and open a meta-analysis on Big Five conscientiousness and wealth or financial well-being specifically. Moffitt's self-control cohort is the closest proxy.
- Financial locus of control was not separately sourced. The CFPB "self-efficacy" construct is the closest.
- No LMIC evidence was found on trait predictors of financial well-being. Moffitt, CFPB, Meier and Klontz are all WEIRD.
- I found no effect sizes for "emotional spending" (spending to repair mood) as a distinct predictor.

## 2. Which money interventions measurably help?

### Takeaway
Classroom-style financial education has small effects: it was once judged near zero and decaying (Fernandes 2014), and a newer RCT meta-analysis finds them positive but still small (Kaiser et al.). Changing the structure of choices works better: commitments to save more later, commitment accounts and goal-linked reminders. But commitment devices can harm people who over-commit, so modest, self-chosen, low-penalty commitments are the safer design.

### Cited Findings
- **Financial education, pessimistic meta-analysis.** Across 168 papers and 201 studies, financial-literacy interventions explained only about 0.1% of the variance in financial behaviours. Effects were weaker in low-income samples. Even large programmes with many hours had negligible effects 20 or more months later. The authors recommend "just in time" education tied to a specific upcoming decision, alongside choice architecture and regulation. [strong] (new: fernandes2014) — [Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer 2014, Management Science (SSRN)](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2333898)
- **Financial education, more optimistic RCT-only meta-analysis.** 76 randomised experiments with more than 160,000 people found positive causal effects on both knowledge and downstream behaviours. The effects are "similar to those realized by educational interventions in other domains" and "at least three times as large" as earlier estimates. They are robust to publication bias, to restricting to adequately powered studies and to restricting to top journals. [strong] (lib: kaiser2020) — [Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban, NBER w27057](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27057)
  - An earlier presentation of the same project (68 RCTs, about 145,000 people) gave the sizes: Hedges' g = 0.205 for knowledge (47 studies) and 0.085 for behaviour (57 studies). It found "no evidence of 'rapid decay' but no evidence against it either". A Brazil RCT effect of 0.07 SD equalled an 11.2% increase in money saved. Re-weighted the same way, behaviour effects were 0.018 in Fernandes versus 0.049 in the new data. These numbers are from a 2019 conference version; the published figures may differ. (new: kaiser2019slides) — [Kaiser et al. slides, GFLEC 2019](https://gflec.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/10-Kaiser-Tim.pdf)
  - **Contested:** the two meta-analyses disagree mainly on metric (variance explained versus standardised effect) and on which studies they include. Both agree that effects on behaviour are small.
- **Commit now to save more later (Save More Tomorrow).** Of employees who had declined a consultant's recommended saving rate, 78% (162 of 207) joined a plan that raised their contribution at each future pay rise. 98% stayed through two raises and 80% through three. Average saving rates rose from 3.5% to 11.6% over 28 months. People who left kept their raised rate but stopped further increases. One company, not randomised; the page is a 2001 working-paper draft of the 2004 paper. WEIRD. [moderate] (new: thaler2004) — [Thaler & Benartzi, UCLA Anderson page](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/faculty/shlomo.benartzi/savemore.htm)
- **Commitment savings account, Philippines (LMIC, RCT).** Of 1,777 surveyed bank clients, 710 were randomly offered an account that restricted withdrawals until a self-chosen goal or date. 28.4% took it up. After 12 months, average savings balances in the treatment group were 81 percentage points higher than in the control group. Women whose survey answers showed more impatience about now than about the future (a present-bias pattern) were significantly more likely to open the account. [moderate] (new: ashraf2006) — [Ashraf, Karlan & Yin 2006, QJE (Yale ISPS)](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps06-009)
- **Commitment can fail and harm (LMIC, RCT).** Low-income clients randomly offered an instalment commitment-savings account chose their own plan and default penalty. About 55% defaulted and lost money; the penalties they chose were too low to overcome their self-control problems. Measures of *sophistication* (awareness of one's own present bias) went with lower take-up, fewer defaults and higher chosen penalties. Average savings effects were large, but most of those who defaulted were made worse off. Philippines; Management Science 2020. [moderate] (new: john2020) — [John, "When Commitment Fails", IPA](https://poverty-action.org/publication/when-commitment-fails-%E2%80%93-evidence-field-experiment)
- **Reminders (LMIC field experiments).** Three field experiments with three banks found that reminders to people who had recently opened commitment-savings accounts increased the share who reached their savings goal. Messages that mentioned *both* the person's own savings goal and financial incentives were "particularly effective". Gain versus loss framing made no difference, and extra late reminders added nothing. J-PAL lists Bolivia, Peru and the Philippines. I could not retrieve effect sizes. [moderate] (new: karlan2016reminders) — [Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan & Zinman 2016 working paper (IPA)](https://poverty-action.org/node/13096/pdf); [J-PAL listing (countries)](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/media/file-research-paper/getting-top-mind-how-reminders-increase-saving)
- **Savings groups (LMIC, clustered RCT).** In Ghana, Malawi and Uganda, promoting village savings groups improved household business outcomes and women's empowerment. There was no evidence of impacts on average consumption or other livelihoods. PNAS 2017; I could not retrieve sample size or effect sizes. [moderate] (new: karlan2017) — [Karlan, Savonitto, Thuysbaert & Udry 2017 (IPA)](https://poverty-action.org/node/35066/pdf)
  - A separate Malawi savings-group RCT (4,560 households, 380 villages) lists "results forthcoming". — [J-PAL evaluation page](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1631?lang=en)
- **Haushofer & Fehr's review** lists commitment savings accounts, savings reminders and lockable savings boxes among interventions that increased saving. — [Haushofer & Fehr 2014, Science (author PDF)](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf)

### Inferences
- What works tends to be small, specific and well timed: a nudge at the moment of decision, a commitment about the future, a reminder of one's own goal. A one-off lesson works less well. This matches Fernandes' "just in time" recommendation.
- John (2020) is a key caution: self-binding helps people who know their own weakness well. People who partly know it set commitments that are too weak and then pay for failing. Accurate self-knowledge, not just good intentions, determines whether commitment helps.
- Related library entries I did not re-open: madrian2001 (automatic enrolment), gollwitzer2006 (when-then plans, general goals), hershfield2011 (future-self images).

### Gaps
- **Implementation intentions specifically for saving:** I found no saving-specific RCT within budget. Only the general meta-analysis (lib: gollwitzer2006) exists, and I did not re-open it.
- **Tracking spending / budgeting apps:** I found no RCT evidence that tracking spending by itself improves outcomes. Treat it as untested here.
- I could not open the published Thaler & Benartzi (2004) version; only the 2001 draft figures (28 months, three raises) are cited. I could not get effect sizes for the reminders or savings-group papers.

## 3. Scarcity and the psychology of poverty (worldwide)

### Takeaway
Poverty plausibly worsens stress and mood, which may push toward short-sighted and risk-averse choices. But the step from stress to economic choice rests mostly on small lab studies, and the "scarcity mindset" literature replicates unevenly. Cash transfers reliably but modestly improve well-being in LMICs. Aspiration-raising interventions show small, fragile effects.

### Cited Findings
- **Feedback-loop model.** Poverty → stress and negative affect → short-sighted, risk-averse choices → harder escape from poverty. The authors stress that the driver is environment, not character. [moderate, review] (new: haushofer2014) — [Haushofer & Fehr 2014, Science](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf)
  - Evidence they cite: poorest income fifths in rich countries have 1.5–2× the depression and anxiety rates of the richest. 79% of 115 studies found a poverty–mental-health link. 18 of 25 studies of poverty alleviation found positive well-being effects, 5 mixed and 2 none. Kenyan farmers had higher cortisol during droughts, and nonfarmers did not. — [same](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf)
  - On stress → choices, the authors call this the "weakest link": evidence is mostly from small lab studies (N = 36–189). Chronic hydrocortisone cut risky choices from about 50% to just over 20% (N = 36), and sadness clips increased discounting of the future (N = 189). Acute versus chronic effects are unclear, and no poverty-trap nonlinearity has been shown. — [same](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf)
  - They summarise a US payday study (lib: carvalho2016): households surveyed before payday were more present-biased for money but not for effort tasks, consistent with liquidity rather than cognition. They describe Mani et al. (2013) as showing that financial worry reduced cognitive performance among the poor. — [same](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf)
- **Scarcity research replicates unevenly.** A 24-author audit started from papers citing a seminal scarcity publication and ran replications of 20 studies. It found "considerable variability in the replicability, with some strong successes and other undeniable failures". The authors deliberately declined to give a single replication rate for this heterogeneous field. PNAS 2021. [strong as an audit] (new: odonnell2021) — [O'Donnell et al. 2021 (Stevens repository)](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/publications/empirical-audit-and-review-and-an-assessment-of-evidentiary-value/); [Tübingen record](https://publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/129017)
- **Cash transfers and well-being, LMIC meta-analysis.** 45 studies with 116,999 people found subjective well-being d = 0.13 (95% CI 0.09–0.18) and mental health d = 0.07 (0.05–0.09), with an average follow-up of about two years. Transfer size, both relative to prior income and absolute, strongly predicted the effect. [strong] (new: mcguire2022) — [McGuire, Kaiser & Bach-Mortensen 2022, Nature Human Behaviour (Oxford INET)](https://www.inet.ox.ac.uk/publications/a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-the-impact-of-cash-transfers-on-subjective-well-being-and-mental-health-in-low-and-middle-income-countries)
- **GiveDirectly Kenya RCT.** Transfers of US$404 versus US$1,525 (PPP) raised monthly consumption from $158 to $193 PPP nine months on, with "large increases" in psychological well-being. There was no overall effect on cortisol; Haushofer & Fehr report that it fell only in the large-transfer group. Monthly instalments improved food security more, while lump sums went more to durables, which suggests savings and credit constraints. [moderate] (new: haushofer2016) — [Haushofer & Shapiro 2016, QJE (IDEAS)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v131y2016i4p1973-2042..html)
- **Aspirations intervention, rural Ethiopia (RCT, n = 2,063, 64 villages).** Households were randomised to four 15-minute documentaries about ordinary local people who improved their lives through their own effort, to a comedy placebo, or to survey only. After six months, aspirations were 0.03 SD higher than control (about 0.12 right after viewing). Savings were 97 birr higher (significant only at 10%). Enrolled children aged 6–15 rose by 0.21 versus control (not significant versus placebo), and school spending was 33 birr higher. There was no change in work hours, time preference or locus of control. Financial outcomes were **not significant after multiple-testing correction**. The study was exploratory, with no pre-analysis plan, and outcomes were self-reported. The authors say aspirations may not be the main mechanism. [early] (new: bernard_futureinmind) — [Bernard, Dercon, Orkin & Taffesse, "The Future in Mind" (IGC copy)](https://theigc.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Bernard-Dercon-Orkin-Taffesse-The-Future-in-Mind_BREAD.pdf)
- **Psychological support as an economic intervention.** Haushofer & Fehr cite a Ugandan trial in which group interpersonal psychotherapy improved people's ability to carry out daily economic tasks. — [Haushofer & Fehr 2014](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a self-reflection app, the honest frame is: *pressure narrows attention and tilts choices toward now, for anyone*. That is not "poor people decide badly", and it is not "you have a scarcity mindset", a label whose evidence is mixed.
- Money itself improves well-being modestly (d ≈ 0.13). Psychological framing is not a substitute for resources. This bounds what "self-knowledge" can do for economic freedom.
- The Ethiopian result suggests that seeing people like you succeed can shift aspirations a little, but the effect fades within months. Treat it as a hint, not a design principle.

### Gaps
- I did not open Mani et al. 2013 (Science) or any direct critique of it. Its status here rests on Haushofer & Fehr's description plus the O'Donnell audit.
- I did not retrieve the longer-term follow-up of the Ethiopian aspirations study, or decay curves for the cash-transfer meta-analysis.

## 4. Does knowing your own money triggers and patterns change behaviour?

### Takeaway
There is no direct trial showing that learning your "money type" or triggers changes behaviour. The indirect evidence is suggestive. People who accurately know their own present bias choose commitments that work. Those who partly know it over-commit and lose. Goal-linked reminders and "just in time" prompts help. Confidence and skill, more than knowledge, track good habits.

### Cited Findings
- **Accurate self-knowledge decides whether commitment helps.** Sophistication about one's own present bias went with lower take-up, fewer defaults and higher self-chosen penalties. About 55% of clients defaulted, and most defaulters ended up worse off. [moderate, LMIC] — [John 2020 (IPA)](https://poverty-action.org/publication/when-commitment-fails-%E2%80%93-evidence-field-experiment)
- **People drawn to commitment were those whose answers showed a present-bias pattern.** This held for women in the Philippine trial, suggesting some people act on their own sense of being impatient now. [moderate, LMIC] — [Ashraf, Karlan & Yin 2006](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps06-009)
- **Reminders that put a person's own goal back in mind raise goal completion.** Goal plus incentive messages worked best. [moderate, LMIC] — [Karlan et al. 2016](https://poverty-action.org/node/13096/pdf)
- **Behaviour is strongly linked to skill (0.398) and self-efficacy (0.324), not knowledge (0.038).** [moderate, US] — [CFPB brief](https://gflec.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/bcfp_financial-well-being_pathways-role-financial-capability_research-brief.pdf?x98192)
- **Education timed to the decision is recommended over general courses.** — [Fernandes et al. 2014](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2333898)
- **Money avoidance goes with not knowing one's own net worth.** The design is cross-sectional, so causation is unknown. [early, WEIRD] — [Klontz et al. 2011](https://qanr.usu.edu/fcse/files/money-beliefs-and-financial-behaviors-development-the-klontz-money-script-inventory-jft-2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- The useful kind of self-knowledge seems to be *calibrated and situational*: "when I'm tired on a payday evening I buy things I later regret", not a fixed type. It pays off most when linked to a concrete, well-timed action.
- Partial self-knowledge combined with a costly commitment can be worse than none (John 2020). Steps should therefore be small, reversible and penalty-free.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT testing whether reflection or self-monitoring alone (diaries, pattern mirrors) improves money outcomes. This is a genuine evidence gap that the app should not overclaim.

## 5. Freedom from judgement: spotlight effect, social anxiety, people-pleasing, conformity

### Takeaway
People feel far more watched and judged than they are, and they expect a refusal to land worse than it does. Social anxiety disorder affects about 4% of people over a lifetime worldwide, with higher measured rates in rich countries. Conformity varies by culture, so "not caring what others think" is not a universal ideal.

### Cited Findings
- **Spotlight effect.** Students wearing an embarrassing (Barry Manilow) T-shirt estimated that about 46% of peers noticed it; about 23% actually did, roughly a two-fold overestimate. A follow-up by the same lab (Gilovich, Kruger & Medvec 2002) extended the finding to other stimuli and older non-students, but it was not independent. I found no documented failed replication. The PubMed page was CAPTCHA-blocked this session; figures come via a secondary summary and match the library entry. US students. [moderate] (lib: gilovich2000) — [Secondary summary (Atticus Li)](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/spotlight-effect/); [library URL (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707330/)
- **Social anxiety worldwide.** 28 World Mental Health surveys (142,405 respondents) found DSM-IV social anxiety disorder at 4.0% lifetime, 2.4% over 12 months and 1.3% over 30 days. Lifetime rates were 5.5% in high-income, 2.9% in upper-middle-income and 1.6% in low/lower-middle-income countries. Onset is early, from mid-to-late adolescence into the early 40s. Persistence was highest in upper-middle-income countries, Africa and the Eastern Mediterranean. Treatment was lowest in low/lower-middle-income countries. About 79% of people with lifetime social anxiety disorder had another lifetime disorder. [strong] (new: stein2017) — [Stein et al. 2017, BMC Medicine](https://bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12916-017-0889-2)
- **Saying no costs less than people fear.** Five experiments with more than 2,000 participants found that people who declined an invitation overestimated how angry and disappointed the inviter would be and how much it would cut future invitations. In a pilot, 77% admitted accepting an invitation they did not want. The pattern held in couples across relationship lengths. The lead author still advises: "don't decline every invitation." JPSP 2023. The press release does not give sample countries. [moderate] (new: givi2023) — [APA press release on Givi & Kirk](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2023/12/say-no-invitation)
- **Conformity varies by culture.** Bond & Smith's (1996) meta-analysis of Asch line-judgement studies found significantly higher conformity in collectivist cultures (examples given: Japan, Hong Kong, Fiji) than in individualist ones (France, UK, USA). **Verified only through an educational secondary summary**, which gave no study counts, sample sizes or effect sizes. [strong design; verification weak] (new: bond1996) — [Themantic Education summary](https://www.themantic-education.com/ibpsych/2016/10/04/key-study-bond-and-smith-1996/)

### Inferences
- Two well-supported ways people misjudge others give the app honest, non-comparative insights: "you may be overestimating how much this was noticed" and "a no is usually received more mildly than expected".
- Lower measured social anxiety in poorer countries may partly reflect measurement or reporting differences. That is my inference and is not tested in the source. Either way, treatment access is lowest where rates are lowest.
- Because conformity norms differ by culture, the app should frame "going along" as a choice with costs and benefits, not as a flaw.

### Gaps
- **People-pleasing / sociotropy:** I found and opened no meta-analysis on sociotropy and well-being or depression within budget. Treat claims about people-pleasing as unsourced, apart from the Givi & Kirk decline studies.
- **Fear of negative evaluation** as a distinct trait: no dedicated source was opened.
- I could not obtain Bond & Smith's numbers or any newer conformity meta-analysis.

## 6. Authenticity, assertiveness/boundaries and autonomy support

### Takeaway
Feeling and acting like your "true self" correlates moderately with well-being (r ≈ .40 across 75 studies). The link varies with individualism–collectivism, and the evidence is correlational and built mostly on Western measures. I could not source the evidence on assertiveness training or autonomy-support interventions in this session.

### Cited Findings
- **Authenticity meta-analysis.** 75 studies (65 on well-being, 10 on engagement; N = 36,533) found authenticity correlated r = .40 with well-being and r = .37 with work engagement. Individualism–collectivism and type of measure significantly moderated the link; age, gender and sample type did not. The author notes most studies use Western or individualist concepts and measures. This page did not report the direction of the cultural moderation or facet-level results. Personality and Individual Differences 2020. [strong, correlational] (new: sutton2020) — [Sutton 2020 (Waikato Research Commons)](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/bafa6bd6-2e91-4e9a-9fb6-6d657d3faa7a)
- **Declining invitations is less costly than expected** (a boundary-setting proxy). — [APA on Givi & Kirk 2023](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2023/12/say-no-invitation)

### Inferences
- Authenticity and well-being move together, but the direction is unclear: feeling well may make people feel more authentic. The app should present authenticity as something to notice, not a cure.
- The cultural moderation means insights about "being yourself" should allow for authenticity expressed *within* relationships and duties, not only as standing apart.

### Gaps
- **Assertiveness training:** no trial or meta-analysis was opened in this session.
- **Autonomy support:** no SDT intervention meta-analysis was opened. The library has vasquez2016 (autonomy-supportive parenting, a meta-analysis), which I did not re-open.
- The direction of Sutton's individualism–collectivism moderation and facet results could not be retrieved; the full-text link served a different paper.

## 7. Autonomy versus wealth for well-being across cultures

### Takeaway
Across countries, feeling free to choose what you do with your life is one of the strongest correlates of national well-being, alongside income, social support and health. In one 63-country study, individualism and autonomy predicted lower distress better than wealth did. Both findings are country-level correlations, and there are signs of diminishing or even reversed returns at very high autonomy.

### Cited Findings
- **Autonomy versus wealth.** Data from 63 countries (420,599 individuals) on negative well-being (general distress, 54 countries, n = 260,449; anxiety, 24–28 countries; burnout, 25 countries, n = 124,149) showed individualism was "a consistently better predictor than wealth". In the anxiety and burnout studies, wealth's effect disappeared once individualism was entered. Gains from individualism levelled off, and in one study reversed, at the high end. Individualism's link with lower distress was steepest in poorer countries. Limits: country-level data only, many student samples, heavy UK/US contribution and a composite individualism index. JPSP 2011. [moderate] (new: fischer2011) — [Fischer & Boer 2011 (HSE PDF)](https://scr.hse.ru/data/2011/11/24/1271296504/fischer%20boer%202011%20psp.pdf)
- **"Freedom to make life choices" in the World Happiness Report.** Measured with the Gallup World Poll question "Are you satisfied or dissatisfied with your freedom to choose what you do with your life?". It is one of six factors, with log GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, generosity and corruption perceptions. Together the six explain more than three-quarters of national life-evaluation differences (adjusted R² = 0.757; 156 countries, 1,964 country-years, 2005–2022). Freedom's coefficient was 1.331 on a 0–1 scale versus 0.359 for log GDP; the units differ, so they are not directly comparable. Freedom is more strongly linked to positive emotions than to life evaluations, and its coefficient fell to 0.505 when emotions were added. Worldwide, correlational. [moderate] (new: whr2023ch2) — [World Happiness Report 2023, Chapter 2](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2023/world-happiness-trust-and-social-connections-in-times-of-crisis/)
- **Materialism's negative link with well-being was moderated by cultures emphasising "affective autonomy" and by economic growth.** — [Dittmar et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/)

### Inferences
- Wealth and freedom overlap: money buys options. But the *felt* sense of choice is a distinct, measurable correlate of well-being worldwide, and it is something the app can reflect back to people ("whose choice was this?").
- The levelling-off in Fischer & Boer is a reason not to equate freedom with maximal independence.

### Gaps
- I did not open Chirkov et al. 2003 (autonomy versus individualism in Korea, Russia, Turkey and the US) or Inglehart et al. 2008 on freedom and rising happiness. Neither is cited here.

## 8. What this means for the app

### Takeaway
The strongest and most honest levers are noticing patterns *in situations*, together with small, well-timed, reversible steps. The patterns are: now versus later, looking versus avoiding, spending to fit in, saying yes to avoid letting someone down, and overestimating how much others notice. The app should not imply that money trouble is a character flaw, should not label disorders, and should not oversell what self-knowledge or education can do.

### Cited Findings (evidence behind each design point)
- Everyday behaviours (paying on time, staying within plan, checking statements, setting goals) and confidence matter more than knowledge — [CFPB brief](https://gflec.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/bcfp_financial-well-being_pathways-role-financial-capability_research-brief.pdf?x98192)
- Education helps most just before the decision, and effects are small — [Fernandes 2014](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2333898); [Kaiser et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27057)
- Committing *future* increases is accepted and sticks — [Thaler & Benartzi](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/faculty/shlomo.benartzi/savemore.htm). Heavy self-penalties often backfire — [John 2020](https://poverty-action.org/publication/when-commitment-fails-%E2%80%93-evidence-field-experiment)
- Reminders naming the person's own goal raise goal completion — [Karlan et al. 2016](https://poverty-action.org/node/13096/pdf)
- Stress and pressure tilt toward now; poverty is environment, not character — [Haushofer & Fehr 2014](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf)
- People overestimate being noticed (about 2×) — [spotlight summary](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/spotlight-effect/). Declines land more softly than expected — [Givi & Kirk](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2023/12/say-no-invitation)
- Authenticity correlates with well-being, moderated by culture — [Sutton 2020](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/bafa6bd6-2e91-4e9a-9fb6-6d657d3faa7a). Felt freedom of choice is a strong worldwide correlate of well-being — [WHR 2023](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2023/world-happiness-trust-and-social-connections-in-times-of-crisis/)

### Inferences

**A. Everyday situations that reveal the patterns** (concrete options; no amounts, no rating scales):
1. *Now versus later (present bias)* [Meier & Sprenger; Ashraf et al.]: "Pay arrives this morning. What happens first?" Options: buy something I've been waiting for / pay what's due / move some aside before anything else / not decided, I'll see how the week goes. Or: "A small treat on the way home, or keep it for the weekend plan?"
2. *Looking versus avoiding (money avoidance)* [Klontz]: "Your bank app shows a new notification." Options: open it now / open it later today / leave it until I feel ready / I've turned those notifications off.
3. *Spending to fit in or for status (materialism, money status)* [Dittmar; Klontz] **and people-pleasing** [Givi & Kirk]: "Friends plan an outing that costs more than you'd planned." Options: go and say nothing / suggest a cheaper plan / say honestly it doesn't fit this month / make an excuse. This one situation reveals both economic and social freedom.
4. *Mood-driven buying* [Maraz; Haushofer & Fehr on stress]: "After a draining day you're scrolling a shopping app." Options: buy what caught my eye / leave it in the basket until tomorrow / close the app / message someone instead.
5. *Follow-through and self-efficacy* [CFPB; Moffitt]: "A money form or bill you've been putting off is still on the table." Options: deal with it now / give it a time slot / ask someone to sit with me / leave it.
6. *Committing your future self* [Thaler & Benartzi; John]: "You get a small pay rise or a windfall." Options: spend it / put part aside automatically / pay something down / haven't thought about it.
7. *Spotlight* [Gilovich]: "You notice a stain on your sleeve just before meeting people." Options: go home to change / cover it / mention it with a laugh / carry on and hope no one sees.
8. *Saying no* [Givi & Kirk]: "A friend invites you to something you don't want to go to." Options: yes, and go / yes, then cancel later / no, with the real reason / no, with a small excuse.
9. *Going along with the group (conformity)* [Bond & Smith, cultural caveat]: "In a group chat everyone backs a plan you think won't work." Options: agree / stay quiet / tell one person privately / say so in the chat.
10. *Felt freedom (autonomy)* [WHR; Fischer & Boer]: "Who decided how you spent your free evening?" Options: I did / someone else did, and I was fine with it / someone else did, and I wasn't / no one, it just happened.

**B. What an insight can honestly say** (about the person's own pattern, never ranking them against others):
- Present bias: "This week you chose 'now' in most money moments that came up in the evening. Pressure and tiredness tend to tilt anyone toward now, so this may be about timing more than willpower." This is grounded in Haushofer & Fehr's stress→choice link, which they call the weakest link, so phrase it as "may".
- Avoidance: "You've often chosen 'later' for looking at money. Avoiding a look is a common way to protect your mood, and it can also make the next look harder." The second half is inference; keep it soft.
- Status or fitting-in spending, named politely: "Several of your answers put keeping up with others ahead of your own plan. Valuing money and status highly tends to go with feeling worse rather than better." Grounded in Dittmar; correlational, so say "tends to go with".
- Spotlight: "You often chose to fix or hide small slips. Research finds people tend to guess that about twice as many others noticed as actually did."
- Saying no: "You said yes to avoid letting people down in most of these moments. In studies, people who declined usually found the other person less upset than they'd feared."
- Autonomy: "Your free time this month was mostly decided by others. Feeling free to choose how you live is closely tied to well-being around the world." This is a country-level correlation; avoid causal wording.
- Negative traits can be named politely and should always be paired with the situation that triggers them ("on payday evenings you…", not "you are impulsive").

**C. Small steps the evidence supports** (optional, reversible, no amounts, no product advice):
- *Decide before the moment.* Pick one future trigger and an action ("Next time pay arrives, I'll set something aside before I spend"). This draws on Save More Tomorrow and commitment accounts [moderate] and the general when-then evidence in the library (gollwitzer2006, not re-opened). Keep it **small and penalty-free**: John 2020 shows that over-ambitious self-penalties make most defaulters worse off.
- *Tie future increases, not present sacrifice.* "If you already set money aside automatically, consider linking an increase to your next pay rise." [Thaler & Benartzi, moderate, WEIRD]
- *A reminder of your own goal, in your own words.* Offer to resurface the goal the person named at a time they choose. [Karlan et al. 2016, moderate, LMIC]
- *Just-in-time micro-learning.* One short, relevant explanation at the moment of a decision, not a course. [Fernandes recommendation; Kaiser effect sizes small]
- *Build skill and confidence through one concrete act.* Look at one statement, pay one item on time, write one goal. These map onto the CFPB behaviour items. [moderate, US, correlational]
- *Test the spotlight.* After a small slip, note whether anyone actually mentioned it. [Gilovich, moderate, WEIRD]
- *Practise one small honest no.* Decline one low-stakes request with a real reason, then note the other person's reaction. Keep in mind the author's advice not to decline everything. [Givi & Kirk, moderate]
- *Notice one choice that was yours.* An evening "whose choice was this?" reflection. [WHR / Fischer & Boer, correlational: the reflection itself is untested]

**D. What to avoid:**
- Implying money difficulty is a personality defect. Income and circumstances dominate (CFPB income → situation 0.400), and poverty's effects run through environment and stress (Haushofer & Fehr). Do not use "scarcity mindset" as a label; the evidence is mixed (O'Donnell 2021).
- Promising that learning about money or about yourself will fix outcomes. Education effects are small (g ≈ 0.085 on behaviour), and no trial shows that reflection alone improves money outcomes.
- Encouraging strict commitments with self-punishment, or "all-or-nothing" savings challenges (John 2020).
- Labels that sound diagnostic: "compulsive buyer", "social anxiety", "people-pleaser" as an identity, "money avoidance disorder". The app is not therapy or a diagnosis. Where answers suggest persistent distress around buying or social situations, a neutral pointer to outside support fits the prevalence data (compulsive buying about 5%, social anxiety disorder 4% lifetime) without labelling the person.
- Presenting "money scripts" as a validated typology (one pilot, convenience sample, WEIRD).
- Treating conformity or deference as failure. Conformity norms differ by culture (Bond & Smith), authenticity's link varies with individualism–collectivism (Sutton), and autonomy's benefits level off at high levels (Fischer & Boer). Offer "your choice, your reasons" framing.
- Comparing with others ("most people save more than you") or naming amounts, both excluded by the app rules. Insights that cite general research tendencies should stay about the person's own pattern.
- Inspirational "success story" content as a lever. The Ethiopian documentary effects were small, faded within months and did not survive multiple-testing correction.

### Gaps
- The biggest gap for the app: no RCT tests whether weekly or monthly pattern "mirrors" or self-monitoring alone change money or social behaviour. Any claims the app makes about its own effect would be unsupported.
- I found no evidence on cooling-off or "wait a day before buying" rules, on spending trackers, or on assertiveness training. These steps would be plausible but unsourced in these notes.
- Most social-judgement and money-belief findings are WEIRD (US students or US convenience samples). The LMIC evidence is concentrated in savings and cash-transfer trials (Philippines, Kenya, Ethiopia, Ghana, Malawi, Uganda, Bolivia, Peru) and in the cross-national WHR, World Mental Health and Fischer & Boer data.
