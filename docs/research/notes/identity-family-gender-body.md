## 1. Family roles: parent identity, caregiving, being a son or daughter, family obligation across cultures

### Takeaway
Parenthood reliably adds meaning but not reliably happiness. Whether parents are happier than non-parents depends on:
- who they are: their age, whether they have a partner, and whether they are a mother or a father;
- what they measure: meaning, momentary mood or overall life satisfaction;
- where they live: family policy erases the happiness gap in some countries.

Family duty is not simply a burden. Across backgrounds, helping one's family usually feels like fulfilling a valued role and goes with more happiness. It turns heavy mainly when caregiving involves difficult behaviour or little sense of control (mastery).

### Cited Findings

**Parent identity**

- **[NEW] Nelson, Kushlev & Lyubomirsky, Psychological Bulletin (in press 2013, published 2014).** Grade: strong for the overall "it depends" and meaning conclusions; moderate for the subgroup findings. WEIRD flag: the authors say most studies used Western samples and may not generalise to non-Western, developing, poor or autocratic nations. — [Nelson et al. author PDF](http://sonjalyubomirsky.com/files/2012/09/Nelson-Kushlev-Lyubomirsky-in-press1.pdf); record at [World Database of Happiness](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/the-pains-and-pleasures-of-parenting-when-why-and-how-is-parenthood-associated-with-more-or-less-well-being-10912/)
  - **Overall result is mixed.** The review concludes that "some studies indicate that parents are happier than nonparents, whereas others suggest the reverse". It argues that whether parents are more or less happy "is not the most meaningful one" of the questions. Parents are unhappy to the extent that they meet relatively more negative emotions, financial strain, sleep disturbance and strain in the couple relationship. They are happier when they experience greater meaning in life, met basic needs, more positive emotions and enhanced social roles and identity.
  - **Meaning in life is consistently higher for parents.** This holds across national samples, experience sampling and day-reconstruction methods. Global happiness, life satisfaction and depression results are inconsistent.
  - **Childcare in daily life is lower in pleasure but rewarding.** Childcare ranked fairly low among daily activities: 12th of 16 for women in one study and 9th of 18 in another. Yet parents reported more positive affect during childcare than during the rest of their day. Time with children ranked 4th of 18 for reward.
  - **Age.** In one analysis, young parents (17–25) were less satisfied than childless peers of the same age, parents aged 26–62 were more satisfied, and older parents did not differ.
  - **Partner status.** Married parents generally did not differ from married non-parents. Unmarried parents reported lower happiness and satisfaction.
  - **Fathers and mothers.** Some studies found that parenthood went with higher life satisfaction only for fathers. Mothers showed larger declines in marital quality across the transition to parenthood.
  - **The early boost fades.** The lift in life satisfaction around pregnancy and birth often fades, with a return to baseline within about 2 years in one study.
  - **Why someone has no children matters.** In one study, infertile women were less happy than mothers, while voluntarily childless women did not differ from mothers.
  - **Limits.** The review is not a meta-analysis: it says the studies' regression-based designs prevented pooling. Selection is a problem, because happier people may be more likely to become parents.
  - **Contested.** The review itself presents the parenthood–happiness question as unresolved.
- **[LIB:glass2016] Glass, Simon & Andersson, 2016.** Grade: moderate. Across 22 rich countries, parents were often less happy than non-parents, most of all in the US. Where paid leave, sick days and cheaper childcare were common, the gap disappeared, and in Norway and Hungary parents were happier. WEIRD flag: European and English-speaking countries, data from 2006–2008. — [Glass et al. brief](https://contemporaryfamilies.utah.edu/publications/posts/2016/june/brief-parenting-happiness.php)
- **[NEW] Girme, Park & MacDonald 2023, citing other studies.** Grade: moderate. WEIRD flag: Europe only. — [Girme et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475216)
  - Single mothers were less satisfied than childless singles in 13 of 24 European countries, equally satisfied in 10, and more satisfied in 1 (Pollmann-Schult 2018, N = 56,431).
  - Supportive family policies reduce this gap (Maldonado & Nieuwenhuis 2015, N = 519,825).

**Being a son or daughter, and family obligation**

- **[NEW] Telzer & Fuligni 2009, Developmental Psychology 45(4):1177–1189.** Grade: moderate.
  - **Design:** a daily-diary study of 752 adolescents aged 14–15 from Latin American, Asian and European backgrounds.
  - **Results:** Daily family assistance "generally was not stressful for adolescents" and was associated with higher happiness. The happiness link was largely explained by the sense of role fulfilment that helping gave. There were few individual or group differences.
  - **Authors' interpretation:** family assistance is a meaningful activity that creates a sense of connection to the family.
  - **Caveats:** The abstract I saw does not state the country, though the backgrounds suggest a migrant-receiving setting. Teenagers, not adults. — [Telzer & Fuligni 2009, Illinois Experts](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/daily-family-assistance-and-the-psychological-well-being-of-adole/)
- **[NEW] Khalaila & Litwin 2011, Aging & Mental Health 15(6):679–686.** Grade: moderate. Non-Western.
  - **Design:** 250 randomly sampled adult-child caregivers of elderly Arab parents in Israel (94% response rate), described as traditional caregivers in a population in transition. Cross-sectional, analysed with path analysis.
  - **Results:** Filial piety did not lower depression directly. It "worked indirectly through caregiving burden": stronger filial piety went with lower burden, and lower burden with fewer depressive symptoms.
  - **Mastery:** A sense of mastery was a major protective predictor of depression and mediated the burden-to-depression link. — [Khalaila & Litwin 2011, Hebrew University](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/does-filial-piety-decrease-depression-among-family-caregivers/)
- **[LIB:zhao2025] Zhao et al. 2025.** Grade: moderate. Across 20 studies of 11,434 children, warm grandparenting went with less low mood and worry, and harsh or controlling grandparenting with more. 18 of the 20 studies were from China; all were cross-sectional. This is one of the few non-Western family meta-analyses in the library. — [Zhao et al. 2025, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11851558)
- **[LIB:pew2019living] Pew Research Center 2019.** Grade: moderate. Across 130 countries, 38% of people live in extended-family households, the most common arrangement worldwide, against 11% in the US. — [Pew 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/12/13/key-findings-how-living-arrangements-vary-by-religious-affiliation-around-the-world/)

**Caregiving identity and burden**

- **[NEW] Pinquart (& Sörensen), J Gerontol B Psychol Sci Soc Sci 58(2):P112.** Grade: strong. The page showed the abstract and the corresponding author (Pinquart, with a co-affiliation at the University of Rochester). I reconstructed the citation from the journal URL and the search-result title "Associations of stressors and uplifts of caregiving with caregiver burden and depressive mood"; the year (2003) is inferred from the volume.
  - **Scope:** a meta-analysis that "integrated findings from 228 studies" on six caregiving stressors plus caregiving uplifts.
  - **Strongest stressor:** the care recipient's behaviour problems were more strongly linked to burden and depressed mood than other stressors.
  - **Dementia care:** amount of care and physical impairment mattered less for dementia caregivers than for caregivers of older adults without dementia.
  - **Spouses and adult children:** for spouses, physical impairment and behaviour problems were more strongly tied to burden than for adult children.
  - **Gaps in the abstract:** no numeric effect sizes. Countries are not stated.
  - [Pinquart meta-analysis, Journal of Gerontology B](https://psychsoc.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/58/2/P112)
- **[LIB:grigoryeva2017] Grigoryeva 2017.** Grade: moderate. In US data, daughters gave more care to older parents than sons. Sons gave less when they had a sister, and daughters gave more when they had a brother. WEIRD flag: US only. — [Grigoryeva 2017, HRS](https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/bibcite/reference/13585)

### Inferences
- **The identity is the source of both meaning and strain.** Nelson et al. list "enhanced social roles and identity" as a pleasure of parenting. Telzer & Fuligni show that the role-fulfilment part of helping carries the happiness. So the same role feels freeing or confining depending on whether it is experienced as one's own, valued role, or as an imposed load without control (mastery).
- **Links to the core cravings:**
  - **Belonging:** family assistance, as connection to the family.
  - **Contentment and meaning:** parenthood is reliably higher in meaning, not reliably higher in moment-to-moment pleasure.
  - **Security and freedom:** the policy context changes how parenthood feels (glass2016), so the strain is partly structural, not a personal failure. That is an honest and hope-preserving point for an insight.
  - **Faith in people and success:** mastery protects caregivers.
- **Insight content (inference):** "a duty can be heavy and meaningful at the same time" is supported. "Duty is bad for you" is not supported.
- **Grief over unchosen childlessness:** Nelson's comparison of infertile and voluntarily childless women suggests that not being a parent by choice versus not by choice are different experiences. The app should not treat non-parents as one group.

### Gaps
- **Fuligni's cross-national family-obligation work** was not opened. This includes adult children and filial obligation in East Asia, South Asia and Africa, beyond Telzer & Fuligni 2009 and Khalaila & Litwin 2011. Budget limit.
- **Caregiving effects:** no effect sizes were available for caregiving burden. No large non-Western caregiver meta-analysis was opened.
- **Critiques of the parenthood findings** were not opened, for example reanalyses that question the "parents are happier" result. The Nelson review itself frames the evidence as mixed.
- **Parenthood in collectivist or low-income countries:** I found no opened evidence. Nelson et al. explicitly flag this gap.

## 2. Partner identity: relationship status and singlehood, attachment security, losing oneself, divorce

### Takeaway
On average, partnered people report somewhat higher life satisfaction than single people. That average varies by country (including reversals), shrinks after accounting for the circumstances around transitions, and hides large differences among single people. Singles thrive when:
- they have good friendships;
- they have low fear of being single and low attachment anxiety;
- they face little pressure or stigma.

Family pressure to couple weighs more in interdependent cultures. Losing a partner can blur one's sense of who one is.

### Cited Findings

**Relationship status and singlehood**

- **[NEW] Girme, Park & MacDonald 2023, Perspectives on Psychological Science 18(5):1097–1120.** A narrative, not systematic, review. Grade: moderate overall. WEIRD flag: the authors say most studies are WEIRD and correlational. — [Girme et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475216)
  - **Single versus partnered:**
    - Between-group studies generally find singles report lower life satisfaction than partnered people.
    - Lucas & Dyrenforth (N ≈ 170,000, 70+ countries) found the link varies by nation: positive, null, even negative (singles happier).
    - Within-person studies show people are already happier just before marriage (160 effect sizes, N = 9,292) and unhappier just before divorce (41 effect sizes, N = 1,828). The authors read this as reflecting the circumstances leading up to these transitions, not partner status itself.
  - **Inner factors:**
    - Singles high in social-avoidance goals were as satisfied with life as coupled people (Girme et al. 2016, N = 187 and 4,024).
    - Lower desire for a partner was consistently linked to better outcomes, with an exception for divorced men (Kislev 2021, N = 17,086).
    - Stronger fear of being single predicted lower satisfaction with singlehood (Adamczyk et al. 2021, N = 175; early).
    - Satisfaction with singlehood appears to rise after about age 40 (Böger & Huxhold 2018, N = 6,188).
  - **Attachment:** Singles high in attachment anxiety had lower life satisfaction, less satisfaction with singlehood and more desire for a partner. Attachment avoidance went with lower life satisfaction but not lower satisfaction with singlehood (MacDonald & Park 2022, N = 1,930).
  - **Sexual satisfaction:** Sexually satisfied singles reported higher life satisfaction, though average sexual satisfaction among singles was below the scale midpoint (Park & MacDonald 2022, N = 1,238).
  - **People around them:**
    - High-quality friendships went with greater life satisfaction and satisfaction with singlehood (Park et al. 2021, N = 3,890).
    - Singles often report less perceived support (Ta et al. 2017, N = 6,955), yet some studies show singles have stronger ties to friends and family.
  - **Society and culture:**
    - Family pressure to couple went with poorer well-being, best documented in Asian contexts (Himawan et al. 2018).
    - Family approval mattered more in interdependent countries (e.g., Indonesia, Japan) than individualistic ones (Australia, Canada) (MacDonald et al.).
    - Day-to-day discrimination against singles ("singlism") was linked to lower well-being (Girme et al. 2022).
    - People stereotype singles as less happy and less likable, though singles did not differ on most actual personality and well-being measures except relationship satisfaction (Greitemeyer 2009).
    - Postmaterialist values went with greater happiness among singles over 30 in the European Social Survey (Kislev 2018).
- **[NEW] Luhmann, Hofmann, Eid & Lucas 2012, JPSP 102(3):592–615.** Grade: strong. WEIRD flag: likely, but sample countries were not visible to me. — [Luhmann et al. 2012, DIW abstract page](https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.775342.de/s_7285.html)
  - **Scope:** a meta-analysis of longitudinal data from 188 publications (313 samples, N = 65,911) covering family events (marriage, divorce, bereavement, childbirth) and work events.
  - **Main result:** life events have "very different effects on affective and cognitive well-being". Effects on life satisfaction are stronger and more consistent. How much events change well-being does not track how desirable they are thought to be.
  - **Missing:** per-event effect sizes; the full text was blocked by a PMC captcha.

**Losing oneself, and divorce or breakup**

- **[LIB:slotter2010] Slotter, Gardner & Finkel 2010.** Grade: moderate. Across three studies, people often felt less sure of who they were after a breakup, and this blurrier sense of self went with more sadness. WEIRD flag: US students and adults. — [Slotter et al. 2010, ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/03/100308132139.htm)
- **[LIB:aron1997] Aron et al. 1997.** Grade: moderate. Gradual mutual self-disclosure builds closeness between strangers. This is the "self-expansion" research programme's best-known method, and a cross-reference only. — [Aron et al. 1997](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167297234003)

**What sustains a partner identity** (cross-references only, already in the library's partnership area)

- **[LIB:joel2020] Joel et al. 2020.** Grade: strong. Across 43 studies of more than 11,000 couples, feeling a partner is committed, feeling appreciated and sexual satisfaction predicted relationship happiness far more than either person's personality. — [Joel et al. 2020](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/machine-learning-uncovers-the-most-robust-self-report-predictors--3/)
- **[LIB:falconier2015] Falconier et al. 2015.** Grade: strong. Couples who handle stress as a team are clearly happier. — [Falconier et al. 2015](https://folia.unifr.ch/global/documents/287390)

### Inferences
- **Security and hope:** Being single is not in itself a deficit. The research points to attachment anxiety and fear of being single as what drains single people's well-being, and friendships and low partner-seeking pressure as what sustains it. Self-knowledge here means noticing whether one's wish for a partner is driven by fear or by desire.
- **Belonging and freedom:** Whether a person feels free in singlehood depends partly on family approval. This matters more in interdependent cultures, so an insight must never imply "just ignore your family". Insights can name the pressure without blaming it.
- **Losing oneself:** The library evidence (slotter2010) covers loss of self-clarity after a breakup. That a person "loses themselves" during a relationship rests only on general self-expansion theory. I did not verify it in this session, so treat it as an unverified idea, not a finding.
- **Contentment:** Because the life-event meta-analysis finds effects on mood and on life judgements diverge, a person may feel fine day to day while judging their life worse after a divorce, or the reverse. Mirrors should not collapse these into one.

### Gaps
- **Attachment security in couples:** no meta-analysis was opened. I had only the singles attachment data in Girme et al. Budget limit.
- **Self-concept change in love:** Aron's self-concept-change studies (1995) and later work on losing or "pruning" parts of oneself in a relationship were not opened.
- **DePaulo:** her work on singlism was not opened directly; singlism is covered only via Girme et al.
- **Divorce and identity:** no per-event effect sizes were available because Luhmann's full text was blocked. No non-Western divorce evidence was opened.

## 3. Gender: roles, masculinity norms and help-seeking, pressure of expectations, gender equality at country level

### Takeaway
Holding tightly to certain masculine norms is reliably linked to poorer mental health and moderately to less help-seeking. These norms are self-reliance, power over women and "playboy" norms; commitment to work is not linked. The pressures on women show up most clearly as unequal unpaid care and mental load. Country-level equality and family policy shape how gendered roles feel. The specific gender-equality and well-being literature was not opened in this session, so country-level claims here are partial.

### Cited Findings
- **[NEW] Wong, Ho, Wang & Miller 2017, Journal of Counseling Psychology 64(1):80–93.** Grade: strong. I read the abstract via the Culture Reframed library page; the original is on APA PsycNet (not opened). — [Wong et al. 2017 abstract, Culture Reframed](https://culturereframed.org/library-article/meta-analyses-of-the-relationship-between-conformity-to-masculine-norms-and-mental-health-related-outcomes/)
  - **Scope:** meta-analyses of 78 samples, 19,453 participants.
  - **Overall:** conformity to masculine norms was "modestly and unfavorably associated with mental health". It was "moderately and unfavorably related to psychological help seeking".
  - **Social versus psychological:** links were stronger with negative social functioning than with psychological indicators of poor mental health.
  - **Specific norms:** self-reliance, power over women and playboy norms were unfavourably, robustly and consistently related to mental health. Primacy of work was not significantly related to any mental-health outcome.
  - **Authors' recommendation:** study specific norms, not "masculinity" as a whole.
  - **Caveat:** the countries of the samples are not stated in the abstract I saw.
- **[NEW] Bostwick et al. 2014, American Journal of Orthopsychiatry 84(1):35–45.** Grade: early to moderate. WEIRD flag: US data; small subsample; cross-sectional.
  - **Design:** 577 lesbian, gay and bisexual US adults from a national survey (NESARC, 2004–05).
  - **Gender discrimination:** gender discrimination alone was associated with higher odds of a past-year mood or anxiety disorder (adjusted odds ratio 2.28). Its score was the only frequency measure that predicted disorder.
  - **Combined discrimination:** discrimination based on sexual orientation went with higher odds of a past-year disorder "only in combination" with other forms. — [Bostwick et al. 2014, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4144327/)
- **[NEW] Bränström & Pachankis 2021, Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology 56(9):1537–1545.** Grade: moderate. Self-selected, cross-sectional, single-item life satisfaction; Europe only.
  - **Data:** the 2012 EU LGBT survey, 28 European countries, 6,771 transgender respondents.
  - **Life satisfaction:** higher country-level structural stigma, meaning laws, policies and public attitudes, went with lower life satisfaction (β = −0.188).
  - **Concealment:** higher stigma went with more concealment of gender identity (β = 0.347).
  - **Mediation:** the effect ran through concealment (indirect β = −0.336). Once concealment was accounted for, the direct effect was no longer significant.
  - **Concealment cuts both ways:** it was partly protective against day-to-day discrimination, yet overall linked to lower life satisfaction.
  - [Bränström & Pachankis 2021, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429389)
- **[LIB:ilo2018care] International Labour Organization 2018.** Grade: moderate. Worldwide, women do about 76% of unpaid care work, more than three times as much as men. — [ILO 2018](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-calls-urgent-action-prevent-looming-global-care-crisis-0)
- **[LIB:pew2023marriages] Pew Research Center 2023.** Grade: moderate. Even in US marriages where both earn about the same, wives do about 4.6 hours of housework a week versus about 2 for husbands. WEIRD flag: US only. — [Pew 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2023/04/13/in-a-growing-share-of-u-s-marriages-husbands-and-wives-earn-about-the-same/)
- **[LIB:daminger2019] Daminger 2019.** Grade: early. In interviews with 35 US couples, the mental work of noticing and anticipating household needs fell more on women. WEIRD flag: US only. — [Daminger 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0003122419859007)
- **[LIB:bleidorn2016] Bleidorn et al. 2016.** Grade: moderate. In about 985,000 people in 48 countries, men on average reported higher self-esteem than women. The gap was larger in rich Western countries and small in many Asian ones. — [Bleidorn et al. 2016, APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2016/01/self-esteem-gender)
- **[LIB:laumann2006] Laumann et al. 2006.** Grade: moderate. Across 29 countries among adults aged 40–80, countries clustered into more and less gender-equal patterns of sexual well-being. — [Laumann et al. 2006, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10508-005-9005-3)
- **[LIB:glass2016]** Family policy removes the parenthood happiness gap; see section 1.
- **[NEW] Girme et al. 2023, citing Day et al. 2011.** Grade: early as reported. Defensive endorsement of relationship-commitment ideology was more common in more gender-equal cultures (N = 33,018). — [Girme et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475216)

### Inferences
- **Freedom:** The evidence supports a neutral framing: rigid adherence to particular norms, especially "I must handle everything myself", narrows the help people allow themselves. This is a pattern any person of any gender can recognise in themselves. It does not require a verdict on masculinity or femininity.
- **Faith in people:** The "self-reliance" norm links directly to faith in people. Wong's finding of a moderate link with less help-seeking suggests small, low-stakes practice in accepting help. This is an inference; Wong et al. did not test an intervention.
- **Security:** For gender minorities, the stigma data show that concealment can be a rational safety choice in hostile settings, even though it costs well-being. Self-knowledge about gender identity must therefore never be framed as "you should be open".
- **Success and contentment:** Unequal unpaid work and mental load are a structural source of pressure. Insights about "always being the one who notices" should describe the pattern, not assign blame.

### Gaps
- **Country-level gender equality and well-being:** no new source was opened, for example studies of whether more gender-equal countries have higher life satisfaction for women and men. Budget limit. The library sources above give only indirect evidence.
- **The pressure of gender expectations on women:** beyond the care-work data, no review was opened, for example on appearance or caregiving norms and women's well-being.
- **Masculinity norms outside the US:** Wong et al.'s sample countries are unknown, so it is unclear how the findings generalise to Asia, Africa or Latin America.

## 4. Body and appearance: body image, body appreciation, social media, cultures, ageing, functionality interventions

### Takeaway
Body appreciation means valuing and respecting one's body regardless of its appearance. Across 240 papers it goes with:
- higher self-esteem, self-compassion and sexual satisfaction;
- less depression, anxiety and disordered eating.

These links hold even after accounting for negative body image. Experiments show that appearance-ideal images on social media worsen body image in the short term by a moderate amount, though long-term links with overall use are very small. Shifting attention from how the body looks to what it does has improved body appreciation in several randomised trials, including a single 15-minute session with mothers.

### Cited Findings
- **[NEW] Linardon, McClure, Tylka & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz 2022, Body Image 42:287–296.** Grade: strong for the cross-sectional links; early for causal direction (only 8 of 240 papers were prospective). — [Linardon et al. 2022, UKY bibliography](https://europe.as.uky.edu/bibcite/reference/29503)
  - **Scope:** a systematic review and meta-analysis of 240 papers, with random-effects meta-analyses of 35 cross-sectional correlates.
  - **Negative links:** body appreciation was inversely associated with eating pathology, dietary restraint, internalising appearance ideals, body surveillance, perceived sociocultural pressure, depression and anxiety.
  - **Positive links:** it was positively associated with self-esteem, self-compassion and sexual satisfaction.
  - **Independent of negative body image:** the associations remained after controlling for negative body image.
  - **Over time:** the qualitative synthesis of prospective studies suggests body appreciation may promote well-being over time.
  - **Missing:** pooled effect sizes and culture moderators were not available on the page I opened.
- **[NEW] de Valle, Gallego-García, Williamson & Wade 2021, Body Image.** Grade: strong for short-term experimental effects; the longitudinal effect is very small. — [de Valle et al. 2021, Universidad Loyola](https://www.uloyola.es/en/research-transfer/publications/social-media-body-image-and-the-question-of-causation-meta-analyses-of-experimental-and-longitudinal-evidence-article)
  - **Ideal images versus non-appearance content:** four meta-analyses. Viewing appearance-ideal social-media images, compared with non-appearance content, had a moderate negative effect on body image (24 experimental samples, n = 3,816, Hedges' g = −0.61). The effect was smaller but still significant without outliers.
  - **Ideal images versus other appearance images:** appearance-ideal images were moderately more harmful (14 samples, n = 2,641, g = −0.68, p = .05).
  - **Context:** comments and captions in "higher-risk" contexts added a small extra harm (21 samples, n = 3,482, g = −0.12).
  - **Over time:** social-media use had a very small negative longitudinal association with body image (10 samples, n = 5,177, Fisher's Z = −0.08).
  - **Moderators:** "No significant moderators emerged."
  - **Practical implication (authors):** manage exposure to appearance-ideal imagery.
- **[LIB:saiphoo2020] Saiphoo et al. 2020.** Grade: strong. Across 121 studies, more social-media use went with slightly lower self-esteem. — [Saiphoo et al. 2020 summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/social-instincts/201911/new-analysis-social-media-use-is-harmful-self-esteem)
- **[NEW] Alleva, Martijn, van Breukelen, Jansen & Karos 2015, Body Image 15:81–89.** Grade: moderate. WEIRD flag: young women, presumably Dutch; the country is not stated in the abstract. — [Alleva et al. 2015, Maastricht University](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/expand-your-horizon-a-programme-that-improves-body-image-and-redu)
  - **Design:** an RCT of "Expand Your Horizon", structured writing about what the body can do, with 81 women with negative body image (mean age 22.8) against an active control.
  - **Results:** appearance satisfaction, satisfaction with functionality and body appreciation all rose, and self-objectification fell. Effects were small to medium.
  - **Follow-up:** one week only.
- **[NEW] Granfield, Kemps, Yager, Alleva & Prichard 2023, Body Image 47:101648.** Grade: moderate for the immediate effect; early for durability (no follow-up). — [Granfield et al. 2023, Victoria University PDF](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/48199/1/1-s2.0-S1740144523001912-main.pdf)
  - **Design:** an RCT with 143 Australian mothers of children aged 0–10. A single 15-minute online writing session about their body's functionality and why it mattered, against a control group writing about the functions of household objects.
  - **Body appreciation** (0–100) rose from 50.5 to 70.9 in the intervention group versus 47.7 to 55.3 in controls (interaction ηp² = .12; within-group d = 1.08 vs 0.46).
  - **Functionality appreciation** rose from 67.9 to 80.3 versus 61.7 to 65.6 (ηp² = .07).
  - **Who benefited most:** mothers low in self-compassion gained most in functionality appreciation.
  - **Prior evidence, as the paper reports it:** it calls Expand Your Horizon "the current most effective positive body image intervention for women" (citing Guest et al. 2019). The three-session format has been tested in three RCTs, including women with rheumatoid arthritis.
- **[LIB:gilovich2000] Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky 2000.** Grade: moderate. People overestimate how much others notice their appearance. In the study, an embarrassing T-shirt was noticed by about 23% of people versus a guessed 46%. — [Gilovich et al. 2000, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707330/)
- **[LIB:ferrari2019] Ferrari et al. 2019.** Grade: strong. Across 27 trials, self-compassion training reduced self-criticism and low mood by moderate to large amounts. This is relevant because self-compassion moderated the functionality effect in Granfield et al. 2023. — [Ferrari et al. 2019](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/87545/self-compassion-interventions-and-psychosocial-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-of-rcts)

### Inferences
- **Freedom and contentment:** Body appreciation is not the same as liking how you look. It is respect for, and attention to, what the body does and feels. Because its benefits hold beyond the absence of negative body image, an app can aim to grow appreciation rather than to "fix" dissatisfaction.
- **Hope:** Functionality-focused writing is one of the few body-image approaches with RCT support that is also short, private, non-comparative and culture-neutral. It is closely suited to a small evening step, and it does not require changing the body.
- **Body changes tied to family roles:** pregnancy, breastfeeding and postpartum changes link the body identity to the parent identity. Granfield et al. show the approach works for mothers.
- **Social media:** The honest claim is narrow. Idealised appearance images tend to make people feel worse about their bodies in the moment. Overall time on social media has only a very small long-term link. Do not overclaim "social media ruins body image".

### Gaps
- **Appearance pressure across cultures:** no source was opened, for example cross-national surveys of body ideals and body appreciation across many nations. I cannot report non-Western prevalence or different ideals; budget limit. Linardon's culture moderators were not visible.
- **Ageing bodies:** no source was opened on body image across adulthood or in older age. This is a gap.
- **Tylka & Wood-Barcalow 2015:** their paper defining positive body image was not opened. The concept is covered via Linardon et al., with Tylka as a co-author.
- **Men and gender-diverse people:** the evidence on functionality interventions for men and gender-diverse people was not seen; both trials opened were with women.
- **Durability:** long-term follow-up of functionality interventions is lacking in the sources opened.

## 5. Sexuality: sexual well-being, sexual self-esteem, minority stress and resilience

### Takeaway
How people feel about themselves and how satisfied they are with their sexual lives rise and fall together over years, in singles as much as in partnered people, and more so for women and older adults. For sexual and gender minorities, prejudice and stigma create extra stress that is linked to higher rates of mental disorder (Meyer's minority-stress model). Country-level stigma pushes people to conceal their identity, and concealment carries much of stigma's cost to life satisfaction, although it can also protect against discrimination.

### Cited Findings

**Sexual well-being and self-esteem**

- **[NEW] Weber, Hopwood, Denissen & Bleidorn 2024, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.** Grade: moderate to strong: large, nationally representative, longitudinal and within-person. WEIRD flag: one country. — [University of Zurich news release on Weber et al. 2024](https://news.uzh.ch/en/articles/media/2024/Self-esteem.html)
  - **Design:** more than 11,000 adults in Germany, 12 years of data.
  - **Both directions:** higher self-esteem went with more frequent sexual activity and greater sexual satisfaction. Changes in sexual satisfaction preceded changes in self-esteem, and the reverse also held.
  - **Who:** the link was stronger for older people and for women. It was as strong for singles as for people in relationships.
  - **Explanation (authors):** self-esteem acts as a "social barometer", and higher self-esteem may help people voice their desires.
  - **Missing:** effect sizes were not given in the release.
- **[NEW] Girme et al. 2023, citing Park & MacDonald 2022.** Grade: moderate. Sexually satisfied singles reported higher life satisfaction (N = 1,238). — [Girme et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475216)
- **[NEW] Linardon et al. 2022.** Body appreciation was positively associated with sexual satisfaction (see section 4). — [Linardon et al. 2022](https://europe.as.uky.edu/bibcite/reference/29503)
- **[LIB:mallory2019] Mallory et al. 2019.** Grade: strong. Across 48 studies (12,145 people), more open communication about sex went with better sexual well-being. — [Mallory et al. 2019 summary](https://www.psypost.org/couples-who-communicate-more-about-sex-tend-to-have-better-sex-study-finds)
- **[LIB:muise2016] Muise et al. 2016.** Grade: moderate. Well-being rose with sexual frequency only up to about once a week. WEIRD flag: US. — [Muise et al. 2016](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/654391)
- **[LIB:laumann2006]** In 29 countries, the predictors of sexual well-being were much the same everywhere, but levels clustered by gender-equality regime. — [Laumann et al. 2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10508-005-9005-3)

**Minority stress and resilience**

- **[NEW] Meyer 2003, Psychological Bulletin.** Grade: strong as a foundational, widely used framework. I opened the abstract via Health and Human Rights Info; the PMC full text was blocked by a captcha, so the specific numbers and the named processes were not verified this session. — [Meyer 2003 abstract, HHRI](https://www.hhri.org/?p=35900); full text at [PMC2072932](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2072932/)
  - **Prevalence:** the review "shows, using meta-analyses, that LGBs have a higher prevalence of mental disorders than heterosexuals".
  - **Cause:** it proposes that "stigma, prejudice, and discrimination create a hostile and stressful social environment that causes mental health problems".
- **[NEW] Bostwick et al. 2014.** Grade: early to moderate. WEIRD flag: US. — [Bostwick et al. 2014, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4144327/)
  - **Design:** a US national survey subsample of 577 lesbian, gay and bisexual adults.
  - **Combined discrimination:** discrimination on several grounds together (orientation, race, gender) went with higher odds of past-year mood or anxiety disorder, while orientation discrimination alone did not.
  - **Bisexual respondents** reported less discrimination yet worse mental health. The authors suggest expectations of rejection, internalised stigma and exclusion from LGBT communities may matter more.
  - **Coping:** social support and identity valence and prominence as buffers are offered as "highly provisional" ideas, not measured.
- **[NEW] Pachankis et al. 2016, Archives of Sexual Behavior 46(5):1491–1502.** Grade: moderate; the authors call the effects small. — [Pachankis et al. 2016, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5346459)
  - **Data:** 174,209 men who have sex with men in 38 European countries (European MSM Internet Survey, EMIS 2010).
  - **Identity labels:** per 1 SD higher structural stigma, men were more likely to identify as bisexual or heterosexual (adjusted odds ratio 1.35).
  - **Concordance:** attraction, behaviour and identity were less likely to line up (adjusted odds ratio 0.87).
  - **Where strongest:** in small towns in high-stigma countries.
  - **Earlier work, as cited:** men who have sex with men in countries with homophobic laws were more likely to conceal their orientation completely.
- **[NEW] Bränström & Pachankis 2021.** In 28 European countries, stigma lowered transgender people's life satisfaction largely through concealment, while concealment partly shielded them from discrimination (see section 3). — [Bränström & Pachankis 2021, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429389)

### Inferences
- **Hope:** The two-way link between sexual satisfaction and self-esteem means that small improvements in either can feed the other. It holds for singles too, so sexual well-being is not only a couple's topic.
- **Security and freedom:** For sexual and gender minorities, how open a person can safely be depends heavily on their country, town and family. The data show concealment both costs well-being and protects. An app used worldwide must treat openness as the person's own judgement about safety, never as a measure of authenticity or growth.
- **Self-knowledge in the stigma data:** in high-stigma places, identity labels diverge from attraction and behaviour (Pachankis 2016). The labels people use are shaped by safety, so the app should never infer orientation from behaviour or answers.
- **Belonging:** Meyer's framework places much of the stress in the social environment, not the person. Insights should reflect this: "this is heavy because of how others treat it", not "you are struggling".

### Gaps
- **Recent minority-stress meta-analyses** were not opened, for example on concealment and mental health, or on resilience and community connectedness. I could not verify the effect sizes of Meyer's processes (expectations of rejection, concealment, internalised stigma) or of coping and resilience in this session: PMC captcha and budget limit.
- **Sexual minorities outside Europe and the US:** no evidence opened.
- **Sexual self-esteem:** no dedicated meta-analysis or measure was opened. Weber et al. use global self-esteem.
- **Asexual people:** no evidence opened on asexuality, or on people for whom sexual identity is not central. The app must not assume sex is part of everyone's well-being.

## 6. What this means for the app

### Takeaway
Five things follow from the evidence:
- **Situations should show meaning versus pressure.** Everyday situations reveal how someone holds these identities when the answer options show whether a role is lived as meaning (chosen, valued, fulfilling) or as pressure (imposed, judged, concealed).
- **Insights can say four things honestly:**
  - roles can be meaningful and tiring at once;
  - support and friendships matter more than partner status;
  - appreciating what the body does is learnable;
  - self-feeling and sexual satisfaction feed each other.
- **Small steps with trial support:**
  - functionality writing (Expand Your Horizon);
  - self-compassion;
  - talking openly about sex with a partner (supported by a meta-analysis);
  - accepting help.

  Asking for one specific kind of help, to build a sense of control (mastery), is an inference.
- **What to avoid:** gendered framing, default family or partner models, pressure to come out, and any comparison or appearance metrics.

### Cited Findings (evidence behind each design rule)
- **Treat roles as meaningful and tiring at the same time.**
  - Parents report higher meaning but lower moment-to-moment pleasure during childcare, alongside more positive affect than during the rest of their day. — [Nelson et al.](http://sonjalyubomirsky.com/files/2012/09/Nelson-Kushlev-Lyubomirsky-in-press1.pdf)
  - Helping family goes with happiness through role fulfilment. — [Telzer & Fuligni 2009](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/daily-family-assistance-and-the-psychological-well-being-of-adole/)
- **Strain is often structural, so say so.** — [glass2016](https://contemporaryfamilies.utah.edu/publications/posts/2016/june/brief-parenting-happiness.php); [ilo2018care](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-calls-urgent-action-prevent-looming-global-care-crisis-0); [Meyer 2003](https://www.hhri.org/?p=35900)
- **Mastery protects caregivers.** For caregivers, a sense of control (mastery) was protective and burden carried the weight of filial duty. Behaviour problems in the person cared for are the heaviest stressor. — [Khalaila & Litwin 2011](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/does-filial-piety-decrease-depression-among-family-caregivers/); [Pinquart meta-analysis](https://psychsoc.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/58/2/P112)
- **Single people thrive on friendship and low fear.** Partner status is not destiny; family pressure weighs more in interdependent cultures. — [Girme et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475216)
- **Self-reliance limits help-seeking.** The self-reliance norm is the help-seeking risk to name, without gendering it. — [Wong et al. 2017](https://culturereframed.org/library-article/meta-analyses-of-the-relationship-between-conformity-to-masculine-norms-and-mental-health-related-outcomes/)
- **Functionality writing is a step tested in randomised trials.** — [Alleva et al. 2015](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/expand-your-horizon-a-programme-that-improves-body-image-and-redu); [Granfield et al. 2023](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/48199/1/1-s2.0-S1740144523001912-main.pdf)
- **Appearance-ideal images worsen body image in the short term.** — [de Valle et al. 2021](https://www.uloyola.es/en/research-transfer/publications/social-media-body-image-and-the-question-of-causation-meta-analyses-of-experimental-and-longitudinal-evidence-article)
- **Self-esteem and sexual satisfaction feed each other.** — [Weber et al. 2024](https://news.uzh.ch/en/articles/media/2024/Self-esteem.html). Open sexual communication helps. — [mallory2019](https://www.psypost.org/couples-who-communicate-more-about-sex-tend-to-have-better-sex-study-finds)
- **Concealment is shaped by stigma and can be protective.** Never push openness. — [Bränström & Pachankis 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429389); [Pachankis et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5346459)
- **Extended-family living is the global norm.** Do not assume a nuclear family. — [pew2019living](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/12/13/key-findings-how-living-arrangements-vary-by-religious-affiliation-around-the-world/)
- **People overestimate how much others notice their looks.** — [gilovich2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707330/)

### Inferences (design proposals; the situations and wording are mine, built on the findings above)

**A. Everyday situations that reveal how someone holds these identities.** These are concrete choices, never scales.

*Family roles:*
- **A family request.** "A relative asks you to help this weekend, and you had other plans. You…"
  - Options: go and feel glad to be useful / go and feel quietly resentful / offer a smaller piece of help / say no and feel guilty / say no and feel fine / pass it to someone else in the family.
  - Reveals: whether duty is lived as meaning, as pressure, or as negotiated.
- **Caregiving moment.** "The person you care for asks the same thing again, or gets upset. You…"
  - Options: answer patiently / step out for a minute / feel your patience run thin / call someone for help.
  - Use only if the person has said they are a caregiver.
- **Parent moment.** "The children are finally asleep. The next hour, you…"
  - Options: tidy and plan tomorrow / sit with your partner / do something only for you / scroll / fall asleep.
  - Reveals: whether the self beyond the parent role gets any room.
- **Being someone's son or daughter.** "Your parent gives advice you didn't ask for about your life choices. You…"
  - Options: thank them and do it your way / argue / go quiet / change your plans to please them.

*Partner identity:*
- **Free Saturday.** "An unplanned Saturday. You end up…"
  - Options: with friends / with family / on your own and enjoying it / on your own and restless / with your partner / doing your partner's plans.
  - Ask everyone; do not assume a partner.
- **Family question about being single.** "At a family meal, someone asks when you'll settle down. You…"
  - Options: laugh it off / explain you're happy as you are / feel a sting / change the subject.
- **Partner's own interests.** "Your partner spends the evening on their own interest. You…"
  - Options: join / do your own thing / wait for them / feel left out.
  - Reveals: closeness versus loss of self.

*Gender, without naming gender:*
- **Hard week.** "A friend asks how you really are after a hard week. You…"
  - Options: say "fine" / share a little / share fully / turn it to them.
  - Reveals: self-reliance.
- **Household noticing.** "You notice the household is about to run out of something. You…"
  - Options: handle it yourself / tell someone else / leave it.
  - Reveals: mental load.

*Body:*
- **Getting dressed.** "Getting dressed this morning, you…"
  - Options: pick what's comfortable / change several times / avoid the mirror / pick something you enjoy wearing.
- **After a photo.** "After seeing a photo of yourself, you…"
  - Options: move on / look again at what you dislike / delete it / notice the moment it captured.
- **What your body did today.** "Today your body helped you…"
  - Options: carry / move / make / care for someone / rest.
  - This prepares the functionality step.

*Sexuality, 18+ only, private, never graphic:*
- **Thinking about intimate life.** "When you think about your intimate life this week, the closest word is…"
  - Options: content / missing something / not a priority right now / complicated / I'd rather not say.
- **Saying what you'd like.** "If you wanted something different with a partner, you would most likely…"
  - Options: say it / hint / wait for them to notice / keep it to yourself / not relevant to me.

**B. What an insight could honestly say.** Each insight is phrased about the person's own pattern, with hedged language and no comparison with others.
- "You often said yes to family even when it cost you. Research with teenagers and adult caregivers suggests helping family often feels meaningful. It tends to weigh more when there's little sense of control. Both can be true for you."
- "In your answers, a parent's day had many tiring moments and some that mattered a lot. Studies of parents find exactly this mix: lower moment-to-moment pleasure, higher meaning."
- "Your free time this week centred on friends. Among single people, strong friendships go with feeling satisfied with single life."
  - Do not say "single people are as happy as couples". The averages differ and vary by country.
- "You usually kept hard weeks to yourself. Holding tightly to 'I must handle it alone' tends to go with less help-seeking. A small ask is one way to see how it feels."
- "You picked what your body *did* more often than how it looked. Valuing what your body does goes with feeling better about yourself in many studies."
- "How you feel about yourself and how you feel about intimacy tend to move together over the years, for single and partnered people alike."

**C. Small steps the evidence supports.**
- **Functionality writing** (Expand Your Horizon style). "Write three things your body let you do today and why they matter to you."
  - Grade: moderate (several RCTs; immediate effects; durability unclear).
- **Self-compassion phrasing.** "Write to yourself about today's slip as you would to a good friend."
  - Grade: strong (ferrari2019; breines2012 in library).
- **One small ask for help.**
  - Grade: inference from Wong et al. and Khalaila & Litwin. Untested as an intervention, so the app should not claim effects.
- **Name the one part of a family duty you'd choose anyway.**
  - Grade: inference from the role-fulfilment finding (Telzer & Fuligni). Untested.
- **Unfollow or mute one account that leaves you comparing your looks**, for those who report this pattern.
  - Grade: an inference grounded in the short-term experimental harm of appearance-ideal images (de Valle). There is no trial of this exact step in the sources opened.
- **Tell a partner one thing you enjoy** (18+, partnered users only).
  - Grade: strong for the correlation (mallory2019); the causal direction is uncertain.
- **Plan one thing with a friend this week**, for people who are not partnered.
  - Grade: correlational (Girme et al. review).

**D. What to avoid.**
- **Stereotyping by gender.**
  - Never ask "as a man / as a woman" questions. Never tailor insights by inferred gender.
  - Describe norms such as self-reliance as personal patterns anyone can hold.
  - Wong et al. themselves recommend looking at specific norms, not "masculinity" as a whole.
- **Assuming one family model.**
  - Extended-family living is the global norm (38%), so the app should not assume a nuclear family.
  - Do not assume a partner, children, heterosexuality, marriage, or that parents are alive or close. Estrangement is common in the library data.
  - Treat childless people as distinct groups, chosen or not chosen; never treat parenthood as the default life.
- **Moralising duty or independence.**
  - In interdependent cultures, family approval carries more weight for well-being (Girme et al.). An insight that says "set boundaries with your family" can be culturally tone-deaf.
  - Frame both duty and autonomy as legitimate, and show the person their own mix.
- **Pushing openness about sexual or gender identity.**
  - Concealment is partly protective in high-stigma places.
  - Never infer orientation or gender identity from answers. Store nothing that could out someone.
  - Offer "I'd rather not say" on every intimate question.
- **Appearance metrics.**
  - No weight, size, BMI or appearance ratings, no before/after framing and no "goal body".
  - Body questions should focus on function, comfort and care.
- **Clinical language.**
  - Minority stress, caregiver burden and low mood are research constructs. Insights must not diagnose. Where answers suggest heavy burden or distress, the app can gently point to outside support without labelling.
- **Overclaiming.**
  - Do not say "parents are less happy", "social media ruins body image" or "being single makes you unhappy". Each is contested or averaged over very different groups.

**E. Mapping to the core cravings.**
- **Freedom:** a role that is chosen versus imposed; the self-reliance norm; concealment.
- **Security:** attachment anxiety; fear of being single; the safety calculus around stigma.
- **Hope:** functionality appreciation can rise after one session; self-esteem and sexual satisfaction rise together; satisfaction with singlehood rises after about 40.
- **Success:** the "primacy of work" norm was not linked to poorer mental health.
- **Belonging:** connection through family assistance; friendships for single people; a community for minorities (theorised only).
- **Faith in people:** help-seeking; open sexual communication.
- **Contentment and happiness:** meaning versus moment-to-moment pleasure in parenting; life events affect mood and life judgements differently.

### Gaps
- **Delivery format:** none of the opened studies tested app-delivered, single-question formats except the 15-minute online functionality session (Granfield et al.). Effects of brief in-app prompts are unknown.
- **Cultural validation of the situations:** the everyday situations proposed above have not been tested anywhere, and some (for example family requests and "settling down" questions) will read differently across cultures. They need local review.
- **Effect sizes:** caregiving uplifts and coping, minority-stress resilience, attachment security in couples, country-level gender equality, cross-cultural body ideals and ageing bodies remain under-evidenced in these notes because of the search and fetch budget.
