## 1. Work identity and meaning: callings, job crafting, meaningful work, workaholism vs engagement, burnout, job loss, retirement

### Takeaway
Work gives people meaning, structure, status and belonging, so losing it does real harm. Unemployment roughly doubles the share of people with psychological problems (34% vs 16%), and the harm is causal and larger where safety nets are weak. Experiencing work as meaningful is strongly linked to engagement and life satisfaction, and people who actively reshape their jobs by adding resources and challenges tend to be more engaged. The trap is compulsive work (workaholism), which goes with stress, burnout and poor health but not with better performance. Another trap is a self that rests on work alone, which leaves people exposed when the job ends.

### Cited Findings
**Callings and how people relate to work**
- Workers split roughly evenly into seeing their work as a *job* (for the pay), a *career* (to move up) or a *calling* (meaningful in itself), even within the same occupation. **Moderate. WEIRD** (196 US employees). [LIB:wrzesniewski1997] — [Wrzesniewski et al., 1997](https://works.swarthmore.edu/fac-psychology/193)
- How much people are paid is only weakly linked to how satisfied they are with their job (r ≈ .15 across 92 samples). Pay is a weak basis for work satisfaction. **Strong.** [LIB:judge2010] — [Judge et al., 2010](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-relationship-between-pay-and-job-satisfaction-a-meta-analysis/)
- In about 12,000 daily diaries from 238 workers, the best workdays were the ones with progress on work that mattered, and setbacks hit about two to three times harder. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:amabile2011] — [Amabile & Kramer, 2011](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity)

**Meaningful work**
- Meta-analysis of 44 articles (N = 23,144). Meaningful work showed "large correlations (r = 0.70+)" with work engagement, commitment and job satisfaction. It showed moderate-to-large links (r = 0.44 to −0.49) with life satisfaction, life meaning, general health and (negatively) intentions to quit, and small-to-moderate links (r = −0.19 to 0.33) with self-rated performance, citizenship behaviour and negative affect. The best-fitting model ran from meaningful work → engagement, commitment and satisfaction → performance and staying. **Strong** as an association. The studies are self-report and correlational, and the abstract gives no country mix. [NEW:allan2019] — [Allan et al., 2019, J. Management Studies 56(3)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jomstd/v56y2019i3p500-528.html)

**Job crafting (reshaping your own job)**
- Meta-analysis of 122 samples, N = 35,670 workers (108 sources, 1,429 effect sizes). Overall job crafting correlated with work engagement ρ = .45, job satisfaction .29, self-rated performance .27, other-rated performance .18 and job strain −.13. It was unrelated to intentions to quit (−.02, not significant). **Strong** as an association. The authors stress the data are "correlates rather than causal". [NEW:rudolph2017] — [Rudolph et al., 2017, J. Vocational Behavior 102](https://www.mentorcoach.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Rudolph-Katz-Lavigne-Zacher-2017-job-crafting-meta-analysis-due-by-week-5.pdf)
- *Which kind* of crafting matters. **Increasing structural resources** (learning skills, gaining autonomy) had the strongest links (engagement .59, satisfaction .40, self-rated performance .40). **Increasing challenging demands** was linked to engagement .45 and other-rated performance .42. **Decreasing hindering demands** (cutting out what drains you) was the only form linked to *more* strain (.15) and more intention to quit (.24), and slightly *less* engagement (−.09). Proactive personality (.54) and self-efficacy (.40) were the strongest personal correlates. Age was slightly negative (−.10). **Strong** (correlational). [NEW:rudolph2017] — [Rudolph et al., 2017](https://www.mentorcoach.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Rudolph-Katz-Lavigne-Zacher-2017-job-crafting-meta-analysis-due-by-week-5.pdf)
- A second meta-analysis (Lichtenthaler & Fischbach, 2019) found the same split. Promotion-focused crafting (adding resources and challenges) was modestly related to *less* burnout, and prevention-focused crafting (cutting demands) was moderately related to *more* burnout. A 2022 meta-analysis (Boehnlein & Baum) tested societal culture as a moderator, but the summary gives no breakdown by culture. **Strong** (reported via a secondary umbrella summary that gives labels, not numbers). [NEW:qicwd2023crafting] — [QIC-WD Job Crafting umbrella summary, 2023](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Job%20Crafting%20081823.pdf)

**Workaholism, engagement and burnout**
- Workaholism means inner pressure to work, constant thoughts about work, bad feelings when not working, and working beyond what is required. In the Clark et al. (2016) meta-analysis it was strongly related to job stress and moderately related to burnout, poor physical and emotional health, work-life conflict and counterproductive work behaviour. It was modestly related to *lower* job satisfaction and **unrelated to job performance**, though moderately related to work enjoyment. Perfectionism and Type A (competitive, ambitious) traits were among the strongest personal correlates, and work overload and role conflict were the strongest work correlates. **Strong** (meta-analysis, reported via secondary summary without numbers). [NEW:qicwd2021workaholism] — [QIC-WD Workaholism umbrella summary of Clark et al. 2016, J. Management](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Workaholism%20101921.pdf)
- Worldwide, only 21% of employees felt engaged at work in 2024, and 40% felt a lot of stress the previous day. **Moderate** (global survey). [LIB:gallup2025] — [Gallup, 2025](https://gallup.com/file/workplace/659528/state-of-the-global-workplace-2025-download.pdf)
- The WHO classes burn-out as an *occupational phenomenon*, not a medical condition: exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about the job, and reduced effectiveness, arising from chronic workplace stress that has not been managed. **Moderate** (definition). [LIB:who2019burnout] — [WHO, 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases)
- Working 55 or more hours a week is linked to a 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from heart disease than 35–40 hours. **Strong** (WHO/ILO global estimate). [LIB:who2021hours] — [WHO & ILO, 2021](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo)
- Across 91 samples (N = 38,124), mentally switching off from work in free time went with less exhaustion and better sleep and life satisfaction. **Strong** (mostly cross-sectional). [LIB:wendsche2017] — [Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah, 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02072/full)

**Losing work: identity loss after job loss**
- Meta-analyses of 237 cross-sectional and 87 longitudinal studies: unemployed people were more distressed than employed people (average d = 0.51), with worse depression, anxiety, psychosomatic symptoms, well-being and **self-esteem**. About **34% of unemployed people had psychological problems vs 16% of employed people**. Longitudinal studies and natural experiments indicate unemployment *causes* distress. Selection (less healthy people losing jobs) exists but is weak. Programmes for people who stayed unemployed were moderately helpful (d = −0.35). **Strong.** [NEW:paulmoser2009] — [Paul & Moser, 2009, J. Vocational Behavior 74](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)
- **Who is hit hardest (worldwide angle):** men and blue-collar workers were more distressed by unemployment. The effect was **stronger in countries with weaker economic development, more unequal incomes, or weaker unemployment protection**. **Strong** (the page does not list the countries included). [NEW:paulmoser2009] — [Paul & Moser, 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)
- Meta-analysis of 104 studies (437 effect sizes): unemployed people had lower psychological *and* physical well-being. Among the unemployed, **work-role centrality** (how central work is to one's identity), coping resources (personal, social, financial and *time structure*), how people appraised the situation and how they coped were more strongly related to mental health than education/skills or demographics. **Strong.** The abstract does not state the direction of the centrality link. [NEW:mckeeryan2005] — [McKee-Ryan et al., 2005, J. Applied Psychology 90(1)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15641890/)
- **Conflict:** McKee-Ryan et al. found the size of unemployment benefits and the current unemployment rate did *not* moderate the harm — [McKee-Ryan et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15641890/). Paul & Moser found *weaker unemployment protection* made it worse — [Paul & Moser, 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304). The later and larger meta-analysis (Paul & Moser) included country-level indicators, which may explain the difference.
- The "incongruence" meta-analysis (Paul & Moser, 2006) found that unemployed people report slightly *higher* commitment to having a job than employed people (d = 0.11). A mismatch between how much someone wants to work and whether they have work was "significantly associated with psychological distress". The page lists r = .19 (mixed distress), .19 (depression), .14 (anxiety), .13 (well-being/life satisfaction) and .08 (self-esteem) for this link. Longitudinal parts rested on "small sample sizes". **Moderate.** [NEW:paulmoser2006] — [Paul & Moser, 2006, J. Occupational & Organizational Psychology 79](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117627884)
- Following over 24,000 Germans for 15 years, life satisfaction dropped after job loss and on average did not fully recover even after re-employment. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:lucas2004] — [Lucas et al., 2004](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/41053/)

**Retirement and identity**
- Systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 studies (2003–2023, PROSPERO-registered). 32% of studies found retirement went with *higher* life satisfaction, 47% with *lower*, and 21% found no link. The pooled effect was non-significant with high heterogeneity, so there was "no consistent impact". Measurement tools moderated results. Limitations: few longitudinal studies and possible publication bias. **Moderate** (small, heterogeneous meta-analysis; authors based in South Africa; countries not listed). [NEW:ejike2024retirement] — [Ejike, Kenni & Sunday, PLOS Global Public Health 4(4)](https://www.mentalhealthdataprizeafrica.aphrc.org/repository/paper/35314/paradox-of-life-after-work-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-on-retirement-anxiety-and-life-satisfaction)
- Among 170 Australians who had retired within the past year, group memberships held *before* retirement helped health and well-being only indirectly, by making it easier to **keep some groups and gain new ones** afterwards (social identity continuity and gain). **Early. WEIRD.** [NEW:simic2023retirement] — [Psychology and Aging 2023, 38(7)](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2023/journal-article-abstracts/pag0000757/)

### Inferences
- Work identity is double-edged. Meaningful work and active crafting are among the strongest correlates of engagement and life satisfaction, which feeds *success, hope and contentment*. A self built mainly on the work role, however, carries a measurable risk when the role disappears (job loss, retirement, informal work drying up), which threatens *security and belonging*. McKee-Ryan's finding that coping resources and appraisals matter more than demographics suggests the self-knowledge that helps most is about what *else* holds you up: people, routine, other roles.
- "Time structure" as a coping resource, together with the retirement finding on keeping and gaining groups, points to two practical anchors for any role transition: a daily rhythm and group ties.
- Workaholism and engagement can look alike from outside (long hours, enjoyment), but workaholism adds compulsion and guilt and buys no extra performance. A reflection app can help people tell "I want to" from "I can't stop".
- The job-crafting data suggest a counter-intuitive insight: trying to cope with a draining job mainly by cutting things out goes with *more* strain, while adding a resource (learning, support, autonomy) or a challenge goes with more engagement. This is correlational, so the direction could also run the other way.
- **Worldwide:** the unemployment harm is largest where safety nets are weakest, which is the case in many low- and middle-income countries. There, a work identity is also an income identity, and the *security* craving dominates.
- **Craving links:** meaningful work → contentment and happiness; crafting → freedom (autonomy) and hope; job loss → security, belonging and self-worth; workaholism → a counterfeit of success that costs contentment.

### Gaps
- No numerical meta-analytic effect sizes were opened for workaholism vs work engagement (the umbrella summary did not address engagement). The well-known distinction (both involve high effort, but only workaholism involves compulsion and ill-health) is not sourced here.
- Job-crafting *intervention* trials (rather than correlations) were not opened, so causal claims about crafting are unsupported here.
- No meta-analysis of callings' "dark side" (e.g., exploitation of people with a sense of calling) was opened.
- No source opened on re-employment restoring self-esteem, or on identity loss after job loss specifically in low- and middle-income countries or in informal work.
- Burnout prevalence across countries was not researched (the library has only the WHO definition).
- The retirement meta-analysis page did not report total N, countries, or moderators such as voluntary vs forced retirement.

## 2. Success: definitions, extrinsic vs intrinsic goals, impostor feelings, fear of failure, comparison at work

### Takeaway
The best evidence says that wanting money, image or fame is not harmful in itself. The cost comes when these goals take priority over growth, relationships and community: relative priority on extrinsic goals goes with lower well-being (r ≈ −.22), and this holds across gender, age and world regions. Impostor feelings, fear of failure and upward comparison are common and corrode the felt sense of success. Many people's deepest regrets are about not becoming who they hoped to be, not about failing to get ahead.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of 92 reports, 105 studies, 1,808 effect sizes, **N = 70,110** across eight world regions (Africa, Asia, Eastern Europe, Middle East, North America, Oceania, South America, Western Europe). Valuing **intrinsic aspirations** (growth, relationships, community, health) correlated with well-being at r = .24 [.22, .27] and with ill-being at r = −.11. Growth was the strongest (r = .25). **Strong.** [NEW:bradshaw2022aspirations] — [Bradshaw et al., JPSP (preprint)](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2022_BradshawEtAl_MetaAspriations_JPSP.pdf)
- Valuing **extrinsic aspirations** on its own (simple scores) was close to zero with well-being (r = .07; wealth alone r = .03, not significant) and slightly positive with ill-being (r = .07). When extrinsic goals were measured **relative to** intrinsic ones (i.e., *prioritised* over them), the link with well-being was r = −.22 [−.32, −.11] and with ill-being r = .23. Within that, image −.24, fame −.19 and wealth −.16. The authors: "Being engaged with goals is better than not being engaged with goals, regardless of goal type", but prioritising extrinsic over intrinsic goals carries a wellness cost. **Strong** for the intrinsic link. **Moderate** for the relative-priority harm, which rests on only 14 studies (N = 2,216) drawn from Asia, Eastern Europe and North America. [NEW:bradshaw2022aspirations] — [Bradshaw et al.](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2022_BradshawEtAl_MetaAspriations_JPSP.pdf)
- Robustness: gender, age and region did not moderate the main models. The only regional exception was that simple extrinsic scores were positively linked to well-being in Eastern Europe (r = .15). Socioeconomic status was under-reported. There was possible publication bias in one model, and heterogeneity was large. **Strong/moderate.** [NEW:bradshaw2022aspirations] — [Bradshaw et al.](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2022_BradshawEtAl_MetaAspriations_JPSP.pdf)
- The original finding: people whose biggest life goals were money, looks and fame had less energy and more aches and pains. **Moderate. WEIRD**, correlational. [LIB:kasser1996] — [Kasser & Ryan, 1996](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167296223006)
- Feeling like a fraud who will be "found out" despite real success was reported by 9% to 82% of people across 62 studies, depending on the measure. Both women and men report it, and no tested treatments were found. **Moderate.** [LIB:bravata2020] — [Bravata et al., 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs11606-019-05364-1)
- Using luxury goods can make people feel like impostors (an unearned privilege) and act less confidently. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:goor2020] — [Goor et al., 2020](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/jcr/ucz044)
- Across 51 economies in 2024, nearly half of adults who saw a good business opportunity said fear of failure would stop them, up from 42% in 2019. **Moderate** (global survey). [LIB:gem2025] — [GEM, 2025](https://www.gemconsortium.org/news/%C2%A0entrepreneurship-fear-of-failure-on-the-rise%2C-according-to-global-entrepreneurship-monitor-report)
- Among US college students (and comparable UK and Canadian samples, N = 41,641), the feeling that others expect perfection from you rose 33% between 1989 and 2016. **Strong. WEIRD.** [LIB:curran2019] — [Curran & Hill, 2019](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2018/01/perfectionism-young-people)
- **Comparison at work:** when University of California staff learned colleagues' pay, those paid below the median felt less satisfied and looked for other jobs, and those above felt no happier. **Moderate. WEIRD** (one employer). [LIB:card2012] — [Card et al., 2012](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16396). Life satisfaction tracked one's income *rank* more than its amount, and upward comparisons counted more. **Moderate. WEIRD** (UK). [LIB:boyce2010] — [Boyce et al., 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797610362671)
- "Benign envy", wanting what another has without wishing them harm, motivated effort and better performance, but only when people believed they could improve. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:vandeven2011] — [van de Ven et al., 2011](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167211400421)
- Asked about their biggest regret, 76% of people named failing to become the person they hoped to be (their "ideal self"), and these regrets lasted longer. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:davidai2018] — [Davidai & Gilovich, 2018](https://news.cornell.edu/node/300686)
- Treating a failure with kindness rather than harsh self-criticism led students to study longer for a retest — [LIB:breines2012, Breines & Chen, 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167212445599) (**moderate, WEIRD**). Across 27 trials, self-compassion training reduced self-criticism, rumination, stress and low mood by moderate-to-large amounts — [LIB:ferrari2019, Ferrari et al., 2019](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/87545/self-compassion-interventions-and-psychosocial-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-of-rcts) (**strong**).

### Inferences
- The most honest message on success is "*what you put first*" rather than "money is bad". Ambition for income or status is compatible with well-being when it is not at the top of the list, and the cost appears when it displaces growth and relationships. This frames *success* in a way that also serves *contentment*.
- Because rank and comparison drive satisfaction more than amounts, a self-reflection tool should avoid adding comparison (consistent with the app's rule). Instead it can help people notice when *they* are comparing, and whether the comparison works as benign envy (motivation plus belief they can improve) or as a drain.
- Impostor feelings are common enough that normalising them is honest. There is no evidence-based treatment, so the app should not promise to fix them.
- **Craving links:** intrinsic goal priority → contentment and happiness; extrinsic priority → a fragile, comparison-driven sense of success; fear of failure → blocks freedom and hope.

### Gaps
- **How people define success across cultures and over a lifetime:** no cross-cultural survey or study on lay definitions of success was opened (search budget exhausted). This is a real gap. The Bradshaw meta-analysis covers aspirations, not definitions.
- Impostor feelings outside Western professional or student samples were not examined.
- No study opened on fear of failure as a *trait* and its link with self-worth (only GEM prevalence).

## 3. Money and self-worth: financial contingencies of self-worth, status, debt and shame, money talk, generosity

### Takeaway
Some people stake their self-worth on financial success. That contingency is **unrelated to how much they earn** but goes with more financial stress, anxiety, comparison, less sense of autonomy and, in a follow-up line of work, less time with loved ones and more loneliness. It is a direct threat to the craving for *economic freedom*, felt inside the person regardless of income. Debt is strongly linked to mental-health problems, in both directions. People tend to look away from their finances when things are bad. Status and generosity findings are partly contested.

### Cited Findings
**Financially contingent self-worth (FCSW)**
- FCSW is "an external, conditional source of self-esteem" in which worth depends on financial success. In an adult US online sample (N = 370, ages 18–76), FCSW was **unrelated to income (r = .02)**, self-rated financial status and gender. It was **lower in older people (r = −.24)** and slightly higher with economic hardship (r = .14). It predicted more perceived financial stress (β = .20 after controls), anxiety symptoms (β = .18), financial social comparisons (β = .24) and financial hassles (β = .22). **Moderate.** WEIRD (US students plus one online sample), single lab, partly correlational. [NEW:park2017fcsw] — [Park, Ward & Naragon-Gainey, 2017, PSPB](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/2017_Park_etal_PSPB.pdf)
- Across studies, high-FCSW people reported **less autonomy**. After a financial threat they showed more behavioural disengagement (β = .40). A brief **self-affirmation** (reflecting on other important values) **eliminated** the autonomy drop after financial threat for high-FCSW people. FCSW correlated r = .60 with materialism, but effects held after controlling for materialism, financial status and goals. **Moderate/early** for the affirmation finding (one study, N = 328 undergraduates). [NEW:park2017fcsw] — [Park et al., 2017](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/2017_Park_etal_PSPB.pdf)
- Five follow-up studies with more than 2,500 participants (including a two-week daily diary) examined social costs. People who base self-worth on finances feel pressure and lack autonomy, pursuing financial goals takes time away from loved ones, and less time with close others is linked to loneliness and disconnection. **Moderate** (only the press release was opened, and it gives no numbers). [NEW:ward2020fcsw] — [University at Buffalo, 2020](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2020/04/014.html)

**Debt, shame and avoidance**
- Meta-analysis (nearly 34,000 participants): people in debt were **more than three times as likely** to have a mental-health problem. Under 9% of people without mental-health problems were in debt, versus more than a quarter of those with them. Debt was also linked to depression, drug dependence and psychosis, and people who die by suicide are more likely to be in debt. The lead author says it is "hard to say which causes which", and the link probably runs both ways. **Strong** as an association, causal direction unclear. Countries were not stated on the page, so it is probably mainly high-income samples (unverified). [NEW:richardson2013debt] — [University of Southampton, 2013](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/psychology/news/2013/09/23_debt_linked_to_mental_health_problems.page); bibliographic record: [Richardson, Elliott & Roberts, 2013, Clinical Psychology Review 33(8), 1148–1162](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/49517)
- Tracking logins to a money app, people checked their accounts right after payday and when balances were healthy, and **looked less often when in debt** (the "ostrich" pattern). **Moderate** (Iceland, one app, working paper). [LIB:olafsson2017] — [Olafsson & Pagel, 2017](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23945)
- In a 2022 US survey, 66% named money as a major stress. Among those stressed by money, 55% said it caused tension in the family. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:apa2022stress] — [APA, 2022](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/stress/2022/concerned-future-inflation)
- Worldwide, 79% of adults have an account, but only 56% could reliably get extra money in an emergency. **Moderate** (global). [LIB:findex2025] — [World Bank Global Findex, 2025](https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/globalfindex)

**Status and generosity**
- Among millionaires, those who *earned* their wealth were slightly happier than heirs. More wealth added only slightly more happiness, and only at very high levels. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:donnelly2018] — [Donnelly et al., 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167217744766)
- People whose spending matched their personality were more satisfied with life, a stronger link than total income or spending. **Moderate. WEIRD** (UK bank data). [LIB:matz2016] — [Matz et al., 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797616635200)
- People told to spend money on someone else felt happier that evening than those who spent it on themselves, whatever the amount. **Moderate.** Small US samples. [LIB:dunn2008] — [Dunn, Aknin & Norton, 2008](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/money-can-buy-happiness-if-you-spend-it-on-other-people)
- **Contested:** Kraus et al. (2012) list "more compassion, giving, and helping, and fewer unethical acts" among lower-class tendencies — [Kraus et al., 2012](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/social_class_solipsism_contextualism.pdf). In large German and US surveys, however, people higher in income and status were *more* likely to donate, volunteer and help. The library notes that earlier small lab studies suggesting richer people are less generous "have not held up well in larger tests" — [LIB:korndorfer2015, Korndörfer et al., 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0133193).

### Inferences
- FCSW is the cleanest construct for this app's purpose. It is *about the person's relationship to money, not the amount*, so the app can explore it without naming sums or income. The finding that it is unrelated to income means a person can feel unfree about money at any income level. Loosening the contingency is a route to *economic freedom* as an inner state. It does not replace the outer, material freedom that other researchers cover.
- The self-affirmation result (one study) is a plausible, low-cost "small step": remind yourself of a value or role that has nothing to do with money before or after a money worry. Evidence is moderate/early.
- Debt links to mental health are large, and avoidance is common, so shame plausibly sits in the middle. That link is an inference; no source on debt *shame* was opened. Insights about debt must not blame, and the app needs a gentle path for people in acute distress, given the suicide association.
- FCSW falling with age fits the broader pattern that self-esteem rises with age, but this is inference.
- **Craving links:** FCSW → economic freedom (inner), security, belonging (loneliness); debt → security and hope; giving → faith in people and happiness.

### Gaps
- **Debt and shame specifically:** no source opened measures shame. The project should treat "debt shame" as plausible but unsourced here.
- **Money talk taboo** (how often people avoid discussing money with family, partners or friends) was not researched within budget.
- FCSW research is from one US lab group, with no cross-cultural replication found or opened. Its numbers for the social-consequences paper were not seen.
- Money and status in low- and middle-income countries, e.g. how being a family's earner or remittance sender shapes self-worth, was not researched.

## 4. Social class identity: how class shapes the self, upward mobility, first-generation students, belonging interventions

### Takeaway
Class works as a culture. Fewer resources and lower rank tend to orient people outward ("contextualism": reading others, seeing outside constraints, relying on each other). More resources and higher rank tend to orient people inward ("solipsism": personal goals, control, self-expression). Institutions such as universities often run on the independent, middle-class model, which can make working-class and first-generation people feel they don't fit. Brief interventions that frame one's background as a source of strength and difference, or that normalise belonging worries, help, but only modestly and only where the setting actually lets people belong. Some famous class findings (e.g., richer people being less ethical or generous) are contested.

### Cited Findings
**How class shapes the self**
- Kraus et al. define social class through material resources (income/wealth, education, occupational prestige) and **subjective rank** relative to others. Rank predicts outcomes even after objective resources are controlled. Scarce resources and low rank foster **contextualism**: greater threat vigilance, lower sense of personal control, a communal self-concept, more empathic accuracy, external explanations for events, and (contested) more prosocial behaviour. Abundance and high rank foster **solipsism**: more sense of control, an agentic and unique self, dispositional explanations, and exchange-based relationships. A higher sense of control may buffer lower-class people's health. **Moderate:** a theory review drawing on national samples, lab studies and some cross-national work (France, Russia, Brazil, India), but "much of the evidence is correlational" and relationship research was "in its infancy". Mostly **WEIRD** (US). [NEW:kraus2012] — [Kraus et al., 2012, Psychological Review 119(3)](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/social_class_solipsism_contextualism.pdf)
- Class differences in independence are *graded*: "middle-class folks are more independent than lower-class folks, but less so than their upper-class counterparts" (Kraus). College-educated participants were more upset than working-class peers when denied an item they had chosen, and working-class participants more often rated maintaining relationships and fitting in as more important than expressing preferences (Markus & Snibbe 2005). **Moderate. WEIRD.** [NEW:apamonitor2015class] — [APA Monitor, 2015](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/02/class-differences)
- **Culture changes class effects:** lower-status Americans expressed more anger than higher-status Americans, but in Japan the pattern reversed (Park, Markus et al. 2013, *Emotion*). Class meanings are not universal. **Moderate** (single cross-national study, reported secondhand). [NEW:apamonitor2015class] — [APA Monitor, 2015](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/02/class-differences)
- **Help-seeking:** in school observations (Calarco), working-class children tended to stay quiet rather than "bother" teachers, while middle-class children got more help because they asked. Working-class parents saw intervening as rude and were "less apt to encourage their children to seek help". **Moderate. WEIRD** (US ethnography). [NEW:apamonitor2015class] — [APA Monitor, 2015](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/02/class-differences)
- **Cultural mismatch:** first-generation, working-class students given *independent* framings of college ("pave your own path") performed worse on tasks than middle-class peers, but performed as well when college was framed *interdependently* (family, collaboration) (Stephens et al. 2012, *JPSP*). The article says this was replicated at several universities. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [NEW:apamonitor2015class] — [APA Monitor, 2015](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/02/class-differences)
- **Contested findings about the rich:** high-status-car drivers cut off others and failed to stop for pedestrians more often (Piff 2012, PNAS), and higher-class people scored higher on entitlement (Piff 2014). Listing benefits of treating others as equals lowered entitlement — [APA Monitor, 2015](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/02/class-differences). Larger survey tests contradict the "rich are less prosocial" claim — [LIB:korndorfer2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0133193). Treat class-and-morality claims as **contested**.
- People with more power report more *coherent* self-views across situations (Kraus 2011): "High-power people stay authentically the same person no matter the context." This suggests that switching selves across settings is more a feature of lower-power positions. **Early.** [NEW:apamonitor2015class] — [APA Monitor, 2015](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/02/class-differences)

**First-generation students: difference-education**
- Randomised trial at a selective private US university: 168 incoming students (147 analysed: 66 first-generation, 81 continuing-generation). A one-hour panel of seniors in which panellists linked their stories to their **social-class backgrounds** (e.g., a first-generation student relying on an adviser because her parents couldn't guide her) was compared with an identical panel without background content. **Strong design, small sample → moderate.** [NEW:stephens2014] — [Stephens, Hamedani & Destin, 2014, Psychological Science](https://edpolicy.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/closing-social-class-achievement-gap.pdf)
  - In the standard condition first-generation students trailed by 0.30 GPA points. In difference-education the gap was not significant and was "**63% smaller**". First-generation students in difference-education outperformed those in the standard panel (d = 0.70). Continuing-generation students were unaffected.
  - The mechanism was **help-seeking**: first-generation students sought college resources about 30% more (p = .087), and resource-seeking mediated the GPA effect.
  - **All** students in difference-education reported less stress and social-identity threat, better social fit (5.63 vs 5.13) and life satisfaction, more academic identification, more time talking with family and friends from home, and more appreciation of difference and perspective-taking.
- The What Works Clearinghouse (Feb 2026 review) rates the trial "**meets WWC standards without reservations**". Its analytic sample was 134, first-year GPA 3.47 vs 3.30, improvement index 17 percentile points, "promising evidence", with the rating based solely on this one study at one university. **Moderate** (independent check, single site). [NEW:wwc2026stephens] — [WWC, 2026](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/study/78652)
- About two years later, difference-education participants mentioned their backgrounds more in a stressful speech task, and first-generation students showed greater "anabolic-balance reactivity" (a hormonal marker the authors read as physiological thriving, i.e., experiencing their background as a strength). **Early.** The page gives no N or effect sizes, and its journal/volume line appears garbled, so verify before formal citation. [NEW:stephens2015] — [Stephens et al., 2015 (Kellogg page)](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2015/a-difference-education-intervention-equips-first-generation-students-to/?p=1)

**Belonging interventions and their replication record**
- The large team-science randomised trial of a brief (<30 minute, story-sharing about 7½ minutes) online **social-belonging** exercise before college: **26,911 students at 22 institutions**. It raised first-year full-time completion by **about 2 percentage points** for racial-ethnic and social-class groups that had historically progressed at lower rates at their school, **but only where the institution afforded opportunities to belong**. Walton: without that, psychological work alone "won't move the needle" ("seed and soil"). Estimated to generalise to 749 US four-year institutions (about 12,136 extra students completing the first year each year if all used it). **Strong** (large RCT) for a **small, context-dependent** effect. **WEIRD** (US). [NEW:walton2023] — [Walton et al., 2023, Science 380(6644), 499–505](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/where-and-with-whom-does-a-brief-social-belonging-intervention-pr/); [Stanford News, 2023](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2023/05/improving-student-success-social-belonging)
- A parallel national trial of a growth-mindset lesson (12,490 US teens) found small effects (about 0.1 grade points), concentrated where peers welcomed challenge. **Moderate.** [LIB:yeager2019] — [Yeager et al., 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1466-y)

### Inferences
- **Class as identity, not just income:** for self-knowledge, the useful questions are about orientation, not class labels. Do I read the room first or state my preference first? Do I ask for help or try not to bother anyone? Do I explain my setbacks by circumstances or by my own traits? These habits are partly class-shaped, and each has strengths (empathy, loyalty, realism about constraints) and costs (not asking, under-claiming, self-blame or the reverse).
- **Upward mobility strain:** the cultural-mismatch evidence implies that people moving between class worlds may feel they must switch selves. The finding that higher power goes with more self-coherence hints that switching is common for less powerful people. Difference-education suggests the *reframe* that helps: "my background gave me tools; settings differ in what they reward." This is inference: no direct study of mobility strain was opened.
- **Belonging work helps only in fertile soil.** An app cannot change a person's workplace or school. It should not imply that feeling out of place is the person's fault, and it should not overpromise. Effects of the best interventions are real but small (about 2 percentage points; 0.1 grade points).
- **Craving links:** belonging (class fit, first-generation); faith in people (contextualist empathy and interdependence as a resource; contested prosociality claims); hope (background as strength); freedom (asking for help as a skill that unlocks resources).

### Gaps
- **Upward mobility and identity strain** (e.g., guilt toward family, the "imposter" feeling after mobility, health costs of striving): no primary source was opened. Search budget exhausted.
- Walton & Cohen's original (2007/2011) single-site results and their effect sizes were not opened, so the claim that the original effects were larger than the 2023 trial's is not sourced here.
- Class-identity research outside the US, UK and Japan, and in caste-based or other non-Western stratification systems (e.g., South Asia, Latin America, Africa), was not found within budget. The Kraus review mentions only some cross-national work.
- No meta-analysis of subjective social status and health or well-being was opened.

## 5. Education and learning identity: academic self-concept, lifelong learning and well-being in adults

### Takeaway
How able people feel academically depends on *who they are compared with*. In high-achieving groups people feel less able (the "big fish, little pond" effect, worldwide). Framing one's background as a strength boosted first-generation students' academic identification. Most adults see themselves as learners, but adult learning is unequal by prior schooling, and education strongly predicts whether a person works formally or informally worldwide. Direct evidence that adult learning raises well-being was not opened here.

### Cited Findings
- Across 33 studies of 1.28 million students, being in a higher-achieving class or school went with feeling *less* able oneself (the big-fish-little-pond effect). It was found worldwide and was strongest in high school and in Asian samples. **Strong.** [LIB:fang2018] — [Fang et al., 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01569/full)
- Difference-education raised academic identification and social fit and reduced social-identity threat, i.e., the learning identity of all students, and especially of first-generation students. **Moderate.** [NEW:stephens2014] — [Stephens et al., 2014](https://edpolicy.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/closing-social-class-achievement-gap.pdf)
- In the US, 73% of adults call themselves lifelong learners, but 87% of graduates vs 60% of those with high school or less did personal learning in the past year. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:pew2016learning] — [Pew, 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2016/03/22/lifelong-learning-and-technology/)
- In the EU about 47% of adults aged 25–64 took part in learning in a year (2022), from over 65% in Sweden to under 25% in Greece. Less-schooled and unemployed adults joined least. **Moderate** (European). [LIB:eurostat2026adult] — [Eurostat, 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Adult_learning_statistics)
- Worldwide, informality falls as education rises: workers with secondary or tertiary education are less likely to be in informal employment than those with no or only primary schooling. **Moderate/strong** (official statistics). [NEW:ilo2018informal] — [ILO, 2018](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/more-60-cent-worlds-employed-population-are-informal-economy)
- People who believed passions are "found" rather than developed were less open to new interests and lost interest faster when things got hard. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:okeefe2018] — [O'Keefe, Dweck & Walton, 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797618780643)

### Inferences
- A learning identity ("I am someone who learns") is a source of *hope* that does not depend on the job or income. But participation is lowest among those with less schooling and those out of work, the same people for whom work identity is most precarious. An app can support the learning identity without requiring formal courses: noticing what one learned today, from anyone.
- Big-fish-little-pond implies that people's sense of competence is *local*. Moving into a stronger group can make a capable person feel small, a learning-side analogue of impostor feelings. An insight can say so honestly.

### Gaps
- The reciprocal relationship between academic self-concept and achievement (Marsh and colleagues' meta-analyses) was not opened.
- **Adult learning and well-being:** no meta-analysis or longitudinal study linking adult or lifelong learning to well-being was opened (budget). This is a real gap; claims that learning in adulthood "makes people happier" are unsourced here.
- No data were opened on education as a source of identity or shame in low- and middle-income countries (e.g., people without schooling).

## 6. Entrepreneurship, side hustles and informal work, including low- and middle-income countries

### Takeaway
Most of the world's workers (about 2 billion, more than 61% of the employed) are informal: no employment contract, social protection or job title in the Western sense. The share is 85.8% in Africa and 68% in Asia-Pacific and the Arab States. Business owners worldwide are on average slightly more satisfied than employees, mainly for the autonomy, but not where laws are weakly enforced. Fear of failure holds back about 2 in 5 people. For informal workers, work identity is tied to survival and lacks the protections that cushion job loss.

### Cited Findings
- About **2 billion workers, "more than 61 per cent of the world's employed population"**, are in informal employment, and 93% of the world's informal employment is in emerging and developing countries. Shares: Africa 85.8%, Arab States 68.6%, Asia-Pacific 68.2%, Americas 40.0%, Europe and Central Asia 25.1%. Men 63.0%, women 58.1% (just over 740 million informal workers are women). Rural residents are almost twice as likely as urban residents to be informal, and agriculture is more than 90% informal. Poverty is "both a cause and a consequence" of informality. For workers informality means "a lack of social protection, rights at work and decent working conditions". **Strong** (official global statistics; data from 2018, so may be outdated). [NEW:ilo2018informal] — [ILO, 2018](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/more-60-cent-worlds-employed-population-are-informal-economy)
- Across 94 studies in 82 countries, business owners were slightly more satisfied with work and slightly happier with life than employees. They did *worse* than employees where laws are weakly enforced and better where laws are strong. **Strong** (worldwide). [LIB:stephan2023] — [Stephan, Rauch & Hatak, 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10422587211072799)
- Most US small-business owners do not aim to grow big. Being their own boss and flexible hours are major reasons for starting. **Moderate. WEIRD.** [LIB:hurst2011] — [Hurst & Pugsley, 2011](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17041)
- Across 53 economies (more than 160,000 adults), fear of failure stopped about 2 in 5 people from starting a business. Those who had closed a business before were more likely to try again. **Moderate** (global). [LIB:gem2026] — [GEM, 2026](https://www.gemconsortium.org/reports/latest-global-report)
- 16% of US adults had ever earned money via gig apps, mostly to supplement income or cover gaps, and about 3 in 10 recent gig workers called it their main job — [LIB:pew2021gig](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2021/12/08/the-state-of-gig-work-in-2021/). 38% of US workers freelanced, most often for flexibility, control over their money and choice of where to work — [LIB:upwork2023](https://www.upwork.com/research/freelance-forward-2023-research-report). **Moderate. WEIRD.**
- The mental-health harm of unemployment is larger in less economically developed, more unequal countries with weak protection, which describes the context of most informal workers. **Strong.** [NEW:paulmoser2009] — [Paul & Moser, 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)

### Inferences
- In most of the world, "what do you do?" is not answered with a job title or employer. A worldwide app must not assume formal employment, a boss, salary, career ladder or retirement plan. Work identity for a market trader, smallholder farmer, domestic worker or driver is closer to "I provide", "I'm resourceful" or "I'm my own boss". That draws on *freedom* and *security* at once, and it lacks the safety net that cushions loss.
- The entrepreneurship well-being gain is mostly autonomy, and it reverses where institutions are weak. This suggests that self-employment by necessity in low- and middle-income countries may *not* carry the same satisfaction as self-employment by choice. That is an inference: no necessity-vs-opportunity study was opened.
- **Craving links:** self-employment → freedom; informality → insecurity; fear of failure → blocked hope and success.

### Gaps
- **Identity and self-worth of informal workers** (how street vendors, domestic workers or smallholders draw dignity from their work): no qualitative or quantitative source was opened. Major gap for a worldwide app.
- **Necessity vs opportunity entrepreneurship and well-being** in low- and middle-income countries: not opened.
- **Side-hustle identity** (e.g., the "slash" identity of "teacher / baker"; whether a second identity buffers self-worth): no source opened.
- Entrepreneurs' grief and identity loss after business failure: not opened.
- ILO informality figures are from 2018. A newer edition may exist but was not opened.

## What this means for the app

### Takeaway
The strongest, most usable levers are these:
1. **What people put first.** Intrinsic over extrinsic priorities. Strong, worldwide.
2. **Whether worth is contingent on money.** Financially contingent self-worth: unrelated to income, linked to stress, lower autonomy and loneliness. Moderate, WEIRD.
3. **How people shape their work.** Adding resources and challenges vs only cutting. Strong, correlational.
4. **What else holds the self up** when work changes. Coping resources, time structure and groups. Strong (meta-analysis) to early.
5. **Background as strength** and help-seeking as a skill. Moderate, WEIRD, small effects.

Each of these can be revealed by everyday choices without naming money amounts, income, class labels or comparisons with others.

### Everyday situations that reveal how worth is tied to work, money or class (concrete options, no scales)
1. **"Someone at a gathering asks, 'So, what do you do?'"** Options: name my job or trade · talk about something I care about outside work · mention my family or community role · steer the talk elsewhere because I'd rather not say. *Reveals work-role centrality and possible shame (job loss, informal work, unemployment).* Works for informal workers too.
2. **"A day off arrives and a work message comes in."** Options: answer it right away · glance and leave it for later · don't look · feel uneasy all day whether I answer or not. *Separates engagement from compulsion (workaholism: inner pressure, bad feelings when not working) and switching off (detachment).*
3. **"A money worry pops up (an unexpected bill, a slow week of sales, a payment that's late)."** Options: look at it straight away · put it aside to deal with later · avoid looking for now · feel it says something about me as a person. *Reveals the ostrich pattern and financially contingent self-worth.* No amounts named.
4. **"You could take extra paid work this weekend, or keep a plan with people you care about."** Options: take the work · keep the plan · try to do both · feel guilty either way. *Reveals extrinsic vs intrinsic priority and the time-with-loved-ones cost of financial contingency.*
5. **"A friend or colleague shares good news about a promotion, a new purchase or a big sale."** Options: feel happy for them · feel spurred to work harder on my own goal · feel a sting and go quiet · start counting what I lack. *Reveals benign vs malicious envy and comparison.* The app reflects the person's own reaction and never makes a comparison itself.
6. **"You're praised for something you did well."** Options: accept it · say it was luck or the team · wonder when they'll find out I'm not that good · change the subject. *Reveals impostor feelings.*
7. **"Something at work or in your trade keeps draining you."** Options: ask for help or advice · learn a way to do it better · take on something new that excites me · cut it out or avoid it where I can. *Maps onto the job-crafting dimensions (structural resources, social resources, challenges, reducing demands).*
8. **"You're stuck on something in a class, at work or with a form."** Options: ask someone right away · try alone a long time before asking · look it up quietly · give up rather than bother anyone. *Reveals class-shaped help-seeking (not "bothering" people) and the help-seeking mechanism behind difference-education.*
9. **"You're in a place where people seem to come from a different background (a new job, a university, an in-law's home)."** Options: adapt how I speak and act · stay exactly as I am · stay quiet and watch · find someone who seems like me. *Reveals self-switching and belonging uncertainty* without labelling class.
10. **"A relative or friend asks to borrow or be given money."** Options: give without hesitation · give but feel resentful · say no and explain · avoid the conversation. *Reveals money-talk avoidance, generosity and obligation* (relevant to interdependent and remittance cultures).
11. **"Your work slows down or ends: a contract ends, a season ends, retirement approaches."** Options: fill the days with a new routine · lean on people and groups · feel lost without it · keep busy so I don't have to think. *Reveals reliance on the work role vs the other supports that protect mental health in job loss and retirement.*
12. **"At the end of the day, what made it feel worthwhile?"** Options: I moved something forward that mattered to me · I earned or saved something · someone noticed my work · I helped someone. *Reveals the person's own definition of success and the progress principle.*

### What an insight could honestly say (examples, with evidence grade)
- On job loss or slow work: "Losing work, or having less of it, weighs on almost everyone's mood and self-respect. Research across hundreds of studies finds it genuinely causes distress; it isn't a personal weakness." **Strong** — [Paul & Moser, 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)
- On priorities: "You chose the people over the extra work. Across about 70,000 people in many regions, putting growth and relationships ahead of money and image goes with feeling better. Wanting money isn't the problem; what comes first seems to matter." **Strong/moderate** — [Bradshaw et al.](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2022_BradshawEtAl_MetaAspriations_JPSP.pdf)
- On a money worry felt as personal: "When a money worry feels like a verdict on you, that's worth noticing. In research, tying self-worth to finances showed up at every income level and went with more stress and feeling less free." **Moderate, WEIRD** — [Park et al., 2017](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/2017_Park_etal_PSPB.pdf)
- On avoiding the bill: "Many people look at their finances less when things are tight. It's a common way of protecting yourself, and it can let worries grow in the dark." **Moderate** — [Olafsson & Pagel, 2017](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23945)
- On the work message: "You felt uneasy even when not working. Researchers separate *loving* work from *not being able to stop*: the second goes with more stress and burnout and doesn't make the work better." **Strong** (secondary summary) — [Clark et al. 2016 via QIC-WD](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Workaholism%20101921.pdf)
- On the draining task: "You chose to learn a better way rather than avoid it. People who add skills, support or a fresh challenge to their work tend to feel more engaged; those who mainly cut things out tend to feel more strained." **Strong**, correlational — [Rudolph et al., 2017](https://www.mentorcoach.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Rudolph-Katz-Lavigne-Zacher-2017-job-crafting-meta-analysis-due-by-week-5.pdf)
- On not asking for help: "You tried a long time alone before asking. Many people are raised not to 'bother' others. That is a real kindness, and it can also keep useful help out of reach." **Moderate, WEIRD** — [APA Monitor, 2015](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/02/class-differences); [Stephens et al., 2014](https://edpolicy.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/closing-social-class-achievement-gap.pdf)
- On adapting in a new setting: "You changed how you spoke to fit in. Different places reward different habits. Where you come from may have taught you things this place doesn't yet see, like reading people well." **Moderate** — [Kraus et al., 2012](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/social_class_solipsism_contextualism.pdf); [Stephens et al., 2014](https://edpolicy.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/closing-social-class-achievement-gap.pdf). Avoid the word "class" unless the person uses it.
- On praise turned aside: "Feeling you'll be 'found out' is very common, even among people who are doing well." **Moderate** — [Bravata et al., 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs11606-019-05364-1)
- On a named negative trait, said politely: "Lately money news has been setting the tone of your days. That pattern can crowd out the things you said matter more."

### Small steps the evidence supports (optional, tiny, never prescriptive)
- **Name one thing you value that has nothing to do with money or work** (a person, a skill, a belief) when a money worry hits. Basis: self-affirmation removed the autonomy drop in people with high financial contingency. **Moderate/early, WEIRD** — [Park et al., 2017](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/2017_Park_etal_PSPB.pdf)
- **Add one resource to your work this week**: ask one person for advice, or learn one small thing that makes a task easier. Basis: increasing structural or social resources has the strongest links with engagement. **Strong**, correlational — [Rudolph et al., 2017](https://www.mentorcoach.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Rudolph-Katz-Lavigne-Zacher-2017-job-crafting-meta-analysis-due-by-week-5.pdf)
- **Set a small "work is done" cue**, such as a set time to stop checking messages. **Strong** (mostly cross-sectional) — [Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah, 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02072/full)
- **Note one bit of progress on something that matters to you** at the end of the day. **Moderate** — [Amabile & Kramer, 2011](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity)
- **Look at one money matter you've been avoiding, just look, no action needed.** Inferred from the ostrich pattern; no trial tested this step. **Early/inference** — [Olafsson & Pagel, 2017](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23945)
- **Ask for help once today before you've struggled long.** Basis: help-seeking mediated difference-education's GPA gains. **Moderate, WEIRD** — [Stephens et al., 2014](https://edpolicy.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/closing-social-class-achievement-gap.pdf)
- **Recall one way your background or upbringing helps you now.** Extrapolated from a panel-based intervention, not tested as a solo reflection. **Early/inference** — [Stephens et al., 2014](https://edpolicy.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/closing-social-class-achievement-gap.pdf); [Stephens et al., 2015](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2015/a-difference-education-intervention-equips-first-generation-students-to/?p=1)
- **Keep or start one group tie that isn't about work** (a team, club, faith group, neighbours), especially before or after a work transition. **Early** for retirement — [Psychology and Aging 2023](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2023/journal-article-abstracts/pag0000757/); social and time-structure coping resources matter in unemployment, **strong** — [McKee-Ryan et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15641890/)
- **After a setback, talk to yourself as you would to a friend.** **Strong** — [Ferrari et al., 2019](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/87545/self-compassion-interventions-and-psychosocial-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-of-rcts); [Breines & Chen, 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167212445599)
- **Do one small thing for someone else** (time or a small gift; never name a sum). **Moderate** — [Dunn et al., 2008](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/money-can-buy-happiness-if-you-spend-it-on-other-people)

### What to avoid
- **No amounts, income brackets, savings targets or product suggestions.** The financial-contingency evidence also argues against making money *more* salient: frame money questions around feelings and choices, and allow skipping.
- **No class labels or inferences about someone's class**, and no comparison with others. The app's no-comparison rule fits the evidence that rank and comparison drive dissatisfaction ([Boyce et al., 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797610362671); [Card et al., 2012](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16396)).
- **Don't present contested class findings as fact**, e.g., "richer people are less kind" or "poorer people are more generous" ([Korndörfer et al., 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0133193) contradicts the small lab studies).
- **Don't put structural problems on the individual.** Belonging interventions work only where the setting affords belonging ([Walton et al., 2023](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2023/05/improving-student-success-social-belonging)), and unemployment harm depends on national safety nets ([Paul & Moser, 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)). Insights should say "this is hard for most people in your situation", not "you need to feel more confident".
- **Don't overpromise.** The best brief interventions produce small effects (about 2 percentage points; about 0.1 grade points). The impostor literature has no tested treatments.
- **Don't glorify hustle, calling or long hours.** Workaholism is unrelated to performance and linked to stress and poor health, and 55+ hour weeks carry cardiovascular risk ([WHO & ILO, 2021](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo)). Don't use "burnout" as a diagnosis: the WHO calls it an occupational phenomenon.
- **Don't assume formal employment.** More than 61% of the world's workers are informal ([ILO, 2018](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/more-60-cent-worlds-employed-population-are-informal-economy)). Phrase situations as "your work or trade", "a slow week", "a customer", not "your boss", "your salary" or "your career".
- **Be careful with debt and job loss.** Debt is linked to suicide risk and job loss to doubled rates of psychological problems ([Southampton, 2013](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/psychology/news/2013/09/23_debt_linked_to_mental_health_problems.page); [Paul & Moser, 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)). The app is not therapy, so it needs a gentle, non-diagnostic way to point people in acute distress toward real help, and must never shame debt.
- **Don't treat retirement as uniformly good or bad.** Studies split roughly a third positive, half negative, a fifth null ([Ejike et al.](https://www.mentalhealthdataprizeafrica.aphrc.org/repository/paper/35314/paradox-of-life-after-work-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-on-retirement-anxiety-and-life-satisfaction)).
- **Flag WEIRD limits internally.** Financial contingency, difference-education, belonging, impostor feelings and most class-psychology findings are US-based. Bradshaw (aspirations), Paul & Moser (unemployment), Stephan (business owners), the big-fish-little-pond effect and the ILO statistics are the most global.

### Craving map (summary)
| Craving | Main evidence here | Self-knowledge that helps |
|---|---|---|
| Freedom (economic) | Financial contingency ↔ less autonomy, at any income; job crafting; self-employment autonomy | "Is my worth riding on money today?"; "What can I add to my work?" |
| Security | Unemployment doubles psychological problems; debt ↔ mental health; informality = no safety net | Knowing what else holds you up: people, routine, other roles |
| Hope | Difference-education (background as strength); learning identity; benign envy | "My background gave me tools"; "I can still grow" |
| Success | Intrinsic vs extrinsic priority; impostor feelings; workaholism ≠ performance | Defining success by what you put first, not rank |
| Belonging | Belonging trials (context-dependent); cultural mismatch; group ties in retirement | Noticing self-switching; keeping groups across transitions |
| Faith in people | Contextualism (reading and relying on others); help-seeking; generosity (contested class link) | Asking for help; giving in small ways |
| Contentment and happiness | Meaningful work (moderate-to-large links, the abstract gives a pooled range of r = .44 to −.49, with life satisfaction, life meaning, health and (negatively) quitting); progress days; detachment | Noticing meaningful progress; switching off |

### Gaps
- None of the suggested small steps has been tested *as a self-reflection app prompt*. They are extrapolated from lab, survey or panel interventions.
- No evidence was opened on money-talk taboos, debt shame, mobility strain, informal workers' dignity, or adult learning and well-being. These remain unsourced in this file and should not be stated as findings.
