# Contentment and lasting happiness: what predicts it, what people wrongly chase, and how personality shapes the path

How to read the tags. Every finding carries `[id · NEW/LIB · grade]`.
- **NEW** means the source is not yet in the evidence library and was opened in this session.
- **LIB (re-opened)** means it is already in the library and was re-opened in this session.

Grades:
- **strong**: a meta-analysis, or a finding replicated many times.
- **moderate**: solid single studies, or large surveys.
- **early**: small, new or exploratory work.

"WEIRD" marks evidence that rests only on Western, educated, rich samples.

## 1. What predicts lasting well-being?

### Takeaway
The best-supported predictors are close relationships and a working set of basic resources: income, health and freedom. Income keeps adding a little happiness on a log scale, but much less for people who are unhappy for other reasons. Happiness is not fixed at a "set point". Some events, such as unemployment, divorce and disability, leave lasting dents on average, while others, such as marriage, fade back within a couple of years. Valuing money and possessions goes with somewhat lower well-being across hundreds of samples. Where attention goes from moment to moment, and whether life feels meaningful as well as pleasant, are separate but related matters.

### Cited Findings
**Relationships (Harvard Study of Adult Development)**
- The study began in 1938 with 724 participants: 268 Harvard College sophomores and 456 young adults from Boston. It now includes 1,300 of their descendants and has followed the first generation from their teens into old age `[waldinger2023 · NEW · moderate]` — [Harvard Gazette interview with Robert Waldinger, 2023](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you)
- Waldinger reports that satisfaction with relationships in midlife, "particularly in their marriages", was the strongest predictor of who was happy and healthy at 80. It predicted better than cholesterol or blood pressure at 50. He proposes stress regulation as the mechanism: isolation keeps the body in low-level fight-or-flight `[waldinger2023 · NEW · moderate]` — [Harvard Gazette 2023](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you)
- Waldinger also claims that "wealth does not increase well-being significantly once we have our basic needs met". He says loneliness is "as dangerous to your health as smoking half a pack of cigarettes a day", and he recommends "social fitness", meaning ongoing maintenance of relationships: "everybody needs at least one solid relationship". These are interview claims from the study director, not data tables. The smoking comparison comes from other research that was not opened here `[waldinger2023 · NEW · moderate for the cohort finding, early for the interview claims]` — [Harvard Gazette 2023](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you)
- Social engagement is also the active ingredient in successful happiness pursuits; see Section 3 (Rohrer 2018 in Germany, Ford 2015 across four cultures). On country differences, social support is one of the six main factors in the World Happiness Report; see Section 6.

**Income (the Killingsworth 2021 study and the 2023 adversarial collaboration)**
- Killingsworth 2021 drew on more than one million real-time reports from a large US sample. Experienced well-being rose linearly with log income, with an "equally steep slope above $80,000 as below it". This contradicts the earlier claim that day-to-day mood levels off at about $75,000 `[killingsworth2021 · NEW · strong for the US association]` — [Wharton/PNAS 2021 abstract](https://wpa.wharton.upenn.edu/content/academic-papers/experienced-well-being-rises-with-income-even-above-75000-per-year/)
- In the 2023 adversarial collaboration, Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers re-analysed the data and found three patterns:
  - The least happy group rises with income up to about $100,000 and then stops rising.
  - The middle group rises linearly.
  - The happiest group's gains accelerate above $100,000.
  
  The earlier $75,000 plateau held only at the unhappy end of the distribution. For people who are "rich and miserable", more money does not help. Killingsworth: "Money is not the secret to happiness, but it can probably help a bit" `[killingsworth2023 · LIB (re-opened) · strong; US only, WEIRD]` — [ScienceDaily 2023](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/03/230306163006.htm)
- The original Kahneman & Deaton 2010 figures were about 450,000 US survey answers. Life evaluation kept rising with income, while day-to-day mood levelled off at about $75,000 `[kahneman2010 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate]` — library record.
- Swedish lottery winners were more satisfied with life for more than a decade, but day-to-day happiness changed much less `[lindqvist2020 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate]` — library record.
- Income rank relative to similar people predicted life satisfaction better than the absolute amount, and comparisons with people above counted more than those with people below `[boyce2010 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate, UK]` — library record.

**Hedonic adaptation and revisions to the set-point idea**
- Lucas reviewed large German and British panel studies that measured people before and after major events:
  - **Marriage:** the boost fades back to baseline "within just a couple of years".
  - **Widowhood:** people adapt, but slowly ("about 7 years").
  - **Divorce, unemployment, and serious illness or disability:** on average people do not return to their earlier level.
  - **Individual differences:** these are large. For example, people who later married and stayed married were already happier five years before the wedding than those who later divorced.
  
  Lucas's conclusion is that happiness levels do change, that adaptation is not inevitable, and that life events matter `[lucas2007 · NEW · strong (large prospective panels); German/British samples]` — [APS press release on Lucas's Current Directions review](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/new-study-reports-on-the-state-of-human-happiness.html). The page is dated March 2008. The underlying Current Directions article is usually cited as Lucas 2007.
- Luhmann, Hofmann, Eid & Lucas 2012 pooled longitudinal data from 188 publications (313 samples, N = 65,911) on eight events:
  - **Family events:** marriage, divorce, bereavement and childbirth.
  - **Work events:** unemployment, reemployment, retirement and relocation.
  
  The events had "very different effects on affective and cognitive well-being". Effects on life satisfaction were generally stronger and more consistent than effects on mood, and they did not depend on how desirable the event was considered `[luhmann2012 · NEW · strong]` — [DIW abstract page](https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.775342.de/s_7285.html)
- A German panel of more than 24,000 people followed for 15 years found that life satisfaction fell after job loss and, on average, did not fully recover even after re-employment `[lucas2004 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate]` — library record.
- Holiday boosts were mostly anticipation. Once home, travellers were no happier than people who had not gone away, and the small glow from a very relaxing trip was gone by about eight weeks `[nawijn2010 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate, Dutch]` — library record.

**Materialism (Dittmar et al. 2014 meta-analysis)**
- The meta-analysis covered 753 effect sizes from 259 independent samples. Materialism was consistently linked to lower personal well-being, and the size depended on the measure:
  - Multifaceted materialist values and beliefs: r = −.19 (ρ = −.24).
  - Relative importance of materialist goals: r = −.16 (ρ = −.21).
  - Emphasis on money alone: r = −.08 to −.11 (ρ = −.09 to −.14).
  
  `[dittmar2014 · NEW · strong (meta-analysis), correlational]` — [PubMed 25347131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/)
- The strongest links were with risky health and consumer behaviour and with negative self-appraisals (r = −.28 to −.44). The weakest were with life satisfaction and negative affect (r = −.13 to −.15). Moderators were age, gender, settings that support materialism, cultures that stress "affective autonomy", economic growth and wealth gaps. Mediation analyses suggested that poor satisfaction of psychological needs explains the link. The authors call for more experimental and longitudinal work, since causality is not established `[dittmar2014 · NEW · strong]` — [PubMed 25347131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/)

**Experiences versus things, and spending on others or on time**
- People tended to say experiences made them happier than objects, but the gap was small for people on the lowest incomes `[vanboven2003 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate; US]` — library record.
- **Later caveat:** in two diary studies, physical purchases gave small bursts of happiness more often over two weeks, while experiences gave stronger but rarer peaks `[weidman2016 · LIB (not re-opened) · early; small Canadian samples]` — library record.
- Waiting for an experience feels more pleasant than waiting for a possession `[kumar2014 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate]` — library record.
- **Buying time:** surveys of 6,271 people in the US, Canada, Denmark and the Netherlands linked paying to save time (for example, help with chores) to higher life satisfaction. A small experiment (n = 60, Canada) found that time-saving purchases lifted mood more than material ones `[whillans2017 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate; rich countries only]` — library record.
- **Spending on others:** in 120 of 136 countries, people who had recently given money away were more satisfied with life (Gallup World Poll, about 235,000 people, plus experiments including Uganda, India and South Africa) `[aknin2013 · LIB (not re-opened) · strong; worldwide]` — library record.

**Mind-wandering**
- In about 250,000 phone check-ins from roughly 2,250 people, minds were wandering 47% of the time, and people were usually less happy while their minds wandered. The volunteers were self-selected iPhone users, mostly from rich countries `[killingsworth2010 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate]` — library record.

**Meaning versus happiness**
- Baumeister, Vohs, Aaker & Garbinsky 2013 found that happiness and meaningfulness overlapped "about half". Once the overlap was removed, the two had different correlates:
  - **Happiness, not meaning:** getting what one wants and needs, having money, good health, being a "taker", and (falling just short of significance) a focus on the present.
  - **Meaning, not happiness:** giving and helping, stress, worry, arguing, thinking about past and future, and self-expression. Of the self-expression items, "a whopping 25" correlated with meaning and "only two with happiness".
  
  The authors call the study exploratory and say its results need confirmation. The second study used mostly students `[baumeister2013 · NEW · early-moderate; US, correlational]` — [Baumeister et al. 2013, J Positive Psychology (author PDF)](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2013-meaning-vs-happiness.pdf)

### Inferences
- A life with strong relationships, enough money, health and some freedom predicts lasting satisfaction better than any single choice. The everyday levers people control, such as who they spend time with, what they spend on and where their attention goes, matter at the margin rather than transforming a life.
- Because some events leave lasting changes, "happiness always returns to baseline" is wrong. So is the opposite promise that any one change will reliably lift someone for good.
- The materialism link is stronger for valuing possessions as a route to status and identity than for valuing money as such. Asking what someone wants money *for* is more informative than asking how much they want it.
- Meaning and happiness come apart in their costs. Caring, effortful, worried investment in others can lower momentary happiness and raise meaning. A content life need not feel "happy" from moment to moment.

### Gaps
- Waldinger & Schulz's own 2023 book and the study's peer-reviewed papers were not opened. The cohort's demographic make-up (for example its sex and ethnicity) is not stated in the source opened, so the report should not describe it beyond "Harvard students and young Boston adults from 1938".
- The 2021 Killingsworth sample size in participants was not in the abstract opened.
- Steger's meaning-in-life work and cross-national findings that meaning runs higher in poorer nations were not opened (the search budget ran out). No claim is made about them.
- The later caveats on experiences versus things, such as social class moderating the experiential advantage, were not opened beyond the library's Weidman & Dunn and Van Boven entries.
- Luhmann 2012's event-by-event adaptation rates were not available from the abstract page; the full text was blocked by a CAPTCHA.

## 2. Contentment specifically: calm positive feelings, satisficing, comparison, gratitude and savouring

### Takeaway
Contentment is the calm, low-arousal side of positive feeling. Across 12 countries, ordinary adults most often define happiness as inner harmony and good relationships rather than excitement. How much people *ideally* want calm versus excitement is shaped by culture, without changing how they actually feel. Habits that undermine contentment include:
- **Maximising:** always searching for the best option, linked to more regret, more comparison and less happiness.
- **Upward comparison and heavy, passive social media use:** linked to lower well-being, most clearly for young people in Western countries.

The two most popular contentment practices work differently:
- **Gratitude exercises:** their effects are real but small.
- **Savouring:** one 2026 meta-analysis finds moderate effects, with the usual small-study caveats.

### Cited Findings
**Low-arousal positive states and ideal affect**
- Tsai et al. 2007 defines "ideal affect" as the feelings people *want* to have, as distinct from the feelings they actually have. In a survey of N = 568 students:
  - European Americans valued high-arousal positive states (HAP: excitement, enthusiasm) more, and low-arousal positive states (LAP: calm, peacefulness, relaxation) less, than Hong Kong Chinese.
  - Asian Americans valued LAP more than European Americans.
  - Group differences in ideal LAP were F(2, 561) = 8.69, p < .001.
  
  `[tsai2007 · NEW · moderate]` — [Tsai, Miao, Seppala, Fung & Yeung 2007, JPSP (Stanford lab PDF)](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/tsai_miao_seppala_fung_yeung_2007.pdf)
- In experiments, people assigned to *influence* others came to value excitement more (d = 0.45). People assigned to *adjust* to others valued calm more (d = 0.39). Influencers were 3.59 times more likely to pick an exciting CD over a calm one. The manipulations changed ideal affect but not actual affect, and the effects did not differ by culture. The paper did **not** test well-being outcomes, and its samples were students, with women only in two studies `[tsai2007 · NEW · moderate]` — [Tsai et al. 2007](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/tsai_miao_seppala_fung_yeung_2007.pdf)
- In the Eudaimonic and Hedonic Happiness Investigation, 2,799 urban adults aged 30 to 60 in 12 countries gave open-ended definitions of happiness. The countries were Argentina, Brazil, Croatia, Hungary, India, Italy, Mexico, New Zealand, Norway, Portugal, South Africa and the US. "Inner harmony predominated among psychological definitions", and family and social relationships predominated among contextual ones, with "little variation by age and gender" `[dellefave2016 · NEW · moderate; cross-national, non-random urban samples]` — [Delle Fave et al. 2016, Frontiers in Psychology](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00030/text)
- The Interdependent Happiness Scale (Hitokoto & Uchida 2015) measures three things:
  - Relationship-oriented happiness.
  - "Quiescent" (calm) happiness.
  - "Ordinary" happiness, meaning feeling about as well off as others rather than above them.
  
  Its nine-item, higher-order structure held up in student samples from Malaysia (n = 263), the Philippines (n = 239) and India (n = 310), though some items were perceived differently and the scale "needs further improvement" `[ihs_asia_validation · NEW · early (psychometric)]` — [IHS validation study abstract (University of Nottingham Ningbo repository)](https://unnc.globalimpact.cn/en/publications/psychometric-qualities-evaluation-of-the-interdependent-happiness/)

**Satisficing versus maximising (Schwartz et al. 2002)**
- The study had seven samples (N = 1,747: students in the US and Canada, nurses, commuters and jury-pool members) and a 13-item Maximization Scale (α = .71). Its main findings:
  - **Happiness:** maximising was negatively correlated with happiness (about r = −.25).
  - **Depression:** positively correlated (r = .34).
  - **Regret:** strongly correlated (r = .52). Regret partly mediated the links with depression and unhappiness.
  - **Optimism, self-esteem and life satisfaction:** maximising went with lower levels of each.
  
  `[schwartz2002 · NEW · moderate; correlational, North American]` — [Schwartz et al. 2002, JPSP (PDF)](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf). Minus signs were lost in the PDF extraction, so directions come from the article's wording.
- Maximisers made more social comparisons (β = .30), both upward and downward, and upward comparison went with regret and lower happiness among them. They were less happy with recent purchases and felt worse after working beside a faster peer. Satisficers showed little change `[schwartz2002 · NEW · moderate]` — [Schwartz et al. 2002](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf)

**Social comparison and social media**
- The World Happiness Report 2026 is devoted to social media. Among more than 270,000 15-year-olds in 47 countries (PISA 2022):
  - Heavy users (7+ hours a day) reported much lower well-being than light users (under 1 hour).
  - **Girls in Western Europe:** the gap was almost 1 point on a 0 to 10 scale, and about half that elsewhere.
  - **Boys in Western Europe:** almost 0.5 points. In the other 35 countries, the gap was essentially zero.
  - **Dose:** for girls, life satisfaction was highest at under 1 hour a day and fell with each extra hour.
  
  `[whr2026 · NEW · moderate; correlational]` — [WHR 2026 executive summary](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)
- In the same report, belonging mattered more than screen time. For girls in the UK and Ireland, moving from low to high school belonging raised life satisfaction about four times more than cutting social media from high to low. Across all 47 countries, the belonging effect was about six times larger `[whr2026 · NEW · moderate]` — [WHR 2026 executive summary](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)
- How people use social media matters:
  - Communicating, learning and creating content went with higher life satisfaction, while scrolling social feeds, gaming and browsing for fun went with lower.
  - In Latin America, platforms built for connection showed positive links to happiness, while algorithm-driven content platforms showed negative links at high use.
  - In the Middle East and North Africa, passive, visual and influencer-driven use was linked to more stress and depressive symptoms and to more negative comparisons with parents' quality of life.
  
  `[whr2026 · NEW · moderate]` — [WHR 2026 executive summary](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)
- The decline in youth well-being is regional, not global. Under-25s are happier now than in 2006 to 2010 in 85 of 136 countries. In the US, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, under-25 happiness fell by an average of 0.86 points over about 20 years `[whr2026 · NEW · moderate]` — [Gallup: World Happiness Report 2026](https://www.gallup.com/analytics/349487/world-happiness-report.aspx)
- In a large US experiment, adults paid to deactivate Facebook for four weeks became slightly happier, spent more time with family and friends, and kept using Facebook less afterwards. They also knew less news, and the well-being gain was small `[allcott2020 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate-strong; US, 2018]` — library record.

**Gratitude interventions**
- Cregg & Cheavens (J Happiness Studies, 2021; online 2020) meta-analysed 27 studies with 3,675 participants on depression and anxiety:
  - Post-test g = −0.29; follow-up g = −0.23. The authors call these effects "relatively modest".
  - Effects were larger against waitlist controls than against active controls. Gratitude was "not much better" than neutral activities such as writing about one's class schedule.
  - No consistent moderators were found, including symptom severity, intervention type and risk of bias.
  
  `[creggcheavens2021 · NEW · strong (meta-analysis showing small effects)]` — [Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/gratitude-interventions-dont-help-with-depression-anxiety/); [Proof Positive summary with effect sizes](https://proofpositive.org/bc-science/gratitude-interventions-effective-self%e2%80%91help-a-meta%e2%80%91analysis-of-the-impact-on-symptoms-of-depression-and-anxiety/)
- The authors add that gratitude still has value: it can improve relationships, and grateful people tend to have fewer mental-health problems. People seeking symptom relief should use treatments with stronger evidence, such as CBT. In Cregg's words, "Gratitude can't fix everything" `[creggcheavens2021 · NEW]` — [Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/gratitude-interventions-dont-help-with-depression-anxiety/)
- **Davis et al. 2016** (the earlier gratitude-intervention meta-analysis) could not be opened directly. A 2020 Dutch three-armed RCT in J Happiness Studies describes it as finding "limited and inconclusive evidence" for gratitude interventions `[gratitude_rct_nl2020 · NEW · secondary characterisation only]` — [Springer page of the RCT](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-020-00261-5)
- Diniz et al. 2023 reviewed 64 trials. Gratitude exercises gave small improvements in mood and worry, but most trials had a high risk of bias and the certainty was low `[diniz2023 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate]` — library record.

**Savouring**
- Chen et al. 2026 (Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being) meta-analysed 20 RCTs with 4,805 participants and 45 effect sizes:
  - **Overall:** g = 0.51 [0.26, 0.77].
  - **Negative emotional symptoms:** g = 0.61.
  - **Positive psychological states:** g = 0.50.
  - **Negative emotional states:** g = 0.33, not significant (p = .056).
  - **Moderators:** control-group type was not significant (Δg = −0.47, p = .068), though point estimates were larger against passive controls. There were no significant differences by delivery format, by culture or country, or by risk of bias.
  - **Publication bias:** the tests were reported as convergent.
  
  `[chen2026savor · LIB (re-opened) · strong but single and recent; effects probably inflated relative to active controls]` — [Synapse Social record of Chen et al. 2026](https://www.synapsesocial.com/papers/69b258a396eeacc4fcec880f)

### Inferences
- "Contentment" lines up with low-arousal positive feeling and with "inner harmony". Both appear to be widespread lay ideals rather than only East Asian ones, but how much weight people give calm versus excitement varies by culture and by social role. An app should treat calm and excitement as equally valid targets.
- Maximising is a plausible, measurable everyday pattern that works against contentment, through regret and comparison. It shows up in small choices such as menus, shopping and streaming, so it is observable without rating scales.
- For gratitude, the honest framing is a small, pleasant practice that may help relationships, not a mood treatment. Savouring currently has somewhat stronger trial evidence.
- What predicts lower well-being is *passive, comparative, algorithm-fed* use and displaced belonging, more than screen time as such.

### Gaps
- Fred Bryant's original savouring research and Smith et al.'s earlier savouring meta-analysis were not opened. Savouring evidence here rests on Chen 2026 plus the library.
- No meta-analysis of maximising and well-being after Schwartz 2002 was opened, so its replication across cultures is unverified here.
- Cregg & Cheavens' confidence intervals and the active-control-only estimate were not available on the pages opened.
- Davis et al. 2016's own effect sizes were not opened.
- No direct measure of "contentment" as a construct (for example peace-of-mind or harmony-in-life scales) was opened beyond Delle Fave and the Interdependent Happiness Scale.

## 3. The paradox of pursuing happiness, and pursuing connection instead

### Takeaway
In US samples, placing a very high value on happiness goes with *lower* well-being, especially when life is going well. Disappointment that one is not happy enough appears to explain it. The effect reverses in Russia and East Asia, where people who pursue happiness tend to define it socially. Across cultures, and in a preregistered German study, the pursuits that work are social ones.

### Cited Findings
- Mauss, Tamir, Anderson & Savino 2011, Study 1 (N = 59 US women who had a recent stressful life event): under low stress, valuing happiness predicted lower hedonic balance (β = −.75), lower psychological well-being (β = −.76), lower life satisfaction (β = −.60) and more depressive symptoms (β = .59). Under high stress, there were no effects `[mauss2011 · NEW · moderate-early]` — [PMC3160511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3160511/)
- Mauss 2011, Study 2 (experiment, N = 69 women, mostly students): reading an article extolling happiness, then watching a *happy* film, led to lower mood than in controls (interaction F(1,65) = 5.65, ηp² = .08). The effect did not appear after a sad film. It was fully mediated by disappointment with one's own feelings (indirect effect −.33, 95% CI −.83 to −.02). The authors flag women-only US samples and cross-sectional mediation as limits `[mauss2011 · NEW · moderate-early]` — [PMC3160511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3160511/)
- Ford et al. 2015 studied N = 786 people: US 307, Germany 91, Russia 184, and East Asia 204 (Japan 109, Taiwan 95). The correlation between motivation to pursue happiness and well-being was:
  - US: r = −.26.
  - Germany: r = −.11, not significant.
  - Russia: r = +.31.
  - East Asia: r = +.25.
  
  `[ford2015 · NEW · moderate; mostly students]` — [Ford et al. 2015, JEP: General, PMC4658246](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658246)
- In Russia and East Asia, the motivation to pursue happiness went with socially engaged definitions of happiness (r = .44 and .47). In the US and Germany it did not (r ≈ 0). Socially engaged definitions predicted higher well-being in *all four* cultures (r = .20 to .44). Ford et al. note several limits:
  - The data are correlational.
  - The motivation scale had modest reliability (α = .62).
  - Only extreme levels of motivation were measured.
  
  `[ford2015 · NEW · moderate]` — [PMC4658246](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658246)
- Rohrer, Richter, Brümmer, Wagner & Schmukle 2018 used a representative German sample (n = 1,178) in which people wrote their own ideas for becoming more satisfied. Active pursuits in general did not predict a change in life satisfaction a year later. Among the 582 who described active pursuits, those with *social* ideas, such as "spending more time with friends and family", were more satisfied a year later, partly through increased socialising. This was the preregistered hypothesis `[rohrer2018 · NEW · moderate; Germany]` — [Psychological Science abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797618761660/); [DIW record](https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.779088.de/s_10732.html)
- Telling others good news lifts mood beyond the event itself, especially when the listener responds with enthusiasm `[gable2004 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate]` — library record.
- Kind acts give a small-to-medium well-being boost across 27 studies `[curry2018 · LIB (not re-opened) · strong]` — library record.

### Inferences
- What hurts is not wanting to be happy but treating happiness as a standard that one is failing to meet, especially in good times, plus a self-focused definition of happiness. This bears directly on app copy: insights that set happiness as a target, or score a day as not happy enough, risk producing the very disappointment Mauss describes.
- "Pursue connection, not happiness" is the most transferable lesson across the US, Germany, Russia and East Asia.

### Gaps
- No direct replication of Mauss 2011 was opened, so its replication status is unknown here. Its samples were small and women-only.
- No intervention study testing whether *shifting* people from a self-focused to a socially engaged pursuit raises well-being was found within budget.

## 4. How well positive psychology interventions work, and person–activity fit

### Takeaway
Positive psychology interventions (PPIs) produce small average benefits, and how small depends on who is counting:
- **Sin & Lyubomirsky 2009:** r ≈ .29.
- **Bolier 2013** (randomised trials only): d ≈ 0.34 at post-test for subjective well-being, and about 0.22 at 3 to 6 months.
- **White, Uttl & Holder 2019**, correcting for small-study bias: r ≈ .10 for well-being, and generally non-significant for depression.
- **Carr 2021:** a broad 347-study meta-analysis, finding g ≈ 0.39 for well-being, with the largest gains in non-Western clinical groups and longer programmes.

Fit matters. People benefit more from activities they enjoy, choose themselves and put effort into. Weekly can beat more frequent practice. Extraverted and open people, and people from Western backgrounds, gained more from self-focused activities in early studies.

### Cited Findings
- Sin & Lyubomirsky 2009 drew on 49 studies of well-being and 25 of depression, and reported r = .29 and r = .31 respectively `[sinlyubomirsky2009 · NEW (via the reanalysis, not opened directly) · strong but inflated]` — [White, Uttl & Holder 2019, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216588)
- Bolier et al. 2013 meta-analysed 39 randomised trials with 6,139 participants:
  - **Post-test:** subjective well-being d = 0.34 [0.22, 0.45]; psychological well-being d = 0.20; depression d = 0.23.
  - **At 3 to 6 months:** subjective well-being d = 0.22; psychological well-being d = 0.16; depression d = 0.17, not significant.
  - **Outliers removed:** subjective well-being d = 0.26.
  - **Quality:** 20 studies were rated low, 18 medium and 1 high, and none met all six criteria.
  - **Depression subgroups:** effects were larger in individual formats (d = 0.88), in clinically referred samples (d = 1.14) and in low-quality studies.
  - **Size relative to Sin & Lyubomirsky:** effects were about 0.3 lower.
  
  `[bolier2013 · NEW · strong]` — [Bolier et al. 2013, BMC Public Health, PMC3599475](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3599475)
- White, Uttl & Holder 2019 re-analysed both meta-analyses using limit meta-analysis to correct for small-study effects:
  - Sin & Lyubomirsky well-being: r = .24 fell to .08 [0.00, 0.15].
  - Sin & Lyubomirsky depression: .25 fell to .04, not significant.
  - Bolier subjective well-being: .17 fell to .13 [0.02, 0.24].
  - Bolier psychological well-being: about .02, not significant.
  
  Their conclusion is that the effect on well-being is "small but significant (approximately r = .10)", while the effect on depression is "variable, dependent on outliers, and generally not statistically significant". They recommend that future trials use larger samples `[whiteuttl2019 · NEW · strong]` — [PLOS ONE 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216588)
- Carr et al. 2021 (J Positive Psychology) meta-analysed 347 studies with more than 72,000 participants in 41 countries. Programmes averaged about ten sessions over six weeks. Post-test effects:
  - Well-being g = 0.39; strengths 0.46; quality of life 0.48.
  - Depression −0.39; anxiety −0.62; stress −0.58.
  
  Gains were maintained at three months. People in non-Western countries with clinical problems, and those in longer programmes with several PPIs, benefited most `[carr2021 · NEW · strong; small-study correction not visible in the abstract opened]` — [Carr et al. 2021 abstract (stafforini.com)](https://stafforini.com/works/carr-2021-effectiveness-of-positive/)
- **The meta-analyses conflict.** Carr 2021 reports small-to-medium effects; White et al. 2019 report about r = .10 after bias correction. Carr includes many more studies, clinical samples and multi-component therapy programmes, which are not the brief self-help exercises that most of the earlier trials tested — [Carr 2021](https://stafforini.com/works/carr-2021-effectiveness-of-positive/); contrasted with [White et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216588)
- **Person–activity fit (Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013):** "person-activity fit" moderates effects alongside activity features (dosage, variety) and person features (motivation, effort). The proposed mediators are positive emotions, positive thoughts, positive behaviours and need satisfaction `[lyubomirskylayous2013 · NEW · moderate (review)]` — [eScholarship abstract](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/079475fr). The full text adds the following, each from small primary studies:
  - Enjoyment predicts how often people do an activity and how much they gain from it.
  - People who chose happiness exercises themselves gained more.
  - Extraverted and open people seem especially likely to benefit (Senf & Liau 2012).
  - Anglo Americans gained more from gratitude and optimism activities than Asian Americans (Boehm et al. 2011). The authors suggest, as only "suggestive" evidence, that collectivists may gain more from other-oriented activities.
  - Counting blessings **once a week beat three times a week**, and doing five kind acts in one day beat spreading them out (Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade 2005).
  - Varied kind acts beat repeated ones, and practising two to four activities at once helped most in one web study.
  - Moderately depressed people benefited more from simple pleasant activities than from reflective ones (Sin, Della Porta & Lyubomirsky 2011).
  - Effort and the belief that effort pays off predicted gains.
  
  `[lyubomirskylayous2013 · NEW · early for each fit claim]` — [Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013, full text PDF](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)
- **Eudaimonic interventions:** van Dierendonck & Lam 2023 meta-analysed interventions measured with Ryff's scales. They concluded that "eudaemonic well-being can be improved", with the strongest effects from integrated programmes built on Ryff's model. The largest gains were in environmental mastery, personal growth and self-acceptance, and "the weakest influence was on Autonomy and Positive Relations with Others" `[vandierendonck2023 · NEW · strong (meta-analysis); effect sizes not on the page opened]` — [Lingnan University repository record](https://scholars.ln.edu.hk/en/publications/interventions-to-enhance-eudaemonic-psychological-well-being-a-me/)

### Inferences
- For a self-reflection app built on brief practices, the realistic expectation is the White and Bolier range: a small boost on average, fading over months unless practice continues. It should not be the Carr range, which is driven partly by longer clinical programmes.
- Fit evidence is suggestive, not conclusive. The most defensible app behaviours are:
  - Letting people choose among practices.
  - Rotating practices for variety.
  - Not over-prescribing frequency.
  - Using light, simple practices when someone seems low, rather than reflective ones.

### Gaps
- No preregistered trial of tailoring by personality, matching practices to Big Five profiles, was opened. Senf & Liau 2012 and Boehm et al. 2011 are known only through Lyubomirsky & Layous's summary.
- Carr 2021's follow-up effect sizes and any publication-bias correction were not visible in the abstract.
- The "best possible self" exercise has no dedicated meta-analysis opened here, so it is covered only as an "optimism activity" in Boehm et al. via Lyubomirsky & Layous.

## 5. Personality and happiness, and whether lasting happiness can be raised

### Takeaway
Personality is among the strongest correlates of subjective well-being:
- **Neuroticism** is linked most strongly to negative feelings and lower satisfaction.
- **Extraversion** is linked most strongly to positive feelings and happiness.
- **Conscientiousness** matters more than is often assumed.

Personality can statistically account for about a fifth to two-fifths of the differences between people, and more for some measures. The popular "40% of your happiness is under your control" pie is an oversimplification that its own authors now disown. Happiness *can* change, through life events and sustained effort, but no one can promise how much.

### Cited Findings
- Steel, Schmidt & Shultz 2008 (Psychological Bulletin) meta-analysed 347 samples with 122,588 participants. Corrected correlations (ρ) for NEO measures:

  | Trait | Life satisfaction | Happiness | Positive affect | Negative affect | Quality of life |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Neuroticism | .45 (k = 36) | .51 | .35 | .64 (k = 73) | .72 |
  | Extraversion | .35 | .57 | .54 (k = 53) | .23 | .54 |
  | Conscientiousness | .27 | .27 | .31 | not given | .51 |
  | Agreeableness | .19 | .36 | not given | not given | not given |
  | Openness | .04 | .14 | .26 | not given | not given |

  The PDF extraction dropped minus signs. The direction is the conventional one: higher neuroticism goes with less satisfaction and more negative affect, while extraversion goes with more positive affect. This should be checked against the published table `[steel2008 · NEW · strong]` — [Steel et al. 2008 (PDF)](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2008-steel.pdf); [University of Calgary repository abstract](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/items/7e681867-4dcc-4cfd-afc2-4a78282a16bf/full)
- Variance explained by NEO personality (attenuated / disattenuated):
  - Happiness: 36% / 43%.
  - Life satisfaction: 18% / 24%.
  - Positive affect: 24% / 34%.
  - Negative affect: 30% / 42%.
  - Quality of life: 39% / 63%.
  
  Using individual measures, the relationship is "typically much larger (e.g., four times)" than earlier meta-analyses suggested. The repository abstract says personality can account for "as high as 41% or 63%" of the variance, while the full text says "39% or 63%", a post-print versus published discrepancy `[steel2008 · NEW · strong; mostly Western samples]` — [Steel et al. 2008 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2008-steel.pdf); [Calgary repository](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/items/7e681867-4dcc-4cfd-afc2-4a78282a16bf/full)
- The "40% pie" (Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade 2005) claimed 50% genes, 10% circumstances and 40% intentional activities. Brown & Rohrer (online 2019; J Happiness Studies 2020) made four main criticisms:
  - The factors interact rather than adding up.
  - Heritability may be higher; they cite estimates of 70 to 80% for the stable component.
  - Circumstances were underestimated because the evidence came from single-country demographic data.
  - The percentages describe differences *between people*, not shares of any individual's happiness.
  
  Sheldon & Lyubomirsky largely agreed. Lyubomirsky: "Like all pie charts, ours was a gross oversimplification". They note that activities may explain as little as 15% in one study, and conclude: "Happiness can be successfully pursued, but it is not 'easy'" `[brownrohrer2020 · NEW (via a secondary source) · moderate]` — [Greater Good Magazine, Feb 2020](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_much_of_your_happiness_is_under_your_control)
- **Can lasting happiness change?** Yes, at least downward and for some people. Divorce, unemployment and disability are followed by lasting average declines, and individual differences in adaptation are large (Lucas). Life events affect life satisfaction more consistently than mood (Luhmann). Intervention effects at 3 to 6 months are small, with subjective well-being d = 0.22 (Bolier) — [APS / Lucas](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/new-study-reports-on-the-state-of-human-happiness.html); [Luhmann 2012](https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.775342.de/s_7285.html); [Bolier 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3599475)
- **Trait-like dispositions beyond the Big Five:** maximising (Schwartz 2002) and strongly valuing happiness (Mauss 2011; culturally moderated per Ford 2015) both predict lower well-being in US samples. Materialist values do too (Dittmar 2014) — see Sections 1 to 3.
- **Personality as a moderator of practice benefits:** extraverted and open people appear to benefit more from positive activities (Senf & Liau 2012, via Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013) `[early]` — [Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013 PDF](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)

### Inferences
- Someone high in neuroticism is not barred from contentment, but their path likely runs through reducing negative states: steadier routines, reappraisal, calm. Chasing more peaks matters less for them. For someone high in extraversion, social activities are a natural lever. These are reasonable matches to the correlations, but they are untested as tailoring rules.
- Conscientiousness's link to quality of life (ρ = .51) suggests that structure and follow-through, such as keeping small commitments, are an under-recognised route to contentment.
- The honest message on change: some of what makes you happier than others is stable, a meaningful part responds to circumstances and habits, and no one can say how much will change for you.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of the Big Five and well-being newer than Steel 2008 (for example Anglim et al. 2020) was opened, and no cross-cultural breakdown of the personality links was opened.
- The heritability figures (70 to 80% for stable well-being) come second-hand via Greater Good. The twin studies themselves were not opened.
- Sheldon & Lyubomirsky's published 2021 revision was not opened directly.

## 6. Worldwide: the World Happiness Report, ideal emotions, interdependent happiness and eudaimonic well-being

### Takeaway
National life evaluations are explained by six factors:
- Income.
- Social support.
- Healthy life expectancy.
- Freedom.
- Generosity.
- Perceptions of corruption, used as a proxy for trust.

Nordic countries keep topping the 2026 rankings, and Costa Rica reached 4th. What a good life *feels like* differs by culture:
- **Ideal emotions:** American contexts favour excitement, while East Asian contexts value calm more.
- **Interdependent happiness:** in interdependent cultures, happiness is more relational, calm and "ordinary".
- **Pursuing happiness:** it predicts well-being positively where it is socially framed.

Eudaimonic well-being, meaning growth, purpose and mastery, can be raised by interventions.

### Cited Findings
- **WHR 2026 rankings:** Finland is first at 7.764. It is followed by Iceland (7.54), Denmark (7.539), Costa Rica (7.439, called the highest-ever ranking for a Latin American country), Sweden, Norway, the Netherlands, Israel, Luxembourg and Switzerland. Afghanistan is last of 147 at 1.446, with Sierra Leone, Malawi, Zimbabwe and Botswana just above it. The scores are three-year averages on the 0 to 10 Cantril ladder, from the Gallup World Poll in more than 140 countries. The six explanatory factors are listed as income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity and perceptions of corruption `[whr2026 · NEW · moderate (large representative surveys, correlational)]` — [Gallup: World Happiness Report 2026](https://www.gallup.com/analytics/349487/world-happiness-report.aspx)
- **Trends:** between 2006 to 2010 and 2023 to 2025, 79 of 136 countries had significant happiness gains and 41 had significant losses. Among Western industrial countries, 15 dropped significantly and 4 gained. Youth well-being fell, both absolutely and relative to adults, only in North America, Australia/New Zealand and Western Europe. Eight of ten global regions show higher youth life evaluations than in 2006 to 2010 `[whr2026 · NEW · moderate]` — [WHR 2026 executive summary](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)
- **Generations:** in European Social Survey data (30 countries, 2016 to 2024), the estimated effect of internet use on well-being was strongly negative for Gen Z, moderately negative for Millennials, near zero for Gen X and slightly positive for Baby Boomers `[whr2026 · NEW · moderate]` — [WHR 2026 executive summary](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)
- **WHR 2025:** in every world region, people who share more meals report higher well-being. People also expect lost wallets to be returned about half as often as they really are `[whr2025 · LIB (not re-opened) · moderate]` — library record.
- **Ideal affect (Tsai):** European Americans ideally want more excitement and less calm than Hong Kong Chinese. This difference is driven by goals to influence versus adjust to others, and it shifts experimentally within cultures `[tsai2007 · NEW · moderate; students in the US and Hong Kong only]` — [Tsai et al. 2007](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/tsai_miao_seppala_fung_yeung_2007.pdf)
- **Lay definitions across 12 countries on five continents:** inner harmony and relationships dominate `[dellefave2016 · NEW · moderate]` — [Delle Fave et al. 2016](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00030/text)
- **Interdependent happiness:** the Hitokoto & Uchida scale has relationship-oriented, quiescent and ordinary components, with structure supported in Malaysia, the Philippines and India `[ihs_asia_validation · NEW · early]` — [IHS validation abstract](https://unnc.globalimpact.cn/en/publications/psychometric-qualities-evaluation-of-the-interdependent-happiness/)
- **Pursuing happiness:** it goes with lower well-being in the US but higher well-being in Russia and East Asia, via socially engaged definitions `[ford2015 · NEW · moderate]` — [PMC4658246](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658246)
- **Culture and PPIs:**
  - In early work, Westerners gained more from self-focused gratitude and optimism exercises (Boehm et al. 2011) `[early]` — [Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013 PDF](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)
  - In a later meta-analysis, people in non-Western countries with clinical problems benefited *most* from PPI programmes `[carr2021 · strong]` — [Carr 2021](https://stafforini.com/works/carr-2021-effectiveness-of-positive/)
  - Savouring effects did not differ significantly by culture or country `[chen2026savor]` — [Chen 2026](https://www.synapsesocial.com/papers/69b258a396eeacc4fcec880f)
- **Prosocial spending:** linked to life satisfaction in 120 of 136 countries `[aknin2013 · LIB (not re-opened) · strong]` — library record.
- **Eudaimonic well-being (Ryff's model):** the six dimensions are autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations, purpose in life and self-acceptance. Interventions improve it, most for mastery, growth and self-acceptance, and least for autonomy and positive relations `[vandierendonck2023 · NEW · strong]` — [Lingnan University repository](https://scholars.ln.edu.hk/en/publications/interventions-to-enhance-eudaemonic-psychological-well-being-a-me/). The dimension names follow Ryff's standard model. The repository page names "Ryff's Scales of Psychological Well-being", explicitly names four of the dimensions and refers to "the six dimensions".

### Inferences
- WHR's factors map onto the individual level. Social support, freedom to make life choices, generosity and trust are all things everyday situations can surface. Income and health are context the app should acknowledge but cannot change.
- The global decline in youth well-being is a myth: it is a Western, English-speaking and Western European phenomenon. Any youth-oriented content should avoid assuming that young users everywhere are struggling.
- Cultural differences are mostly about *which* positive states people prize, and *whether* happiness is framed as personal or relational. They are not about whether relationships matter, since relationships matter everywhere.

### Gaps
- The WHR 2026 statistical chapter, with how much of the cross-country variance the six factors explain, could not be opened. The ranking table was read via Gallup's page.
- Uchida & Kitayama 2009 (happiness concepts in East and West) and Hitokoto & Uchida 2015 (the original scale paper) were not opened. Interdependent happiness rests on one validation study.
- Ryff's own reviews (PMC4241300) were blocked by a CAPTCHA on two attempts, so Ryff's health and mortality findings and her Japan–US MIDUS/MIDJA comparisons are not cited.
- Tsai's later work linking ideal-actual gaps to depression and health was not opened. The 2007 paper itself does not test well-being.
- African, South Asian and Middle Eastern evidence on what contentment means is thin in this set. The exceptions are Delle Fave (which includes India and South Africa), the IHS validation (India), WHR MENA chapter summaries and Aknin.

## 7. What this means for the app

### Takeaway
The evidence supports an app that helps people notice *what their contentment depends on*. That means noticing:
- How much it depends on people.
- Whether calm or excitement is their ideal.
- Whether they satisfice or maximise.
- How they handle comparison.
- Where their attention goes.
- Whether they get meaning as well as pleasure.

The app should do this without promising more happiness and without making happiness a target. Practices should be offered as choices with honest, modest expectations, rotated for variety, and fitted loosely to personality and culture. The fit evidence is early.

### Cited Findings
These are design implications. Each points to the evidence above.

**Everyday situations that reveal what someone's happiness depends on.** Concrete options, no scales.
- **Social versus solitary sources.** Example: "A free evening opens up. You: call a friend over / go out with a group / have a quiet night alone / get something done." This rests on the relationship findings ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you)), the finding that social pursuits work ([Rohrer 2018](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797618761660/)) and extraversion's link to positive affect ([Steel 2008](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2008-steel.pdf)).
- **Calm versus excitement as an ideal.** Example: "Your ideal Saturday morning: a slow coffee with nothing planned / something new and lively." Both answers are valid, and the insight should say so ([Tsai 2007](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/tsai_miao_seppala_fung_yeung_2007.pdf)). "Inner harmony" is a common lay definition worldwide ([Delle Fave 2016](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00030/text)).
- **Maximising versus satisficing.** Example: "Choosing something to watch tonight: pick the first decent option / browse until you find the best / let someone else pick." Maximising goes with more regret and more comparison ([Schwartz 2002](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf)).
- **Windfalls of money or time.** Example: "An unexpected small sum or free afternoon: treat someone / book an experience / buy something you've wanted / pay to get a chore off your plate." This draws on materialism ([Dittmar 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/)), giving and time purchases (library: aknin2013, whillans2017) and experiences (library: vanboven2003, weidman2016).
- **Comparison.** Example: "A friend posts big news. You: feel happy for them / feel a pang about your own life / message them / keep scrolling." This rests on the WHR 2026 findings on passive and comparative use ([WHR 2026](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)), comparison in maximisers ([Schwartz 2002](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf)) and relative income rank (library: boyce2010).
- **Savouring style.** Example: "A lovely moment, such as a sunset or a good meal: take a photo / tell someone / slow down and take it in / move on to the next thing." This draws on savouring ([Chen 2026](https://www.synapsesocial.com/papers/69b258a396eeacc4fcec880f)) and sharing good news (library: gable2004).
- **Attention.** Example: "During the commute or washing up, your mind is usually: on the task / planning tomorrow / replaying worries / on music or a podcast." Source: library killingsworth2010.
- **Meaning versus pleasure.** Example: "Given a free day: help someone with a hard task / do something purely enjoyable." These come apart in their correlates ([Baumeister 2013](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2013-meaning-vs-happiness.pdf)).
- **What a good day means.** Example: "A good day for you is mostly: things went smoothly and calmly / I got something done / I felt close to people / something exciting happened." This surfaces the harmony, relational and achievement framings ([Delle Fave 2016](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00030/text); [IHS](https://unnc.globalimpact.cn/en/publications/psychometric-qualities-evaluation-of-the-interdependent-happiness/); [Ford 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658246)).
- **After a setback.** Example: "Something goes wrong at work: talk it through with someone / think it over alone / distract yourself / get straight to fixing it." This hints at neuroticism and coping style, which matter most for negative affect ([Steel 2008](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2008-steel.pdf)).

**What an insight can honestly say**
- **Report studies of many people, not predictions.** For example: "In studies of many people, time with others is one of the most reliable companions of lasting satisfaction. Your answers this week lean social." Avoid "This will make you happier" ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you); [Rohrer 2018](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797618761660/)).
- **Say effects are modest.** For example: "Practices like this give a small lift on average; people differ a lot." This is backed by Bolier, White and Cregg & Cheavens ([White 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216588); [Bolier 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3599475)).
- **Normalise calm as a full form of happiness**, and normalise satisficing as "good enough is often better for you" ([Tsai 2007](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/tsai_miao_seppala_fung_yeung_2007.pdf); [Schwartz 2002](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf)).
- **Frame money insights around what it is for, not how much.** Use for others, for time or for experiences ([Killingsworth et al. 2023](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/03/230306163006.htm); [Dittmar 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/)).
- **Name meaning without ranking it.** Some days may feel meaningful rather than happy, and both count ([Baumeister 2013](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2013-meaning-vs-happiness.pdf)).

**Which practices fit which people.** This is early evidence, so present the options as choices, not prescriptions.
- **Savouring:** the broadest fit and the best current trial evidence, with no culture or format differences detected. It suits people who prize calm, and people whose mind often wanders or who rush past good moments ([Chen 2026](https://www.synapsesocial.com/papers/69b258a396eeacc4fcec880f)).
- **Three good things (gratitude):** pleasant and low-risk but small in effect. Offer it about weekly rather than daily, since counting blessings once a week beat three times a week ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf); [Cregg & Cheavens](https://news.osu.edu/gratitude-interventions-dont-help-with-depression-anxiety/)). For people whose answers show relational framings of a good day, offer other-oriented versions, such as noticing what someone did for you or sending thanks. Collectivist-leaning participants may gain more from other-oriented activities; this is suggestive only ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)).
- **Best possible self (optimism):** in one study, Western participants gained more than Asian American participants (Boehm et al. 2011). Offer it as one option. It may suit goal-oriented, excitement-seeking users more ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)). The suggested match to maximisers or highly neurotic users is untested, so do not tailor on it.
- **Socially engaged small steps** ("message one person", "share a good moment"): these fit nearly everyone. They have the most cross-cultural support of any step for building contentment ([Ford 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658246); [Rohrer 2018](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797618761660/); [WHR 2026 belonging finding](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)).
- **When someone's answers suggest a low patch:** favour simple pleasant activities over reflective writing ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)). The app should not imply that gratitude or savouring treats depression ([Cregg & Cheavens](https://news.osu.edu/gratitude-interventions-dont-help-with-depression-anxiety/)).
- **General rules:** let people pick, rotate practices for variety, and track enjoyment. Enjoyment and self-selection predict both uptake and benefit ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)).

**What to avoid**
- **Promising happiness or framing it as a target to hit.** In US samples, highly valuing happiness predicted lower well-being when life was going well, through disappointment ([Mauss 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3160511/)). Weekly and monthly mirrors should therefore not score "how happy you were".
- **The "40% of happiness is under your control" claim** ([Greater Good 2020](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_much_of_your_happiness_is_under_your_control)).
- **Pushing more frequent practice as better.** Over-frequent counting of blessings was less effective in one study ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)).
- **Implying excitement is better than calm**, or that one culture's happiness is the standard ([Tsai 2007](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/tsai_miao_seppala_fung_yeung_2007.pdf); [Ford 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658246)).
- **Any comparison with other users,** which the app's own rules already forbid. Upward comparison drives regret and lower satisfaction ([Schwartz 2002](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf); library: boyce2010).
- **Treating neuroticism or low baseline happiness as fixed destiny**, or as a diagnosis. Personality explains only part of the variance between people, and life events and habits shift levels ([Steel 2008](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2008-steel.pdf); [Lucas](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/new-study-reports-on-the-state-of-human-happiness.html)).
- **Becoming another passive, comparative feed.** Mirrors should prompt real-world connection rather than more app time ([WHR 2026](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)).

### Inferences
- The strongest single design principle from this research is to point people *outward*, toward connection, giving and savouring shared moments, rather than inward toward monitoring their happiness.
- Mirrors work best as descriptions of patterns ("this month, your calmest evenings followed time outdoors with someone") rather than as verdicts. That keeps them in self-reflection territory and away from implied diagnosis.
- Fit should be learned from what each person enjoys and keeps doing, not inferred rigidly from a trait profile. The trait-tailoring evidence is early.

### Gaps
- No trial was found that tests a self-reflection app built on concrete situational choices rather than rating scales. Whether such questions validly measure maximising, ideal affect or social orientation is untested; they are face-valid adaptations.
- Best-possible-self and three-good-things lack dedicated, bias-corrected meta-analyses in this set.
