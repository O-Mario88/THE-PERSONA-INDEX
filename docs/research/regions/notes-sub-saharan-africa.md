# What matters to people in Sub-Saharan Africa (as of October 2026): cravings, everyday issues, social, economic, cultural and mindset patterns

Scope note for the report writer. "Sub-Saharan Africa" (SSA) is 46 to 49 countries depending on the definition. Several continent-wide sources below also include North African countries (Egypt, Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria, Libya, sometimes Mauritania and Sudan). I mark these where it matters. Data years are given for every figure; anything from before 2023 is marked **[older data]**.

Research budget used: 23 web searches and about 41 page fetches. Some primary PDFs (the PMC systematic review, a Frontiers paper on Ubuntu, the full African Youth Survey 2024) could not be read: they were blocked or came back as binary. In those cases I used secondary summaries, and I say so each time.

---

## 1. What do people most want for their lives, and what do large surveys show about priorities, optimism and life evaluation?

### Takeaway
The most current pan-African survey (Afrobarometer Round 10, 2024/2025, 38 countries) shows that daily life is dominated by material insecurity. Health care, jobs and the cost of living are the top problems, and 79% of people went without cash income at some point in the past year. Even so, about half still expect things to improve. Well-being studies keep finding a split: Africans score low on life evaluation (the Cantril ladder, money-weighted) but comparatively high on meaning, relationships and character.

### Cited Findings
**What people say their problems are (Afrobarometer R10, fieldwork 2024/2025, 38 countries, 50,961 interviews)**
- Share naming each among their country's top three problems: health 38%, unemployment 33%, cost of living 23% (tied with education, infrastructure/roads and water supply), poverty 10%, management of the economy 8%, wages 5%. — [Afrobarometer Dispatch AD1191, June 2026](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- Lived poverty, meaning went without at least once in the past year (38-country average): cash income 79%, medical care 65%, food 58%, clean water 57%, cooking fuel 52%. — [Afrobarometer AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- Trend across 28 countries from 2014/2015 to 2024/2025: going without medical care rose from 49% to 63%, food from 45% to 57%, water from 45% to 56%, cooking fuel from 37% to 50%, cash income from 74% to 80%. All of the increase happened by 2021/2023, and levels have held steady since. — [Afrobarometer AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- 59% say the country's economy is bad (28% good). 49% say their personal living conditions are bad (33% good). 51% say the economy is worse than 12 months ago. — [Afrobarometer AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- People rate governments badly on keeping prices stable (82%), narrowing rich-poor gaps (79%), creating jobs (76%) and improving the living standards of the poor (73%). — [Afrobarometer AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)

**Optimism ("hope")**
- 49% expect economic conditions to improve over the next 12 months, 29% expect them to worsen and 15% expect no change. Optimists outnumber pessimists in 26 of 38 countries. Yet 58% say their country is going in the "wrong direction" (39% "right"). — [Afrobarometer AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- Youth priorities for government investment, in ranked order: job creation, education, job training, access to business loans. — [Afrobarometer news release, Aug 2026](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/News-release-More-than-half-of-African-youth-consider-emigration-Afrobarometer-bh-20aug26.pdf)
- African Youth Survey 2024 (Ichikowitz Family Foundation; about 500 face-to-face interviews in each of 16 countries): 7 in 10 plan to start a business, with access to capital and corruption named as the main barriers. Youth are negative about the continent's direction but expect a better life than their parents had (the summary describes this only qualitatively). — [Daily Maverick summary, Sept 2024](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2024-09-16-after-the-bell-what-young-africans-want.md)

**Migration as a craving**
- 55% of 18- to 35-year-olds in 38 countries have considered emigrating at least "a little bit." Among potential emigrants, 52% name better work opportunities as the main reason and 18% name escaping economic hardship. — [Afrobarometer news release, Aug 2026](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/News-release-More-than-half-of-African-youth-consider-emigration-Afrobarometer-bh-20aug26.pdf)
- Nuru Trust Network's executive director, quoted at the release: "The bigger question is what would make staying a genuine choice?" — [Afrobarometer article, Aug 2026](https://www.afrobarometer.org/articles/more-than-half-of-african-youth-consider-emigration-but-better-opportunities-at-home-could-make-staying-a-choice-advocates-say/)
- African Youth Survey 2024: 60% are "very" or "somewhat" likely to consider emigrating, the highest being Nigeria at 85%. Preferred destinations are France 41%, the US 37% and the UK 34%. 65% of would-be emigrants say the move would be temporary. Corruption is the top push factor in South Africa (38%) and Gabon (32%). — [Daily Maverick, Sept 2024](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2024-09-16-after-the-bell-what-young-africans-want.md)

**Life evaluation (World Happiness Report 2026, Gallup World Poll, Cantril ladder 0–10, about 1,000 respondents per country per year)**
- 41 African countries were ranked, out of 147 worldwide. Mauritius is Africa's highest at 73rd globally (score 5.939). Sierra Leone is lowest at 146th. Other SSA ranks: Mozambique 93, Gabon 96, Côte d'Ivoire 98, Cameroon 100, South Africa 101, Niger 103, Nigeria 106, Senegal 107, Namibia 108, Kenya 110, Ghana 115, Uganda 118, Ethiopia 135, Tanzania 138, DR Congo 140, Botswana 143, Zimbabwe 144, Malawi 145. — [Ecofin Agency, March 2026](https://www.ecofinagency.com/news/2503-54080-mauritius-tops-africa-s-happiness-rankings-in-2026-sierra-leone-ranks-last)
- WHR 2026 is the first to flag intensive social media use as linked to lower youth well-being worldwide. The effect depends on platform, time spent and demographics. — [Ecofin Agency, March 2026](https://www.ecofinagency.com/news/2503-54080-mauritius-tops-africa-s-happiness-rankings-in-2026-sierra-leone-ranks-last)
- According to a secondary summary of WHR 2026, Togo has climbed 20 places since being ranked the world's least happy country in 2013, with stronger social support named as a factor. Congo-Brazzaville, Niger and Togo show a "surge in life satisfaction" among younger people. — [africa.com, March 2026](https://africa.com/lifestyle/world-happiness-report-africas-youth-are-finding-reasons-to-smile)

**Flourishing beyond money (Global Flourishing Study, wave 1, 22 countries, over 200,000 participants; published 30 April 2025)**
- Five African countries took part: Nigeria, Kenya, Tanzania, South Africa and Egypt (Egypt is North African). On a flourishing score that excludes financial indicators, Nigeria ranked 5th of 22, Kenya 7th, Egypt 10th, Tanzania 11th and South Africa 13th. Nigeria's strengths were close relationships and character/virtue (forgiveness, helping others). Its weak points were finances, housing, ethnic discrimination and education. — [Victor Counted, Greater Good / The Conversation, 2026](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/what_african_countries_can_teach_everyone_about_flourishing); [AllAfrica republication](https://allafrica.com/stories/202506240003.html)
- Global findings: middle-income countries generally scored better on meaning, purpose and relationships than rich ones. In many countries 18- to 24-year-olds reported the lowest flourishing. Religious service attendance was associated with flourishing in almost every country. — [Gallup GFS press release, 30 Apr 2025](https://www.gallup.com/file/660158/Global%20Flourishing%20Study%20Press%20Release.pdf)
- Counted's 2024 study (Gallup World Poll 2020–2022, 40 African countries, 38 indicators) **[older data]**: Rwanda and Ethiopia scored strongly on social well-being (feeling respected, learning something daily) despite low economic indicators. Senegal and Ghana scored high on emotional well-being (daily enjoyment, laughter). Southern African countries showed resilience through community ties. — [AllAfrica / The Conversation](https://allafrica.com/stories/202506240003.html)

### Inferences
- The strongest "cravings" in the data are material and dignity-linked: a steady income, affordable health care, food and water security, a job that fits one's education, the chance to start a business, and for many young people the option to leave, often temporarily. "Respect" and "being able to provide" sit close behind these. The flourishing data suggest that relationships, character and meaning are where people already feel strongest. An app that reflects back those strengths, rather than only the deficits, matches how people actually rate their lives.
- Hope and frustration coexist. Pessimism about the country (58% "wrong direction") sits next to personal optimism (49% expect improvement). The app should not treat optimism as naïveté, or frustration as a mental-health symptom.
- Life-evaluation ranks (WHR) and Afrobarometer data do not line up neatly. For example, Mozambique ranks 4th in Africa on WHR 2026 while 61% of Mozambicans report looking for work. Any single index is a poor guide to how a given user feels.

### Gaps
- I did not reach World Values Survey wave 7 data for SSA countries (fieldwork about 2017–2022, covering Nigeria, Kenya, Ethiopia, Zimbabwe and others). The WVS "materialist vs post-materialist" and "survival vs self-expression" placements are therefore not documented here.
- I did not get country-level GFS dimension scores (meaning, health, character) for Kenya, Tanzania or South Africa, only ranks. The GFS press release does not give African age patterns, and the claim that the youngest flourish least is a global pattern not verified for each African country. The brief names *Nature Mental Health* as the journal; the Gallup press release does not name it.
- Full African Youth Survey 2024 report figures on identity, trust, religion and mental health were not retrieved; only a newspaper summary was.

## 2. Social life: kin obligations ("black tax"), community, elders, marriage and bride price, gender roles, religion, Ubuntu

### Takeaway
Social life is strongly relational and reciprocal. Families are the first safety net: 47% asked family for help to make ends meet in the past year. Earners face real, measurable pressure to share income, which shapes career and business choices and can push people to hide money. Religion is near-universal and religious leaders are more trusted than politicians. Bride-price customs persist but are being renegotiated under cost pressure. Gender attitudes are broadly egalitarian in principle, with large practical gaps and wide variation between countries.

### Cited Findings
**Kin obligation and reciprocity**
- In the past year, 47% asked family for help to make ends meet: highest in Namibia (65%), Guinea and Gabon (59%), lowest in Seychelles (20%) and Mauritius (22%). 33% asked friends or neighbours (Namibia 51%, Zimbabwe and Madagascar 50%). 12% asked religious or community groups (Kenya 23%, Liberia 21%, Angola 20%). — [Afrobarometer AD1191, June 2026](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- "Black tax" (South Africa) is defined as "a socio-cultural expectation to support extended family financially." In a 2024 study of young Black professionals, many put financial stability ahead of career fulfilment, which led to skill mismatches, slower professional growth and "significant psychological strain." "Cultural duty often overshadowed personal goals." People from lower-income backgrounds sacrificed earlier. Senior professionals reported aligning work with passion only after they had reached financial stability. — [Moleleki, GIBS/University of Pretoria MBA dissertation, June 2024](https://repository.up.ac.za/items/260b83b3-c498-40e0-bcaa-f37c203fe503/full)
- Kenya, "kinship tax": 93% of surveyed Kenyans believed business success brings financial demands from friends and family. About a third of entrepreneurs faced pressure to share. About one in six would give up as much as 70% of their earnings to keep that income secret. Kin pressure cut firm productivity by about a quarter. The most productive entrepreneurs faced the highest rates, and some kept their businesses small on purpose to avoid attention. One vendor hid her earnings so relatives would think she had no money. — [Munir Squires, The Conversation via The African Mirror, July 2024 (study DOI 10.1093/ej/ueae025)](https://theafricanmirror.africa/news/kinship-tax-puts-the-brakes-on-business-kenyan-study-measures-the-impact-of-helping-family/)
- In an earlier working-paper version (1,805 rural Kenyans) **[older data, 2018]**, about one-fifth of women and one-third of men were willing to pay to hide income. Rates were higher for men, the more educated and entrepreneurs. The author's model also found that the *informal insurance* value of kin sharing outweighs its efficiency costs, so sharing is unlikely to disappear while state support is thin. — [VoxDev/PEDL summary of Squires 2018](https://voxdev.org/node/62811)

**Ubuntu and relational personhood**
- Ubuntu is described as a moral framework from African, "primarily Bantu-speaking," traditions, summarised as "a person is a person through other persons." On this view personhood is realised through relationships. Empathy and solidarity affirm one's humanity; indifference or exclusion diminish it. Service to the community is a condition of flourishing. Critics note that Ubuntu is a lived, pragmatic ethic rather than a formal theory. — [Hofmeyr, Acta Academica 57(1), 2025](https://www.scielo.org.za/pdf/aa/v57n1/03.pdf)

**Marriage and bride price**
- South Africa, January 2026: families ask for lobola of roughly R15,000 to R90,000. Couples are delaying negotiations and spending the money on housing or household basics instead. Families have become "more open to negotiation" in the cash-tight January, after December spending and with school fees and debts falling due. Paying lobola is still widely seen as a sign of respect and commitment ("We want to do things properly"). — [Scrolla via AllAfrica, Jan 2026](https://allafrica.com/stories/202601160009.html)

**Gender roles**
- Afrobarometer R9 (2021/2023, 39 countries) **[older data, but the latest full gender dispatch]**:
  - 73% back equal land and inheritance rights for women, ranging from 31% in Mauritania to 92% in Cabo Verde.
  - 58% reject giving men priority for jobs when jobs are scarce (women 66%, men 50%), with lows in Madagascar (32%) and Niger (35%).
  - 75% back women's equal chance to be elected (no generation gap), and 52% expect a woman candidate to face harassment.
  - Women make household financial decisions alone 35% of the time, men 44%.
  - 38% say gender-based violence is common in their community, ranging from 62% in Angola and 57% in Namibia to 19% in Tanzania. GBV is the top women's-rights priority (31%).
  - Gender gaps: women hold bank accounts at 34% vs men 43%, and reach secondary education at 51% vs 59%.
  
  — [Afrobarometer AD749, Dec 2023](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/AD749-PAP6-Africans-back-gender-equality-but-gaps-persist-Afrobarometer-20dec23.pdf)
- Afrobarometer R10 (2024/2025, 38 countries):
  - 57% support equal employment rights and 38% say men should get priority when jobs are scarce.
  - Nearly a quarter say husbands or families frequently prevent women from taking paid work.
  - More than one in four say schoolgirls often face harassment or demands for sexual favours from teachers.
  - Two-thirds of women say they or a family member went without needed medicine in the past year.
  - 78% say police and courts must do more to protect women and girls.
  
  — [Further Africa summary of Afrobarometer, June 2026](https://furtherafrica.com/2026/06/22/afrobarometer-survey-shows-strong-support-for-womens-rights-across-africa/)

**Religion in daily life**
- 95% identify with a religion: 56% Christian, 34% Muslim, 5% other, 4% none. 30% are leaders or active members of religious groups that meet outside worship (Liberia 58%, Kenya 56%, Zambia 55%). 43% contacted a religious leader in the past year (Kenya 71%, Uganda 63%). 69% trust religious leaders, compared with 52% for the president and 43% for MPs (Senegal 91%, Uganda 90%, South Africa only 49%). 87% would welcome or not mind neighbours of another religion. Younger and urban people are slightly less active. **[older data: Afrobarometer R7, 2016/2018, 34 countries]** — [Afrobarometer Dispatch 339, Jan 2020](https://afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/migrated/files/publications/Policy%20papers/ab_r7_dispatchno339_pap12_religion_in_africa.pdf)
- Pew (19 SSA countries, over 25,000 interviews in more than 60 languages) **[older data, published 2010]**: religion is "very important" to more than three-quarters of people in 17 of 19 countries (Senegal 98%, Mali 93%, Botswana lowest at 69%, vs 57% in the US). More than half in Tanzania, Mali, Senegal and South Africa believed sacrifices to ancestors or spirits can protect from harm. Indigenous beliefs are often blended into Christianity and Islam, and Pentecostal/prosperity-gospel Christianity was spreading fast. — [Baptist Standard summary of Pew 2010](https://baptiststandard.com/news/faith-culture/survey-finds-africa-is-most-religious-part-of-world)

### Inferences
- Family obligation runs both ways. People are asked for help, and they ask for help: 47% asked family. "Black tax" is therefore not simply a burden to "set boundaries" against. It is part of an insurance system that people value, and it carries pride, duty and status as well as strain. An insight card that frames supporting relatives only as a drain will feel alien. One that names both the love and the cost will ring true.
- The finding that people pay to *hide* income is a strong signal that a **private** space to think through money and obligation is genuinely valuable. Money prompts should never nudge disclosure to others.
- Ubuntu is a Southern and East African (Bantu-language) frame. Using the word for Sahelian, Horn of Africa or many West African users would itself be a stereotype. Relational personhood shows up across the region, but under other names and traditions.
- Religion is safe to acknowledge as a *practice* (a service, prayer, fasting, a group meeting, a pastor's or imam's visit) without asking *which* religion, which fits the app's rule. Trust in religious leaders is lower in South Africa and some Central African countries, so faith-centred framing should be offered, not assumed.

### Gaps
- No pan-African quantitative data were found on how much of their income earners send to kin, how many people feel strained versus proud, or "black tax" outside South Africa. The African Youth Survey summary had nothing on family.
- No recent data on bride price outside South Africa (for example Uganda, Zimbabwe, Nigeria, Ethiopia), on marriage age trends, or on elders' authority. I also found no recent sourced data on polygamy, on cohabitation without bride price, or on how young urban couples handle marriage negotiations.
- Afrobarometer religion data are from 2016/2018 and Pew from 2010. No 2024–2026 measure of religion's importance was retrieved.

## 3. Economic life: informal work, youth (under)employment, side hustles, mobile money, savings groups, cost of living, remittances, land, debt

### Takeaway
Work is overwhelmingly informal (nearly 9 in 10 workers) and often poorly paid (about 6 in 10 workers live below US$4.20 a day). A third of adults, and 43% of young people, describe themselves as looking for work. Money moves on phones: SSA handled $1.4 trillion, or 66% of global mobile money value, in 2025. Rotating savings groups and remittances are central household tools. Costs and prices are the most widely felt grievance.

### Cited Findings
- **ILO, 2026 report:** about nine in ten SSA workers are in informal employment, and close to six in ten live in households below US$4.20 a day (PPP). In 2025 the labour force grew by 15.4 million while 14.6 million jobs were created. 46% of 15- to 29-year-olds have completed only primary or lower secondary school as their highest level, and only 6.5% have completed technical or vocational training. — [Nairametrics on ILO Employment and Social Trends 2026, Jan 2026](https://nairametrics.com/2026/01/16/sub-saharan-africa-labour-force-grew-by-15-4-million-in-2025-ilo-report/)
- **Afrobarometer R10, self-reported work status (ages 18–65):** 35% unemployed and looking, 22% full-time, 11% part-time, 32% outside the labour force. Young people looking for work: 43%, vs 17–33% of older groups. Highest job-seeking: Mozambique 61%, Angola 56%, Lesotho and Botswana 55%. Lowest: Seychelles 4%, Mauritius 6%. — [Afrobarometer AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- **Africa Youth Employment Outlook 2026:** youth unemployment is consistently above adult unemployment. In SSA, open unemployment "may appear lower" because informal and subsistence work counts as employment. Young women are more likely to be NEET (not in employment, education or training). — [The Star (Kenya), Feb 2026](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/africa/2026-02-11-report-africas-youth-jobs-crisis-soars-as-millions-join-workforce-with-few-jobs-on-offer)
- **South Africa:** youth unemployment is cited at 57% (secondary source; definition and age band not stated). — [africa.com, March 2026](https://africa.com/lifestyle/world-happiness-report-africas-youth-are-finding-reasons-to-smile)
- **Mobile money (GSMA State of the Industry 2026):** SSA processed $1.4 trillion in 2025, 66% of the global value of more than $2 trillion. The figure doubled between 2021 and 2025. East Africa processed $806bn and West Africa $498bn. East Africa produced almost half of all new active accounts globally. Live services: West Africa 76, East Africa 62, Central Africa 20. There are 30 million registered agents, and agent cash-in/cash-out totalled $430bn. About 75% of accounts are *not* used in a given month. Account ownership has a wide gender gap in 7 of 10 surveyed countries. Credit is the most common add-on service, with savings close behind. — [Connecting Africa on GSMA SOTIR 2026, March 2026](https://www.connectingafrica.com/mobile-money/-1-4t-flowed-through-mobile-money-in-sub-saharan-africa-in-2025-gsma)
- **Savings groups:** "more than 11 million South Africans are believed to be members of stokvels" (NASASA chair; an estimate). Stokvels are used for weddings, funerals, business and property, and young people are said to be joining "in droves." — [EWN, Aug 2025](https://www.ewn.co.za/2025/08/14/more-than-11-million-south-africans-are-members-of-stokvels-nasasa)
- **Remittances, 2025 (Ecofin data):** all of Africa (including North Africa) received $124.2bn. Nigeria $22.78bn, Ethiopia $7.13bn, Kenya $5bn. As a share of GDP: Gambia 22%; Liberia, Comoros and Lesotho 21% each. Sending $200 costs on average 7.2%, about 20% less than a decade ago. — [africa.com/Ecofin, Sept 2026](https://africa.com/top-10-news/egypt-nigeria-lead-africas-2025-remittance-inflows)
- In 2023 Nigeria received $19.5bn, reported as 35% of SSA's remittances **[older data; headline figure attributed to the World Bank]**. — [AllAfrica/ThisDay, July 2024](https://allafrica.com/stories/202407010180.html)
- **Prices:** cost of living is a top-three problem for 23%. 82% say government handles price stability badly, and Nigeria is the most negative at 97%. — [Afrobarometer AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- **Kin pressure and business:** very productive entrepreneurs keep their businesses small to avoid relatives' attention, and cash windfalls under heavy kin pressure are not reinvested. — [Squires via The African Mirror, 2024](https://theafricanmirror.africa/news/kinship-tax-puts-the-brakes-on-business-kenyan-study-measures-the-impact-of-helping-family/)

### Inferences
- "Unemployed" in Africa rarely means idle. With about 90% informal work and many people "outside the labour force," the realistic picture is a mix of several small income streams, irregular cash and waiting for opportunities. The app should ask about *income days*, *slow days*, *a customer who didn't pay*, *a hustle that worked*, not "your job" or "your manager" as the default.
- Irregular cash plus the large number of mobile money accounts that go unused in a given month suggest people keep a lot of cash and run stop-start balances. Monthly-budget framing (paycheck, rent on the 1st) fits salaried urban users but not most users.
- Savings groups (stokvel, chama, susu, tontine, esusu; local names vary) are social as much as financial. A missed contribution, a payout turn or a group dispute are everyday moments with real emotional weight.
- The "January squeeze" (school fees, debts after December, lobola delays) is a sourced seasonal stress point, at least in South Africa. The app could time gentle check-ins around it.

### Gaps
- No ILO SSA-wide unemployment, youth unemployment or NEET *rates* were retrieved. The Afrobarometer "looking for work" figure (35%) uses a broader, self-reported definition than the ILO and must not be compared with it directly.
- No sourced data were retrieved on chama or susu membership outside South Africa, on digital lending or over-indebtedness (for example Kenyan mobile overdrafts, Nigerian loan-app harassment), on land tenure disputes, or on salary arrears among civil servants. These are plausible everyday issues but not verified here.
- No sourced data were found on side-hustle prevalence, or on how many salaried workers also run a business.

## 4. Mindset: stress, mental-health stigma, resilience, faith as coping, youth optimism vs frustration, attitudes to self-improvement

### Takeaway
Distress is common and services are very scarce. Many families first read mental distress through spiritual or moral frames and turn to religious leaders and family before clinics. Faith is a major source of meaning and coping. Young people combine entrepreneurial ambition and personal hope with deep anger at governance, shown in the 2024–2025 youth protest wave (Madagascar's government was dissolved in 2025). Men are least likely to seek help and most likely to die by suicide (South African data).

### Cited Findings
- **Burden and workforce:** WHO data put nearly 150 million people in Africa living with mental health conditions in 2025. There are about 1.4 mental health workers per 100,000 people, against a global average of about 9 (2025 UNICEF regional analysis). Both figures are relayed secondhand by Capital FM, without links to the original reports. — [Capital FM, May 2026](https://capitalfm.africa/shortage-of-mental-health-specialists-puts-pressure-on-african-economies/)
- **Psychiatrist numbers:** South Africa has roughly 1.5 psychiatrists per 100,000 people, and many African countries have fewer than one per 500,000. The source cites nothing for these figures. Most governments spend less than 1% of the health budget on mental health. — [Daily Maverick, Oct 2025](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2025-10-09-africas-mental-health-crisis-demands-urgent-action-as-populations-surge-and-resources-dwindle.md)
- **Treatment gap:** fewer than 1 in 10 people in SSA who need mental health care receive evidence-based care. The authors attribute this to limited reach of services rather than unwillingness to seek help. — [Adewuya et al., Cambridge Prisms: Global Mental Health, June 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4257506383356AA5F8FDAD588F631102)
- **Spiritual explanations (Nigeria, Lagos religious leaders, CLERIC study, 2026):** spiritual causation was the most strongly endorsed explanation of mental illness and biological causation the least. Most leaders held *pluralistic* views. Three types emerged: spiritually oriented and restrictive (38.2%), pluralistic and benevolent (35.7%), and biologically oriented (26.1%). Training predicted more supportive attitudes. About 26% of people with mental illness consult a religious leader before any formal service (figure cited from earlier Nigerian studies). — [Adewuya et al. 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4257506383356AA5F8FDAD588F631102)
- **Ethiopia, holy water priests (2024):** priests attributed mental illness to possession, evil spirits, the evil eye, curses or witchcraft, but also to khat or drugs, HIV, poverty, trauma and migration harm. Most combined prayer and holy water with biomedical care. Families often resisted clinical referral, seeing distress as a spiritual deficit. Holy water sites were a refuge "without alienation." Some patients arrived in shackles. — [Baheretibeb et al., Transcultural Psychiatry, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10943614)
- **Task-sharing:** lay-worker models such as Zimbabwe's Friendship Bench, with similar programmes in South Africa and Uganda, are recommended as scalable. People with mental illness are still chained in homes or faith institutions in some places. — [Daily Maverick, Oct 2025](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2025-10-09-africas-mental-health-crisis-demands-urgent-action-as-populations-surge-and-resources-dwindle.md)
- **Who calls SADAG:** young people are SADAG's largest group of callers, citing unemployment, poverty, crime and violence. Callers present overlapping stressors: cost of living, unemployment and load-shedding. Men are less likely to seek help but more are now calling. — [TimesLIVE / Sunday Times, June 2026](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/south-africa/2026-06-08-our-phone-bill-alone-is-r150000-a-month-how-sadag-keeps-sas-only-suicide-crisis-helpline-running/)
- **Suicide in South Africa:** men account for nearly 80% of recorded suicides. Suicide is the second leading cause of death among 15- to 29-year-olds (Minister Chikunga). The national rate is about 23.5 per 100,000 (Minister) or 21.1 (WHO, cited as fourth-highest in Africa), and is probably underreported. — [TimesLIVE / Sunday Times, June 2026](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/south-africa/2026-06-08-our-phone-bill-alone-is-r150000-a-month-how-sadag-keeps-sas-only-suicide-crisis-helpline-running/)
- **Faith and flourishing:** religious service attendance is associated with flourishing across almost all GFS countries. — [Gallup GFS press release, Apr 2025](https://www.gallup.com/file/660158/Global%20Flourishing%20Study%20Press%20Release.pdf). Nigeria's GFS strengths were forgiveness and helping others. — [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/what_african_countries_can_teach_everyone_about_flourishing)
- **Youth frustration (Afrobarometer):** 18- to 35-year-olds are the most likely to say their country is going in the wrong direction (59%). Pessimism climbs with deprivation: 67% of the most deprived say "wrong direction," vs 44% of the least deprived. — [Afrobarometer AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- **Protests:** Madagascar, September 2025: youth protests over water and electricity cuts lasting more than 12 hours led President Rajoelina to dissolve the government. The UN counted at least 22 deaths. The median age is 19.2. Kenya saw decentralised, online-organised protests against tax policy in 2025. Morocco's (North Africa) "GenZ 212" organised via TikTok, Instagram and Discord. — [Vision of Humanity, Oct 2025](https://www.visionofhumanity.org/the-rise-and-spread-of-gen-z-protests/)

### Inferences
- Spiritual and biomedical explanations are usually held *together*, not as either/or ("Trust in God, but tie your donkey," as one Ethiopian paper's title puts it). The app should not position itself against faith, and it should not use clinical labels ("depression," "anxiety disorder"). Plain, experience-near words such as heavy, tired, worried, carrying a lot, "my spirit is low" will travel better and also respect the app's no-diagnosis rule.
- Resilience language must not romanticise endurance. Southern African data show high male suicide and low male help-seeking. A "you're so strong" frame can reinforce the silence. Small, private, non-clinical prompts may suit men who will not call a helpline.
- Self-improvement and "hustle" framing likely resonates with the 7 in 10 who plan to start a business, and with the prosperity-gospel strand of Pentecostalism. It turns harmful when it implies that personal effort alone fixes structural problems like no cash income, no electricity or no jobs. Insight cards should separate "what is yours to move" from "what is not your fault." (This is an inference; I found no study of African attitudes to self-help content.)
- Youth anger is political and collective. A reflection app should not reframe it as a personal emotional-regulation problem. It also must never ask about party or protest participation.

### Gaps
- No pan-African prevalence data for depression or anxiety, and no study of attitudes to self-improvement or wellness apps in Africa, were retrieved.
- The systematic review on care pathways in SSA (PMC12173185) was blocked by a CAPTCHA, so proportions of first contact with traditional healers vs clinics across countries are not documented beyond the Nigerian ~26% figure.
- No sourced data on how people *talk* about stress in local languages (idioms of distress such as "thinking too much," reported in several African settings in the wider literature). Not verified here.

## 5. Diversity inside the region: West, East, Central and Southern Africa; urban/rural; generations; within-country variation

### Takeaway
Variation between countries is very large on almost every measure: deprivation, optimism, religiosity, gender attitudes, mobile money use and unemployment. A "region-wide" figure often hides a range of 30 to 90 percentage points. Urban/rural gaps on outlook are small in Afrobarometer, but deprivation and age split opinions sharply.

### Inferences
**Where a region-wide assumption would go wrong:**
1. Assuming everyone is Christian, or that "church" is the default. About a third of people are Muslim, with Muslim majorities across the Sahel, Senegal and much of the Horn and coastal East Africa.
2. Assuming "Ubuntu" applies everywhere.
3. Assuming mobile money is equally ubiquitous. It is strongest in East Africa and thin in Central Africa.
4. Assuming English. Francophone and Lusophone countries plus Swahili, Hausa, Amharic, Yoruba, isiZulu and others matter. The app's copy needs localisation, not just translation.
5. Assuming load-shedding is universal. It is a South African word. Other countries have different words for power cuts, and different experiences of them.
6. Assuming pessimism. Mali, Gabon, Senegal and Tanzania are net optimistic.
7. Assuming the poorest are the least happy. Rankings scramble once relationships and meaning are counted.

**Within-country:** deprivation level and age are better predictors of outlook than urban/rural location or gender. The app should adapt to a person's *situation* (cash flow, caring load, work type) rather than to a presumed national or regional culture.

### Gaps
- No sourced within-country regional data, for example northern vs southern Nigeria, coastal vs inland Kenya, or the Anglophone regions of Cameroon. No ethnic breakdowns were sought, deliberately, given the app's rules, but this means within-country cultural variation is under-documented here.
- No 2024–2026 sourced detail on conflict-affected populations (eastern DRC, Sudan, the Sahel insurgencies, Ethiopia's Amhara and Tigray regions). Displacement and insecurity would change "everyday moments" fundamentally for these users.
- Gabon's and Mali's outlier optimism is reported, but I did not source its causes (likely political transitions; this is unverified).

## 6. Everyday moments that ring true, and Western defaults to avoid

### Takeaway
The truest prompts are about small, concrete, relational and money-and-time moments. Examples: a request from a relative, a group contribution, a delay in cash, a power or water cut, a religious obligation, January bills, a choice between a calling and a paycheck. Assumptions to avoid are individual autonomy, salaried stability, therapy access, constant power and data, secular framing and a nuclear household.

### Cited Findings (grounding for moments)
- Relatives' requests and hiding income: [Squires/The African Mirror 2024](https://theafricanmirror.africa/news/kinship-tax-puts-the-brakes-on-business-kenyan-study-measures-the-impact-of-helping-family/). Asking family for help (47%): [AD1191](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)

- Stokvel contributions and payouts for funerals and weddings: [EWN 2025](https://www.ewn.co.za/2025/08/14/more-than-11-million-south-africans-are-members-of-stokvels-nasasa)
- Load-shedding as a stressor (South Africa): [TimesLIVE 2026](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/south-africa/2026-06-08-our-phone-bill-alone-is-r150000-a-month-how-sadag-keeps-sas-only-suicide-crisis-helpline-running/). ### Inferences
**Candidate daily-question moments.** Each is grounded in the sources above. Items marked * are plausible but not directly sourced in this research and should be validated with local testers.

**Kin and community**
- "Someone in the family asked you for money today. What did you feel before you answered?"
- "Did you say yes to something because it was expected of you?"
- "Who did you help this week, and who helped you?" This makes reciprocity visible both ways.
- "A family group chat got busy or tense."*
- "You attended, or chose not to attend, a funeral, wedding or naming ceremony and the contribution that goes with it."*

**Money and work**
- "Today was a slow day, or a good day, for income."
- "You put money into your group, or it was your turn to receive."
- "You kept some money aside that nobody knows about. How does that sit with you?" Gentle, non-judgemental, private.
- "A payment you were waiting for didn't come." This could be a salary, a customer or a transfer.*
- "You chose between something you love and something that pays."

**Infrastructure**
- "The power or water went off and changed your plans." Use local words: load-shedding in South Africa, and the local term for power cuts in Ghana, Nigeria and elsewhere.*

**Faith and meaning**
- "A prayer, a service or a quiet moment of faith today. Did it lighten anything?" Keep this optional and never ask which faith.
- "Something you did today that you're proud of as a person: forgiving, helping, keeping your word." This matches the GFS strengths of character and relationships.

**Futures**
- "You thought about leaving: town, country, job. What were you looking for?"
- "One small step on the business or plan you've been carrying."

**Seasonal**
- January: school fees and the bills after December.
- Religious fasting and feast periods.* Offer prompts for these, but never assume or ask about them.

**Western defaults to avoid**
1. "Set boundaries with toxic family" as default advice. Kin sharing is insurance and identity. Offer "what can you give without breaking yourself?" instead.
2. Assuming a monthly salary, a "boss" or "your job." About 90% of work is informal.
3. Assuming therapy access. There are about 1.4 mental health workers per 100,000 people. Never make "talk to a therapist" the only step offered.
4. Assuming steady power, data and phone charge. Daily prompts need to tolerate missed days without streak shame.
5. Assuming a secular framing, or alternatively a Christian one.
6. Assuming the self is purely individual. Insight cards should allow "we" framings: household, family, group.
7. Assuming dating-app romance norms. Marriage involves families and negotiation (lobola and other bride-price customs).
8. Assuming "being alone" or "me-time" is the cure for stress. Solitude may read as isolation.
9. Clinical vocabulary. This also conflicts with the app's no-diagnosis rule.
10. Treating political frustration as an individual mood problem, or prompting about protests or parties.
11. Assuming a private phone. Shared or borrowed phones may be common, which would make lock or privacy options important.*
12. Assuming US-style emergency numbers. Ghana's emergency number is 193, for example. — [findahelpline](https://findahelpline.com/countries/gh)

### Gaps
- No sourced 2024–2026 data on WhatsApp penetration, family group-chat dynamics, mobile data costs or shared-phone prevalence in SSA.
- No sourced data on salary arrears, landlord or rent pressure, school-fee stress outside South Africa, or funeral-contribution burdens.
- Localised words for power cuts and for distress idioms were not researched.

## 7. Help-seeking and crisis: support people turn to, helpline availability, pointing to help respectfully

### Takeaway
People turn first to family (47%), friends and neighbours (33%) and religious or community figures (12% for material help; religious leaders are de facto mental-health gatekeepers). Formal crisis lines exist in many countries but are underfunded and unevenly verified. In some countries, attempting suicide has been, or still is, a criminal offence. Kenya's colonial-era law was struck down by its High Court in January 2025, but repeal was still pending in parliament as of late 2025. Any crisis flow must be careful, private and locally verified.

### Cited Findings
- **Where people turn for help to make ends meet:** family 47%, friends and neighbours 33%, religious or community groups 12% (Kenya 23%). — [Afrobarometer AD1191, 2026](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AD1191-PAP13-Africans-experience-economic-hardship-despite-signs-of-recovery-Afrobarometer-6june26.pdf)
- **Religious leaders as gatekeepers:** they are "de facto mental health gatekeepers" in Nigeria. Families often turn to them first for spiritual support, counselling and crisis management, and training shifts their attitudes toward supportive ones. — [Adewuya et al. 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4257506383356AA5F8FDAD588F631102)
- **Faith healers and clinics (Ethiopia):** priests who collaborate with clinics value being treated as equals and want two-way referral. Trust and respect matter more than shared beliefs. — [Baheretibeb et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10943614)
- **Criminal law on attempted suicide:** in January 2025 Kenya's High Court declared Section 226 of the Penal Code, which made attempted suicide a misdemeanour, unconstitutional. A repeal bill was awaiting its Third Reading, then the Senate and presidential assent, as of December 2025. ### Inferences
**Pointing to help respectfully**
- Offer a ladder, not a single door: (1) someone you trust (family, friend, elder, faith leader, *if they feel safe to you*); (2) a community or lay counsellor; (3) a verified free helpline for the user's country; (4) emergency services. This mirrors real pathways and does not dismiss faith-based support. It also avoids telling people to rely on leaders who may hold "restrictive" spiritual views. The Nigerian study classed 38% of leaders as spiritually oriented and restrictive.
- Because attempted suicide was criminal in Kenya until the 2025 ruling (repeal still pending), and may still be criminal elsewhere, crisis copy should never imply that a disclosure will be reported, and the app should never auto-share. Messages should stress that helplines are confidential.
- Helpline numbers in SSA change and lines run on thin funding. The app should source numbers from a maintained, verified directory (for example findahelpline/Throughline), show the number with its last-verified date, and fall back to emergency services. It should not hard-code a list.
- Men's low help-seeking, plus the male share of suicides (South African data), argues for discreet, low-friction options, such as a number shown without a call to action, or text-based options where they exist.

### Gaps
- **Countries where attempted suicide is still criminal:** I did not confirm a current list for SSA. Background knowledge suggests several, possibly including Nigeria, Uganda and Tanzania, but this is unverified here and must be checked before launch. Ghana's repeal year was not given in the fetched source.
- **SADAG's phone numbers and WhatsApp/SMS channels:** not given in the fetched article. South Africa, Tanzania, Zimbabwe and Namibia had no entries in the Intellect directory. Before shipping, verify every number per country, along with its hours and languages.
- **Proportions of first contact:** no SSA-wide data on the share of people who first contact family, religious leaders, traditional healers or clinics for *emotional* distress, beyond Nigeria's ~26% religious-leader figure. The relevant systematic review could not be accessed.
