# Tailoring without stereotyping: cross-cutting evidence for a global self-reflection app (as of October 2026)

Scope note: these notes cover cross-cutting questions only (variance within vs between groups, cultural adaptation evidence, WEIRD research bias, diaspora/minority experience, region detection and privacy, identity-based personalisation, helpline routing). Regional content is left to the regional researchers. Data older than 2024 is marked **[older: year]**. Budget used: about 21 searches and 40 fetches. Several primary pages (nature.com, PMC, the University of Melbourne helpline survey) blocked automated fetching; where a secondary source stands in for a primary one, the finding says so.

## 1. How much variation lies within countries/groups versus between them, and what that implies for tailoring by region

### Takeaway
Most of the differences between people in values, personality and attitudes lie *within* countries, not between them. Country explains roughly 2–12% of individual differences in values. Countries and world regions together explain about 11% of variance across personality, attitude, value and norm scales. Region-level cultural models, including UN world regions, explain only about 5–7% of the variation in research findings. A user's region is a weak predictor of what they value or what bothers them. It is a better guide to their *context*: institutions, economy, language, calendar and services.

### Cited Findings
- Fischer & Schwartz (2011, *J. Cross-Cultural Psychology* 42(7):1127–1144, DOI 10.1177/0022022110381429) analysed three datasets: Schwartz Value Survey (67 countries, N = 41,968), Portrait Values Questionnaire (19 European countries, representative, N = 42,359) and World Values Survey (62 countries, representative, N = 84,887). They found "more consensus than disagreement" on value priorities across countries, which challenges the idea that culture determines values. **[older: 2011]** — [HUJI CRIS record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/whence-differences-in-value-priorities-individual-cultural-or-art/)
- In the same paper, values tied to **autonomy, relatedness and competence** were rated highly important nearly everywhere and showed high cross-national consensus. Only **conformity** values looked like good markers of culture as a shared meaning system. — [HUJI CRIS record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/whence-differences-in-value-priorities-individual-cultural-or-art/)
- A later paper summarises Fischer & Schwartz as: "Country of origin explains on average only 2–12% of inter-individual variance." Hanel et al. (2018) add that for how values are *instantiated* (applied to concrete situations), "within-country variability outweighs the between-country variability." **[older: 2018]** — [Hanel et al. 2018, Frontiers in Psychology 9:849](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00849/pdf)
- Stankov (2011) split 38 subscales covering personality, social attitudes, values and social norms into individual, country (45 countries) and world-region (9 "societal clusters") components. Individual variability accounted for **67.69% to 96.43%** of variance. Country and region together averaged about **11%**. Cultural effects were *smaller* for personality and values than for social attitudes and norms. A "conservatism" composite was the exception, with about **25%** of variance cultural (Europeans more liberal). Caveat: the sample was TOEFL test-takers (N = 1,600) plus US students (N = 429), not population-representative. **[older: 2011]** — [Stankov 2011, Learning and Individual Differences](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/q4597/individual-country-and-societal-cluster-differences-on-measures-of-personality-attitudes-values-and-social-norms)
- Field et al. (2021, *J. International Business Studies* 52:1797–1817) ran 25,296 multilevel meta-analyses on the metaBUS database (>1,000,000 findings). Six cultural models (Hofstede, GLOBE practices and values, Schwartz, Ronen & Shenkar clusters, **UN M49 world regions**) each explained only about **5–7%** of the variance in findings after artifact correction, and the models performed similarly. Culture shifted observed effect sizes by roughly |r| = .05–.07. — [Field et al. 2021 abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/369762)
- A *Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology* article (author not listed on the indexing page; widely attributed to Schwartz) cites Fischer & Schwartz to say "values vary much more within countries than between countries." It proposes treating culture as a latent normative system that sits in a society's **institutions**, not as values shared in individual minds. — [MetaTOC record](https://metatoc.com/papers/2584-rethinking-the-concept-and-measurement-of-societal-culture-in-light-of-empirical-findings)
- The Global Flourishing Study (2025) found that demographic patterns *differ by country*. In many countries men and women flourish about equally, but men flourish more in Brazil and women more in Japan. — [Springer Nature blog, 30 Apr 2025](https://www.springernature.com/gp/advancing-discovery/springboard/blog/blogposts-sustainability-inclusion/publishing-the-gfs/27774920)

### Inferences
- A region label such as "Africa" or "Asia" says little about what a particular person values or struggles with. A prior learned from the person's *own* answers will usually be more informative after a few days than any regional prior. (Inference from the 2–12% and ~11% figures.)
- Region matters most where cultures differ most: **social attitudes and norms** (e.g. conservatism, family and gender norms, the place of religion) and **institutions and context** (cost of living, health systems, public holidays, migration, languages). This is where regional tailoring belongs. It does not belong in assumptions about inner values.
- Some values are near-universal (autonomy, relatedness, competence). They make safe defaults for daily questions anywhere, which supports a common core of questions with regional context layered on top.
- UN M49 regions did no better or worse than elaborate cultural-dimension models (Field et al.). The owner's region-based framing is therefore not worse than the alternatives, but every one of these models is weak at the individual level.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved this session gives a within- vs between-country variance split for **well-being or life satisfaction** (e.g. Gallup World Poll or GFS intraclass correlations). The GFS flagship paper (nature.com) blocked fetching.
- Exact per-dataset intraclass correlations from Fischer & Schwartz (2011) were not retrieved. Only the secondary "2–12%" summary was.
- No retrieved source decomposes within-country variance by ethnicity or region inside a country (e.g. how much of a "national" pattern is driven by subgroups).

## 2. Culturally adapted psychological and digital interventions: what meta-analyses show, what worked, what backfired

### Takeaway
In face-to-face treatment, culturally adapted interventions beat comparison conditions (g ≈ 0.67 overall). They beat the *same* unadapted intervention by a smaller margin (g ≈ 0.52, only 9 studies; d ≈ .32 in Benish et al.). Adapting how the person **explains their own problem** (the explanatory model) and delivering in the person's **language** stand out as active ingredients. For digital interventions the evidence is thinner. Most adaptations are surface-level, *more* adaptation was not linked to better outcomes, and as of 2021 no trial had directly compared adapted and unadapted apps. Effects were also smaller for prevention (g = 0.25) than for treatment, which matters for a non-clinical reflection app.

### Cited Findings
- **Hall et al. (2016, *Behavior Therapy*):** 78 studies, 13,998 participants (about 95% non-European American), 307 effect sizes. Adapted vs other conditions **g = 0.67**. Adapted vs unadapted versions of the same intervention **g = 0.52**, from only nine direct comparisons. Remission odds ratio **4.68** (16 studies). **Treatment g = 0.76 vs prevention g = 0.25.** Language of intervention, US vs international setting, therapist–client ethnic matching, and top-down vs bottom-up adaptation were *not* significant moderators. A funnel plot suggested publication bias, but the fail-safe N was 1,055. Few studies used culture-specific outcome measures. **[older: 2016]** — [Hall et al. 2016 PDF](https://wou.edu/wp/ibarakia/files/2018/08/Hall-et-al.-2016-BT.pdf)
- **Benish, Quintana & Wampold (2011):** adapted psychotherapy beat unadapted *bona fide* psychotherapy by **d = .32**. Adapting the **illness explanation** (explanatory model) was the *sole* significant moderator (d = .21). Taken from a secondary review. **[older: 2011]** — [Benuto & O'Donohue 2015, IJPSY](https://www.ijpsy.com/volumen15/num3/425/is-culturally-sensitive-cognitive-behavioral-EN.pdf)
- **Griner & Smith (2006):** 76 studies, d = .45. Interventions in clients' **native language** were "twice as effective" as those in English. Interventions targeted to one cultural group were "four times more effective" than those for mixed groups. Taken from a secondary review. **[older: 2006]** — [Benuto & O'Donohue 2015](https://www.ijpsy.com/volumen15/num3/425/is-culturally-sensitive-cognitive-behavioral-EN.pdf)
- **Smith, Rodríguez & Bernal (2011):** 65 studies, N = 8,620, a medium effect favouring adapted treatments over controls. **Huey:** 10 randomised trials of culturally tailored vs generic therapy for ethnic minorities found tailoring effects "small and non-significant." Taken from a secondary review. **[older: 2011–2014]** — [Benuto & O'Donohue 2015](https://www.ijpsy.com/volumen15/num3/425/is-culturally-sensitive-cognitive-behavioral-EN.pdf)
- **Spanhel et al. (2021, *npj Digital Medicine* 4:128):** systematic review of 55 articles covering 42 culturally adapted internet- and mobile-based interventions, mostly for people in LMICs, migrants/refugees and Indigenous peoples. It found 17 adapted components (10 content, 4 methodological, 3 procedural). **Content changes were mostly surface-level** (characters, language, settings). Deeper changes covered values, burdens, concepts of mental health, and treatment goals and methods. Extraction followed **Bernal's framework** (language, persons, metaphors, content, concepts, goals, methods, context). **Greater extent of adaptation was not associated with effectiveness or adherence.** "No included study conducted a direct comparison" of adapted vs non-adapted versions. Hedges' g for RCTs ranged 0.15–2.70 (high heterogeneity). Adherence averaged 63.7% of sessions, with 50.6% completing all sessions. Users were rarely involved in design. Recommends factorial trials. **[older: 2021]** — [Spanhel et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41746-021-00498-1)
- **Naderbagi et al. (2024, *JMIR* 26:e55130), narrative review of 16 digital health adaptation studies.** Surface adaptations are labels, illustrations, images, colours and layout. Deep adaptations include cultural views of mental illness, gender norms and family roles; culturally adapted idioms and metaphors can count as deep. The Chinese adaptation of WHO's *Step-by-Step* reframed depression as **stress management**. — [Naderbagi et al. 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e55130)
- **Backfire examples, same review.** In the *Play Kindly* parenting app, an avatar educator and a rule requiring correct answers made some participants feel they were **parenting incorrectly**, and users disagreed on how culture-specific the app should be. In *Circle of Life*, an impersonal web format clashed with **community-based knowledge sharing** and was changed to a hybrid model; poor internet and poverty also reduced engagement. — [Naderbagi et al. 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e55130)

### Inferences
- The best-supported "deep" adaptation for a reflection app is to **let the person's own framing lead**. Mirror their words and causal explanations ("stress", "pressure", "spirit", "tiredness", "bad luck") instead of imposing clinical or Western-psychology labels. This is the app-level equivalent of explanatory-model adaptation, the one moderator Benish et al. found.
- **Language and idiom** (Griner & Smith) are probably the highest-value surface-to-deep investment. Machine translation without idiom work is likely to underperform.
- Spanhel et al. found that more adaptation did not mean better outcomes, and Hall et al. found smaller effects for prevention. Regional tailoring is therefore unlikely to transform outcomes. Its main value may be *relevance and engagement* rather than effect size.
- The *Play Kindly* case warns against tones that grade or correct. The app's "one small optional step" and insight cards should avoid "right answer" framings.
- Griner & Smith's "group-targeted beats mixed groups" sits in tension with the identity-threat backfire evidence in section 6. One way to reconcile them: tailoring helps when the *content and context* fit, and harms when the person feels *labelled*. This is inference, not tested.

### Gaps
- This session found no meta-analysis of culturally adapted **self-reflection, journaling or well-being** apps; all the evidence comes from treatment or self-help for mental disorders.
- Post-2021 direct comparisons of adapted vs non-adapted digital interventions (e.g. later Step-by-Step trials, 2023–2026 LMIC digital mental health reviews) were not retrieved.
- Primary texts of Benish et al. (2011) and Griner & Smith (2006) were not fetched; figures come from a secondary review.

## 3. The WEIRD problem: how Western is the evidence, and which findings hold up globally

### Takeaway
Top psychology journals still draw just over 60% of authors and samples from the US. Majority-world authors and samples make up only 4–5%, and about 89% of the world's population is effectively unrepresented. The Global Flourishing Study (22 countries, 200,000+ people, 2025) is the largest corrective. Middle-income countries such as Indonesia, Mexico and the Philippines score higher than rich ones on meaning, relationships and prosocial behaviour, while rich countries lead on financial security. Japan ranked lowest. Many Labs 2 found that effects which replicate tend to replicate across WEIRD and less-WEIRD samples alike. Effects that fail tend to fail everywhere.

### Cited Findings
- Thalmayer, Toscanelli & Arnett (2021, *American Psychologist* 76(1):116–129) studied six leading APA journals over 2014–2018. Authors and samples were "a little over 60%" American, down from over 70% in Arnett's 2008 analysis, while the US is about 5% of world population. The shift came mainly from other English-speaking and Western European countries. About 11% of the world's population is represented, leaving 89% neglected. "Majority world authors and samples (4–5%) are still sorely lacking." — [Clark University record](https://commons.clarku.edu/faculty_psychology/687)
- **Global Flourishing Study (GFS):** 200,000+ people in 22 nations, surveyed annually 2022–2027. The flagship paper appeared in *Nature Mental Health* alongside more than 30 papers across Springer Nature journals (blog 30 April 2025). **Indonesia** ranked highest across a broad range of indicators and **Japan** lowest. Wealthier nations (e.g. Sweden, US) scored higher on material and financial items. **Middle-income countries scored higher on prosocial behaviour, close relationships and meaning in life.** The traditional U-shape in age no longer holds: flourishing is flat from 18–49 and rises later. — [Springer Nature blog](https://www.springernature.com/gp/advancing-discovery/springboard/blog/blogposts-sustainability-inclusion/publishing-the-gfs/27774920)
- More GFS findings: 18–24-year-olds reported lower flourishing in many nations. Indonesia, Mexico and the Philippines reported higher meaning, purpose and social connectedness than Japan and the UK. The page describes religious engagement as "a strong contributor to flourishing, even in secular societies". The source is a press release from Regent University, so treat its emphasis cautiously. — [Regent University news, 6 May 2025](https://www.regent.edu/news/first-wave-of-findings-released-from-the-highly-anticipated-global-flourishing-study/)
- Funding and interest context: Baylor's Institute for Studies of Religion is a GFS partner ([Springer Nature blog](https://www.springernature.com/gp/advancing-discovery/springboard/blog/blogposts-sustainability-inclusion/publishing-the-gfs/27774920)). Lead author T. J. VanderWeele discloses consulting fees from Gloo Inc. ([Nature comment, VanderWeele & Johnson, 30 Apr 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-01254-1)).
- **Many Labs 2** (Klein et al. 2018, *AMPPS* 1(4):443–490) tested 28 findings across 125 samples (15,305 participants, 36 countries and territories). 15 of 28 (54%) replicated, and median d fell from 0.60 (original) to 0.15. Significant heterogeneity appeared for 11 of 28 effects, mostly the largest ones. An exploratory comparison found **little heterogeneity between WEIRD and less-WEIRD samples**. "Variability in effect sizes was driven more by the effect itself than by the sample or setting." **[older: 2018]** — [Many Labs 2 record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/many-labs-2-investigating-variation-in-replicability-across-sampl/)

### Inferences
- A design built around **meaning, close relationships and prosocial acts** is not a Western import. In the GFS these dimensions are, if anything, *stronger* in middle-income countries. A design built around **financial security** will hit hardest where it is most lacking. Weekly mirrors can reflect both kinds of flourishing without ranking either.
- Rich countries can score low on social and meaning dimensions (Japan, UK in the GFS). The app should not treat high-income regions as "well-off" by default.
- Many Labs 2 suggests the bigger risk is building on **weak findings**, which fail everywhere, rather than on findings that work only in the West. That said, Many Labs 2 tested mostly lab social-cognition effects, not well-being constructs, so it cannot certify well-being findings as universal.
- 18–24-year-olds have low flourishing across many countries. Young users may be a higher-need group regardless of region.

### Gaps
- The full list of the 22 GFS countries, and any published measurement-invariance or translation critiques, were not retrieved (nature.com blocked fetching). GFS coverage of the world is partial: 22 countries are not the whole world, and no source here established which regions are missing.
- This session found no replication-across-countries evidence specific to positive-psychology interventions (gratitude, savouring, etc.).

## 4. Diaspora, migrants and ethnic minorities in Western countries: what matters and what not to assume

### Takeaway
Discrimination is common and rarely reported. Among people of African descent in 13 EU countries, 45% reported racial discrimination in the past five years (over 70% in Germany and Austria), and only 9% reported it to anyone. Identity is plural and shifts across generations. Second-generation Hispanic and Asian Americans are twice as likely as immigrants to feel "typical American" yet still often name their family's origin country first. Holding both cultures (biculturalism) is on average linked to better adjustment, but not uniformly across groups. Remittances are a large, everyday part of life for many families: $656bn flowed to LMICs in 2023.

### Cited Findings
- **FRA *Being Black in the EU* (published 25 Oct 2023):** over 6,700 people of African descent in 13 EU countries. **45%** experienced racial discrimination in the past five years, up from 39% in FRA's previous survey, and over **70%** in Germany and Austria. Only **9%** reported it. **30%** experienced racist harassment. **58%** of those stopped by police in the past year saw their most recent stop as racial profiling. 34% faced discrimination when job-hunting, 31% at work and 31% in housing. Young people and those with higher education were most affected, and young women were more often harassed. More parents reported their children facing racism at school than in 2016. — [FRA press release](https://fra.europa.eu/pl/news/2023/black-people-eu-face-ever-more-racism)
- **Pew (2012 Asian-American survey and a Hispanic survey).** Most-used self-description among Hispanics: immigrants 61% country of origin, 29% pan-ethnic, 8% "American"; **second generation 38% origin, 20% pan-ethnic, 37% American.** Among Asian Americans: immigrants 69% origin, 18% pan-ethnic, 9% American; **second generation 45% origin, 23% pan-ethnic, 27% American.** "Typical American": 33% of Hispanic and 30% of Asian immigrants vs **61%** of the second generation in both groups. About 80% of second-generation Hispanics speak Spanish well, but only 41% of second-generation Asian Americans speak their family language well. Most see passing on the language as important (94% of second-generation Hispanics, 76% of second-generation Asian Americans). **[older: 2012–2013]** — [Pew, "Chapter 3: Identity"](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2013/02/07/chapter-3-identity/)
- **Nguyen & Benet-Martínez (2013, JCCP 44(1):122–159), meta-analysis.** 83 studies, 23,197 participants. Biculturalism was positively related to psychological adjustment (e.g. life satisfaction) and sociocultural adjustment, and *more strongly* than orientation to either culture alone. Reported random-effects estimates: r ≈ .48 psychological, r ≈ .49 sociocultural; these are our extraction from the PDF, so verify before quoting. The link was stronger in the US (r = .62) than in other countries (r = .32). **It did not hold for African (r = .01) or Indigenous (r = −.71) samples.** Health-related adjustment was near zero. The evidence is correlational and heavily weighted toward US Latino and Asian samples. **[older: 2013]** — [Nguyen & Benet-Martínez 2013 PDF](https://psychology.fullerton.edu/amnguyen/pdf/jccp2013.pdf)
- **Active debate.** Bierwiaczonek & Kunst (2021) found a limited role for acculturation strategies relative to context. Grigoryev, Berry et al. (2023, *IJIR* 97:101897) counter with multilevel meta-analyses of three multinational datasets that support the integration hypothesis, "especially for positive adaptation indicators such as life satisfaction and self-esteem." — [Grigoryev et al. 2023 record](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/7cbcf69e-9925-415e-956a-62577b56b7a6)
- **Remittances (World Bank, 26 Jun 2024).** Flows to LMICs reached **$656bn in 2023** (+0.7%), with 2.3% growth projected for 2024. India received $120bn and Mexico $66.2bn. By region: South Asia $186bn, Latin America and the Caribbean $156bn, East Asia and Pacific excluding China $85bn, Europe and Central Asia $71bn, Middle East and North Africa $55bn, Sub-Saharan Africa $54bn. **Tonga: 41% of GDP.** Sending $200 cost **6.4%** on average in Q4 2023 against an SDG target of 3%. **[older: 2023 data]** — [World Bank press release](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2024/06/26/remittances-slowed-in-2023-expected-to-grow-faster-in-2024)

### Inferences
- **What the app must not assume:**
  - that a user's region of *residence* equals their cultural reference point;
  - that their heritage region is where their concerns lie;
  - that second-generation users identify with either "side" exclusively;
  - that they speak the heritage language (only 41% of second-generation Asian Americans do well);
  - that family obligations or remittances are a burden, or that they are not;
  - that discrimination is absent because it goes unmentioned (only 9% report it).
- A practical model: one "where you live now" setting, plus an optional, user-written or user-chosen "places that feel like home / where family is" that can include more than one place. Content draws on both, and the user can turn either off.
- Prompts about **unfair treatment and belonging** should be available everywhere, phrased without naming a group. For example: "Was there a moment today when you felt you had to prove you belonged?" or "Did anyone treat you unfairly today?" The person then names the cause if they want to. This fits the FRA picture of frequent, unreported discrimination and avoids asking about ethnicity.
- Remittance and family-obligation reflections ("money or help you sent to family", "a duty you carried today") are relevant to a very large population across South Asia, Latin America, Africa and the Pacific. They should allow pride, love, strain and guilt equally.
- Bicultural benefit is not universal; the meta-analysis found none for African-heritage samples and a negative link for Indigenous ones. Insight cards should not celebrate "the best of both worlds" as a default message.

### Gaps
- No source was retrieved this session on **code-switching** (e.g. its costs for Black professionals), so claims about it are unsupported here.
- Figures from OECD/EU *Indicators of Immigrant Integration 2023* on perceived discrimination, belonging and life satisfaction were not retrieved; the fetched presentation contained no numbers.
- No source was retrieved on the mental-health effects of remittance and family obligations (e.g. "black tax" research in Southern Africa).
- Non-Western migration (South Asians in the Gulf, intra-African, intra-Asian and Latin American migration) and minorities *within* Africa and Asia are not covered here.

## 5. Detecting region: privacy and accuracy of time zone/locale, IP geolocation and asking; GDPR treatment; risks of inferring ethnicity

### Takeaway
IP geolocation is accurate at country level (vendor claims >99% excluding VPNs) but poor at city level (20–75%). It fails for VPN users, and calling a third-party lookup sends the user's IP to that vendor. Under GDPR, *inferring* ethnicity or religion is itself special-category processing. EDPB guidance says this holds whether or not the inference is correct, and the CJEU (C-184/20, 2022) extends Article 9 to data "liable indirectly to reveal" sensitive traits. The safest pattern is to **pre-fill a coarse country guess on the device and let the user confirm or change it**, and **never to infer ethnicity**.

### Cited Findings
- **MaxMind (2026 page):** country-level accuracy "exceeding 99%, especially when excluding VPN usage"; state/province 55–80% depending on country; city 20–75%. VPNs and proxies show the server's location. Mobile-carrier IPs can be reassigned between users hundreds of kilometres apart within minutes. Small satellite operators make even country-level geolocation hard. iCloud Private Relay and similar services keep users "in the general region." These are vendor self-reports. — [MaxMind accuracy page](https://www.maxmind.com/en/geoip/ip-geolocation-accuracy)
- **GDPR Art. 9(1)** prohibits processing of "personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership," as well as genetic data, biometric data used to identify someone, health data, and data on sex life or sexual orientation. Art. 9(2)(a) allows it with **explicit consent** "for one or more specified purposes" unless EU or member-state law forbids it. Recital 51 says the term "racial origin" does not imply acceptance of theories of separate human races. — [GDPR Art. 9 text and commentary](https://gdpr-text.com/en/read/article-9/)
- **EDPB Guidelines 8/2020** (targeting of social media users): if observed data is used "to categorise users as having certain religious, philosophical" (or other special-category) traits, this "must obviously be seen as processing of special category of personal data" "regardless of whether the categorization is correct/true or not". "It does not matter how the category is labelled." — [GDPR Art. 9 commentary quoting EDPB 8/2020](https://gdpr-text.com/en/read/article-9/)
- **CJEU C-184/20, *OT v Vyriausioji tarnybinės etikos komisija* (1 Aug 2022):** any personal data "liable indirectly to reveal sensitive information concerning a natural person" falls under the Art. 9(1) prohibition unless an Art. 9(2) exception applies. In this case a partner's name revealed sexual orientation. Commentators note the outer limits are unclear. One reading is that a controller who does not publish the data and forbids staff from drawing inferences may fall outside Art. 9; this is uncertain. — [Covington Inside Privacy, 10 Aug 2022](https://www.insideprivacy.com/eu-data-protection/special-category-data-by-inference-cjeu-significantly-expands-the-scope-of-article-9-gdpr/)
- Helpline-routing vendors advertise location-based localisation. ThroughLine claims "Automatic localization based on user location" without disclosing the method. Its embeddable widget uses **country and topic dropdowns**, i.e. user choice. — [ThroughLine](https://www.throughlinecare.com/)

### Inferences
- **Device locale and time zone** are read on the device with no permission prompt and no network call, which makes them the most private signal. Neither is reliable. Locale reflects language and format preference, not residence: many phones worldwide ship with en-US. Time zones span several countries, and travellers and diaspora users may keep home settings. These are reasoned points, not sourced in this session. They are fine for a *pre-filled suggestion* and unsafe as a silent decision.
- **IP lookups** give a better country guess. But calling a third-party geolocation API sends the user's IP to that vendor, which conflicts with the app's anonymous-key design. If IP is used at all, prefer an on-device or self-hosted database and discard the IP immediately.
- **Asking** ("Where do you live now?", pre-filled from locale or time zone, with a change option always visible in settings) is the most accurate and most respectful choice. It also handles VPN users, travellers and diaspora users. It should be framed as optional and explained ("so we can show relevant questions and local helplines").
- **Never infer ethnicity, religion or political views.** Under the EDPB and CJEU positions, even a wrong or harmlessly labelled inference, such as a "diaspora-South-Asian content track" switched on from name, language or answer patterns, could count as special-category processing needing explicit consent. Content should follow what the user *chose*, not what the system guessed.
- A user-entered "heritage region" or "where family is from" field may itself reveal ethnic origin indirectly under C-184/20. It should be optional, explained, deletable, ideally stored only on the device, and kept out of any server-side analytics. This is a legal inference; get counsel to confirm.
- Country of residence is ordinary personal data (GDPR Art. 4(1) lists "location data" among identifiers; that article text was not re-fetched this session). Storing it under an anonymous key is not anonymisation if it can be combined with other answers. Keep it coarse (country, not city) and minimise retention.

### Gaps
- No EDPB guidance retrieved specifically on country-level IP geolocation or on reading device locale under ePrivacy Art. 5(3) (terminal-equipment access).
- Apple and Google platform documentation on locale and region APIs and approximate location was not fetched.
- Non-EU regimes were not covered: India DPDP Act, Brazil LGPD, China PIPL, South Africa POPIA, US state laws. Some treat ethnicity or location differently, and a "global" app will need them.
- No independent (non-vendor) accuracy audit of IP geolocation by country or continent was found. Accuracy in Africa and parts of Asia may differ from vendor averages.

## 6. Personalisation fairness: stereotype threat and identity-based messaging, and what "speak to any ethnicity" should mean in practice

### Takeaway
Naming someone's group can backfire. In US experiments, Black adults who *believed* a health message was chosen because of their race felt judged, trusted the provider less, paid less attention and were less motivated to act; White adults showed no effect (Derricks & Earl 2023). Classic stereotype-threat effects on test performance look smaller and less reliable than once thought (d ≈ −.14 in operational-like settings, with publication bias; a large Dutch registered report found nothing). Even so, identity threat from being *labelled* is a live risk. "Speak to any ethnicity" should mean **removing defaults and assumptions, using user-chosen context and the user's own words**, not assigning group labels.

### Cited Findings
- **Derricks & Earl (2023, *Journal of Communication*, DOI 10.1093/joc/jqad022).** Study 1 had 200 Black and 201 White adults. When Black adults believed an HIV or flu message was race-targeted (assignment was actually random and the message did not mention race), they felt judged by race, attended less and trusted the provider less; White adults showed no difference. Study 2 had 381 Black adults. Race-targeting *also lowered motivation for HIV-prevention behaviours*. The mechanism was social identity threat, which the authors call "overaccommodation." Recommendations: base messages on important cultural values rather than superficial group membership, and express support. — [SPSP blog, 27 Nov 2023](https://spsp.org/news/character-and-context-blog/derricks-health-messaging-targeted-by-race); [abstract record](https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/too-close-for-comfort-leveraging-identity-based-relevance-through-targeted-health-information-backfires-for-black-americans)
- **Shewach, Sackett & Quint (2019, *J. Applied Psychology*):** in studies with features likely in real high-stakes testing, stereotype threat **d = −.14** (k = 45, N = 3,532). Operational settings and motivational-incentive subsets ranged d = .00 to −.14. Publication bias was "nontrivial." The effect in operational settings "may be negligible to small." **[older: 2019]** — [Shewach et al. abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/369898)
- **Flore, Mulder & Wicherts (2019, registered report):** 2,064 Dutch students aged 13–14 in 86 classes. Girls' threat vs control **d = −0.05** (95% CI −0.18 to 0.07), with Bayes factors favouring the null. The paper notes the earlier Flore & Wicherts (2015) meta-analysis found an average of about 0.22 for school-aged girls, with heterogeneity and a pattern "expected from publication bias." **[older: 2019]** — [Flore et al. PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/pure.uvt.nl/0c8b3ab078844672621b0ffb5ccc18fc093af4a0.pdf)
- An open-science textbook chapter summarises the literature this way: meta-analyses show heterogeneous effects, mixed moderators and flagged publication bias; it also discusses "stereotype lift" in non-stereotyped groups. — [FORRT open social psychology, ch. 11](https://forrt.org/open-social-psychology/chapter11.html)
- In contrast, therapy interventions *targeted to one cultural group* were reported as about four times more effective than those for mixed groups (Griner & Smith 2006, via a secondary review). — [Benuto & O'Donohue 2015](https://www.ijpsy.com/volumen15/num3/425/is-culturally-sensitive-cognitive-behavioral-EN.pdf). Adapting the person's **explanatory model** was the only significant moderator in Benish et al. 2011, via the same secondary review — [Benuto & O'Donohue 2015](https://www.ijpsy.com/volumen15/num3/425/is-culturally-sensitive-cognitive-behavioral-EN.pdf)

### Inferences
- **When naming a group seems to harm:** the system assigns the label rather than the user; the group is stigmatised in that context; the message implies risk or deficit ("people like you are at higher risk of…"). Derricks & Earl show all three can backfire. These conditions are inferred from one US study plus the mechanism it identifies.
- **When it may help:** the person has *chosen* the frame; content reflects their values or circumstances rather than their category; there is no deficit framing. This is inferred from Griner & Smith and Benish set against Derricks & Earl. No study in this session directly tests this in apps.
- Stereotype-threat effects on test scores look small. The app should not overclaim that "naming identity lowers performance." The more relevant risk is **trust and engagement loss from feeling labelled**.
- **What "speak to any ethnicity" can mean in practice.** These are design proposals derived from the evidence above, not sourced practices:
  - **Remove hidden defaults:** no assumed nuclear family, partner, alcohol, dating culture, Christmas or Western holidays, car ownership, individual career choice, or secular framing. Write prompts that work whether someone lives with grandparents, sends money home, prays five times a day or not at all.
  - **Diverse examples, not labels:** rotate names, foods, places, family structures and work types in insight-card examples across the world's cultures, without saying "for [ethnicity] users".
  - **Open slots for the person's culture:** "Who in your family or community came to mind today?" or "Was there a tradition, prayer, song or saying that helped?" The user supplies the content and the app mirrors it back in their words (the explanatory-model principle).
  - **Context, not category, drives regional content:** the confirmed country (and optionally places that feel like home) selects local calendars, public holidays, languages, idioms, currency and helplines. It never selects assumed beliefs.
  - **No group comparison or risk statistics** about the person's presumed group, consistent with the app's no-comparison rule.
  - **Visible control:** show *why* a question appears ("because you said you live in Kenya") and offer "this doesn't fit me" feedback that tunes future questions.
- Tone matters. Avoid quizzes with right answers or corrective feedback, given the *Play Kindly* backfire in section 2.

### Gaps
- Derricks & Earl is US-only, compares only Black and White participants, and tests health messages. No study was found testing identity-based personalisation in **reflection or well-being apps**, or outside the US.
- Values-affirmation and "identity-safe" interventions, and their replication record, were not retrieved.
- No empirical study was found evaluating "inclusive default" content strategies, such as diverse examples without labels, against group-targeted strategies.

## 7. Helplines across the world: routing people to help in their own country reliably

### Takeaway
ThroughLine's Find A Helpline is the de facto global directory. IASP points to it as its main directory, and it is used by Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Discord and others. It claims daily-maintained, helpline-verified listings. Its coverage claims are inconsistent: "100+", "150+", "170+", "175+" and "178" countries appear on different pages. No independent accuracy audit was found. Route by **user-confirmed country**, offer more than one channel (phone, text, chat, WhatsApp), and keep a local emergency-number fallback.

### Cited Findings
- Find A Helpline says it works with helplines in "over 175 countries", that helpline organisations verify their own information directly with ThroughLine, and that data is "Verified data, maintained daily." It lists Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Pinterest, Roblox, Match Group and Discord as partners. It states no limitations, and its "largest, most accurate" claim is self-description. — [Find A Helpline, About](https://findahelpline.com/about)
- Find A Helpline's widget page advertised "real-time crisis support in **100+** countries" (search-result title); the URL now redirects to ThroughLine. — [Find A Helpline widget page](https://www.findahelpline.com/get-the-widget)
- ThroughLine offers an **API** (a `/countries` endpoint requiring authorisation), a **widget** with country and topic dropdowns, a web app with a quick-exit button, and "FirstStep", an AI guide that connects users to helplines. It claims "1500+ verified services across **170+** countries" while its own metadata says 178. It also claims "anonymous access" and "privacy-first". No public pricing; it offers a free "SB 243 compliance and implementation guide". IASP appears among partner logos. — [ThroughLine](https://www.throughlinecare.com/)
- **IASP** (page last modified 15 Jun 2026) states "IASP is not a crisis centre and does not provide direct support." It points to Find A Helpline as listing verified helplines "in over **150** countries", reachable by phone, text, online chat or WhatsApp and searchable by topic and population (youth, LGBTQ+, veterans). It also lists IFOTES, **Befrienders Worldwide** (349 centres in 32 countries, an estimated 7m people a year, unsourced) and **Lifeline International** (members in 20 countries). It mentions a WHO handbook on establishing crisis lines (2019). — [IASP crisis centres & helplines](https://www.iasp.info/?p=10328)

### Inferences
- Coverage numbers vary from 100+ to 178 across ThroughLine's own pages and IASP. The app should **check coverage per country** at build time and handle countries with no listing gracefully: show the local emergency number and a general note.
- Routing should use the **user-confirmed country**, not IP. This keeps the user's answers and IP away from the helpline vendor; pass only a country code and optionally a topic. A cached, regularly refreshed copy on the device lets the help screen work offline and without a network call that reveals a crisis moment.
- Diaspora users may want helplines in their **language**, or in their heritage country. Most crisis services serve callers physically present in their country, so the default should be the country of residence, with a "show helplines in another country" option. The cross-border point is an inference, not verified.
- "SB 243" is probably California's 2025 companion-chatbot law requiring crisis-referral protocols. This was **not verified** this session; check whether it applies if the app uses AI-generated text.
- Find A Helpline lists Anthropic as a partner. This is neutral for the app's choice but worth disclosing if relevant to the owner.

### Gaps
- A global survey of suicide helpline services across 105 countries is indexed by the University of Melbourne ([record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1969983-evaluating-the-global-landscape-of-suicide-helpline-services--a-global-survey-across-105-countries)), but the page blocked fetching. Its findings on hours, languages, funding and LMIC gaps are unknown here.
- No independent audit of Find A Helpline's accuracy (dead numbers, hours, languages) was found.
- This session did not establish which countries have no listed helpline, or the language coverage per country.
