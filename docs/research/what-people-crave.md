# Everyone Craves the Same Things, Differently

People everywhere crave the same short list of things: to feel that their choices are their own, to feel capable, to be connected and cared for, to be respected, and to be safe with enough to live on. Across 123 countries each of these goes with higher well-being, and none has to wait in line for the others. Hope, meaning, calm and success are better seen as what grows when those needs are met than as separate drives, and 'success' splits sharply in two: doing well and being respected feed people, while putting money, image or fame first goes with feeling worse. Personality and culture do not decide *whether* someone needs these things (people who say they want connection less are not protected when it is missing), but they do decide which craving feels loudest and which route a person takes to meet it. Anxious, emotionally sensitive people lean towards security, open people towards freedom, warm people towards belonging, and people low in honesty and humility towards visible status. Self-discovery does help people towards economic and social freedom, security, hope, faith in others and contentment, but only modestly and only in one form: a concrete description of what someone did in a situation, said kindly and accurately, in a private space with nothing to lose, and turned into one small step the person chooses and actually takes. Reflection on its own is weak and can slide into brooding, flattering or vague insight is believed without being true, and no amount of self-knowledge replaces income, safety nets or freedom from discrimination. The biggest and most universal gains come from pointing people outwards, towards relationships, small kindnesses and testing their usually too-gloomy predictions about other people, rather than inwards towards monitoring their own happiness. For a private, worldwide self-reflection app, the evidence turns its existing rules (no comparisons, no diagnosis, no sides on religion or politics) from ethical choices into the very conditions under which people accept hard truths about themselves.

## Most effects here are small, and saying so is the honest position

**Almost every finding below is a pattern seen across many people, not a promise for any one person, and most of those patterns are small to moderate.** Where numbers appear, a correlation (written r, or ρ when statistically corrected) of about 0.1 is a small link, 0.3 a moderate one and 0.5 a large one. A difference between groups (written d or g) of 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. An odds ratio of 2 means twice the odds. A small effect can still matter when it touches millions of daily choices, but it never justifies telling someone their life will change. Most studies show that two things go together rather than that one causes the other, and much of the psychology rests on what researchers call WEIRD samples (Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic), so evidence from many countries is flagged where it exists. Where a figure could not be verified, it has been left out rather than guessed. No study anywhere has yet tested the exact format the app uses: daily everyday-situation questions with forced choices, a personal insight, small steps and weekly or monthly mirrors. Every design recommendation at the end is therefore an informed extrapolation that the app should check in its own users.

## Five needs sit beneath every craving, and belonging is the loudest

**Under the many things people say they want, the research keeps finding the same five: to feel their choices are their own, to feel capable, to feel connected and loved, to feel respected, and to feel safe with enough to live on.** The largest test is a Gallup World Poll analysis of **60,865 adults in 123 countries**, which found that meeting each of these needs went with higher well-being "consistently… across world regions" ([Tay & Diener 2011](http://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-101-2-354.pdf)). The needs did different jobs. How people rated their life overall depended mostly on basic needs such as food, shelter and money, but **how good their days felt depended most on being treated with respect (36% of the explained variation) and on love and support (24%)**. Bad days tracked lack of respect, unmet basic needs and lack of freedom in roughly equal measure. Crucially, the needs added up rather than unlocking in a strict order. Maslow's famous ladder held only as a "soft" tendency, and meeting needs "out of order" was not less fulfilling. A study in Belgium, China, the USA and Peru found that freedom, capability and connection predicted life satisfaction and energy with no meaningful difference between the four countries ([Chen et al. 2015](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/2014_Chen-et-al._need-satisfaction.pdf)). Values research across **63 nations** points the same way: caring for the people close to you ranked **first in every type of sample**, self-direction and concern for others came second and third, security fourth or fifth, and power **last everywhere** ([Schwartz & Bardi 2001](https://repository.royalholloway.ac.uk/items/4c815cf2-e6e6-9a01-7090-90d03bbe5645/7/Schwartz_Bardi_2001_Value_Hierarchies_across_Cultures.pdf)). When people in the US and South Korea described their most satisfying events, freedom, competence, connection and self-esteem came top, and money, luxury and popularity came lower ([Sheldon et al. 2001](https://scholarship.miami.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/What-Is-Satisfying-About-Satisfying-Events/991031600454702976)).

Belonging stands out as the loudest of the five. A classic review concluded that people need "frequent, nonaversive interactions within an ongoing relational bond" and that "social isolation is practically incompatible with high levels of happiness" ([Baumeister & Leary 1995](https://www.hendrix.edu/uploadedFiles/Academics/Faculty_Resources/2016_FFC/Baumeister%20and%20Leary%20%281995%29.pdf)). When needs are actively thwarted rather than merely unmet, **being rejected or left out was the strongest predictor of low mood in all four countries** of the Chen study (b = .35, against .26 for being controlled and .17 for feeling incompetent). Two close cousins of belonging matter too. Respect, meaning esteem freely given by the people around you, predicted well-being even after accounting for feeling accepted. It works locally: income raises status only relative to people nearby, and losing status harms mood and health ([Anderson et al. 2015](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PPP387/Desire%20for%20Status%2C%20Human%20Motive%2C%20APA.pdf)). Being understood is the other. People's feelings warm to praise, but their judgements and their actual choices favour accurate feedback. In risky new relationships people prefer flattery; in safe, committed ones they choose to be seen accurately, flaws included ([Kwang & Swann 2010](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/10/kwangSwann2010.pdf)). Feeling understood goes with better days, but it is only modestly tied to whether others really do understand us ([Reis, Lemay & Finkenauer 2017](https://utrecht-main-test.atmire.com/items/1df3db17-70bb-4df9-84c2-6da7cb6cf86a)).

### The four named cravings are four different kinds of thing

**Freedom and success are core needs in disguise, security is a need when it is missing and a substitute when other needs are frustrated, and hope is an outlook rather than a need.** Freedom, in the research, means volition: acting from genuine endorsement, not independence from other people. In South Korea, Russia, Turkey and the US, how far people endorsed their own reasons for following cultural practices predicted well-being "across cultures and gender", so doing something for your family can be as free as doing something for yourself ([Chirkov et al. 2003](https://scholarship.miami.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Differentiating-Autonomy-From-Individualism-and-Independence/991031598656802976)). Across 63 societies and 420,599 people, a sense of individual autonomy predicted lower distress more consistently than national wealth did. It mattered most in poorer countries, and its benefit levelled off, and in one study reversed, at the very top ([Fischer & Boer 2011](https://scr.hse.ru/data/2011/11/24/1271296504/fischer%20boer%202011%20psp.pdf)).

Security is the most context-bound craving. Feeling safe and having enough depended heavily on which country people lived in ([Tay & Diener 2011](http://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-101-2-354.pdf)), and security and tradition values rise with age while the appetite for novelty falls, in all 12 European countries studied ([Robinson 2012/13](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/8968/)). Theorists disagree about its status. Self-determination researchers treat security as a substitute people reach for when freedom, competence or connection are frustrated ([Vansteenkiste, Ryan & Soenens 2020](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/2020_VansteenkisteRyanSoenens_BPNSIntro_MOEM.pdf)), whereas Carol Dweck lists predictability among three needs present from birth ([APA on Dweck 2017](https://www.apa.org/pubs/highlights/spotlight/issue-111.html)). The practical reading is that both are true at different moments: real lack of safety is a genuine need, but an endless hunger for more money, control or certainty is often standing in for something else.

Hope is not a need in any major model. It is an outlook whose level varies enormously. On a 0–10 scale across 202,898 people in 22 countries, hope averaged **9.17 in Indonesia but 5.95 in Japan**. People attending worship more than weekly scored 8.68 against 7.82 for those who never attend, and unemployed people scored lower than the self-employed ([Global Flourishing Study](https://globalflourishingstudy.com/paper/where-hope-lives-who-stays-hopeful-around-the-world)). Success splits in two. Across **70,110 people in eight world regions**, valuing growth, relationships, community and health went with higher well-being (r = .24). Valuing money, image or fame on its own was roughly neutral (wealth alone r = .03), but **putting them ahead of the intrinsic goals went with lower well-being (r = −.22)**, though that figure rests on only 14 studies ([Bradshaw et al. 2022](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2022_BradshawEtAl_MetaAspriations_JPSP.pdf)). Doing something well and being respected feed people; getting ahead materially, when it comes first, drains them.

### The evidence adds meaning, calm and being seen

Three further cravings earn a place. Meaning runs higher in poorer, more religious nations even where life satisfaction runs lower, across 132 countries ([Oishi & Diener 2014](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/residents-of-poorer-nations-find-greater-meaning-in-life.html)). Calm matters more than Western self-help assumes. Asked to define happiness in their own words, 2,799 adults in 12 countries from Argentina to India and South Africa most often named **inner harmony** ([Delle Fave et al. 2016](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00030/text)), and a reanalysis of 61 countries found that wanting to *maximise* happiness is itself a distinctly WEIRD ideal ([Krys et al. 2025](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/happiness-maximization-is-a-weird-way-of-living/)). Culture shapes the feelings people *want*, calm or excitement, far more than the feelings they actually have ([Tsai et al. 2025](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/entities/publication/d4fbbc23-48dc-4d47-8232-efe6fe572280)). When any core craving is actively frustrated, people compensate with rigid habits, self-worth that hangs on conditions, defiance, or substitutes such as money, image and extra security ([Vansteenkiste, Ryan & Soenens 2020](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/2020_VansteenkisteRyanSoenens_BPNSIntro_MOEM.pdf)). A lost sense of significance can drive people towards sacrifice and extreme positions ([Kruglanski et al. 2022](https://ruj.uj.edu.pl/xmlui/handle/item/311546)).

| What people say they crave | What the evidence says it really is | How universal | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freedom | Feeling your choices are your own (volition), not standing apart from others | The need is universal; the 'independence' version varies by culture and falls with age | Strong |
| Security | Safety, enough to live on, predictability | Level and importance vary most by country and age; whether it is a basic need is disputed | Moderate |
| Hope | An outlook built from "I can act" and "I can see a way" | Levels vary hugely (Indonesia 9.17, Japan 5.95 out of 10) | Moderate |
| Success | Two opposite things: doing well and being respected (good for you) versus money, image or fame put first (bad for you) | Competence and respect are universal; status works only locally | Strong |
| Belonging and love | Ongoing, caring bonds | The most universal; top value in 63 nations | Strong |
| Respect | Being treated with respect by the people around you | Universal; the strongest predictor of good days | Moderate |
| Being understood | Being seen accurately and still accepted | Probably universal | Moderate |
| Meaning | A sense that life matters | Higher in poorer, more religious places | Moderate |
| Calm and peace | Low-key positive feeling, inner harmony | A common ideal worldwide; how much weight it gets varies by culture | Moderate |

## Personality decides which craving shouts, not whether it matters

**Personality changes how easily someone's needs are met, which craving feels loudest and which route they take, but it does not change the needs themselves.** In the four-country study, how much people said they wanted freedom, competence or connection did not change how much they benefited from having them or suffered from losing them ([Chen et al. 2015](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/2014_Chen-et-al._need-satisfaction.pdf)), and a later review found no case where people low in a need escaped harm when it was frustrated ([Vansteenkiste, Ryan & Soenens 2020](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/2020_VansteenkisteRyanSoenens_BPNSIntro_MOEM.pdf)). A meta-analysis of more than 10,500 people found that emotionally sensitive people (high neuroticism) report less satisfaction of all three core psychological needs, while outgoing, open, warm and conscientious people report more. The links are small to moderate ([Vukasović Hlupić et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12144-022-04158-9)). The wider link between personality and happiness is strong. Across 122,588 people, neuroticism was tied to negative feelings (ρ ≈ .64), extraversion to happiness (≈ .57) and conscientiousness to overall quality of life (≈ .51), and personality accounted for roughly a fifth to two-fifths of the differences between people in happiness and life satisfaction ([Steel, Schmidt & Shultz 2008](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2008-steel.pdf)). The popular claim that "40% of your happiness is under your control" is one its own authors now call "a gross oversimplification" ([Greater Good 2020](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_much_of_your_happiness_is_under_your_control)).

Traits and values are related but distinct ([Parks-Leduc et al. 2015](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/personality-traits-and-personal-values-a-meta-analysis/)). Across 14 countries, openness was most strongly tied to how people weigh security and tradition, and agreeableness to caring and fairness values. Most of these links **weakened where life was financially, socially or ecologically threatened** ([Fischer & Boer 2015](https://www.rlp-forschung.de/public/publications/106634)). When life is precarious, security grows loud for almost everyone, whatever their temperament. Insecure attachment, whether anxious or avoidant, went with lower satisfaction of all three needs, which in turn explained lower life satisfaction in a Chinese student sample ([Chen, Khir et al. 2026](https://www.techscience.com/IJMHP/v28n7/68247)). People low in honesty and humility are, by definition, "motivated by material gain" and want "to enjoy and to display wealth and privilege" ([HEXACO scale descriptions](https://hexaco.org/scaledescriptions)). The table sets out the most defensible pairings. The link between each trait and need satisfaction is the best-evidenced part; the routes and traps are reasonable inferences, not tested rules.

| Trait or style | Craving that tends to be loudest | Typical route | Typical trap | How solid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Emotionally sensitive (high neuroticism) | Security, predictability, reassurance | Planning, avoiding risk, seeking comfort | Brooding; more frustration of every need | Lower need satisfaction is meta-analytic; the pull to security is inferred |
| Outgoing (extraversion) | Belonging, being noticed | Company, visibility | Chasing status through attention | Strong link to good feelings; route inferred |
| Open to experience | Freedom, novelty, self-direction | Exploring, changing plans, reflecting | Reflection that never settles into insight | Moderate |
| Warm and cooperative (agreeableness) | Belonging, harmony, caring | Giving, fitting in | Saying yes to avoid letting people down | Moderate |
| Conscientious | Competence; security through order | Follow-through, routine | Perfectionism, compulsive work | Strong link to quality of life; trap inferred |
| Low honesty-humility | Status, visible wealth | Display, privilege | Money and image put first | Scale definitions only |
| Anxious attachment | Being understood and reassured | Seeking closeness | Fear-driven search for a partner | Early |
| Avoidant attachment | Freedom from depending on others | Self-reliance | Thin connection | Early |

Some of the most useful patterns for self-discovery are not personality traits at all but habits of mind that shift more easily. Always hunting for the best option ("maximising") went with more regret (r = .52), more depression (r = .34) and less happiness across seven North American samples ([Schwartz et al. 2002](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Schwartz-et-al.-2002.pdf)). Valuing possessions and status went with lower well-being across 259 samples (r ≈ −.19) ([Dittmar et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/)). Staking self-worth on money was **unrelated to income (r = .02)** but went with more financial stress, anxiety and comparison, and it eased with age ([Park, Ward & Naragon-Gainey 2017](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/2017_Park_etal_PSPB.pdf)). Prizing happiness itself predicted lower well-being in the US (r = −.26) but higher well-being in Russia (+.31) and East Asia (+.25), where people who pursue happiness tend to pursue it with others ([Ford et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658246)). Holding tightly to "I must handle everything myself" predicted poorer mental health and less help-seeking across 78 samples ([Wong et al. 2017](https://culturereframed.org/library-article/meta-analyses-of-the-relationship-between-conformity-to-masculine-norms-and-mental-health-related-outcomes/)). Brooding self-focus tracks neuroticism while curious self-reflection tracks openness ([summary of Trapnell & Campbell 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-absorption_paradox)), so the same daily question can soothe one person and stir another.

Age, gender and culture shape cravings less than people assume, and personality itself is more changeable. Sex explains less of the differences in values than age does, and much less than culture, with a median gap of about d = .15 across 70 countries ([Schwartz & Rubel 2005](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/sex-differences-in-value-priorities-cross-cultural-and-multimetho/)). Across 207 intervention studies, traits shifted by about **d = .37 over an average of 24 weeks**, mostly towards emotional stability, and the changes lasted ([Roberts et al. 2017](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/a-systematic-review-of-personality-trait-change-through-intervent/)). A randomised trial of a daily coaching app, PEACH, with 1,523 people produced self-reported change of about half a standard deviation in the direction people wanted over three months, still present three months later; friends and family confirmed the increases but not the decreases ([Stieger et al. 2021](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/261917/)). The decisive ingredient was doing. In a 15-week study of 377 students, **completing small weekly challenges predicted real change, while accepting challenges and not doing them predicted change in the opposite direction** ([Hudson et al.](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2018/11/Hudson-et-al.-2018.pdf)). Personality also shapes which self-help practices suit someone: in early studies, outgoing and open people gained more from positive exercises, people who chose and enjoyed an exercise gained more, and counting blessings once a week beat doing it three times a week ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)).

## Self-discovery helps when it is specific, kind and turned into action

**Learning about yourself helps when it describes what you did in a particular situation, is said warmly and accurately in a setting with nothing to lose, and leads to one small step you actually take. It backfires when it delivers verdicts, flatters, compares, or invites an endless "why am I like this?".** Across 607 effect sizes, feedback improved performance on average (d = .41), yet **more than a third of feedback made performance worse**, mainly when it pulled attention away from the task and onto the self through verdicts on the person, praise, threats to self-esteem or comparison with others ([summary of Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/kluger-denisi-1996/)).

Flattery is an old trap. In 1949, 39 students rated an identical description, largely copied from an astrology book, at **4.3 out of 5** for accuracy. Later work found that favourable statements and anything labelled "for you" are accepted more readily, that people struggle to tell their real personality profile from a fake one, and that people given astrological profiles later rated themselves higher on the traits described ([Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf)). A set of statements tends to be judged by its single worst one ([CORE thesis record](https://core.ac.uk/works/43440345)), so one generic or wrong line damages trust in everything around it. Self-knowledge is also uneven. People know their own inner states, such as anxiety, better than their friends do, but friends judge intelligence and creativity more accurately than the self ([Vazire 2010](https://source.washu.edu/2010/03/others-may-know-us-better-than-we-know-ourselves-study-finds/)). An app that only sees a person's own choices is on firm ground describing how situations feel and what the person tends to do, and on weak ground describing how clever, kind or likeable they are.

Hard truths become bearable under specific conditions. Privacy matters because people choose accurate feedback when rejection is not at stake ([Kwang & Swann 2010](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/10/kwangSwann2010.pdf)). Explaining *why* a criticism is given works dramatically. In US schools, a note saying "I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know that you can reach them" lifted essay revision among African-American students from 27% to 64%, and **among students who trusted their teachers least, from 0% to 82%** ([Yeager et al. 2014](https://sparq.stanford.edu/solutions/wise-critiques-help-students-succeed)). Self-compassion does not make people soft. After a self-kindness exercise, students studied longer following failure and were more motivated to fix a weakness ([Breines & Chen 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167212445599)). Self-compassion is tied to well-being at r = .47 across 79 samples ([Zessin et al. 2015](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Zessin.meta-analysis.pdf)) and to lower depression, anxiety and stress at r = −.54 ([MacBeth & Gumley 2012](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/64162)), and training in it cut self-criticism by moderate to large amounts across 27 trials ([Ferrari et al. 2019](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/87545/self-compassion-interventions-and-psychosocial-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-of-rcts)). The main obstacle is the belief that kindness to yourself breeds laziness; simply reading that self-compassion helps people pursue goals raised self-compassion a week later (d = .54) ([Chwyl et al. 2021](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Chwyl-et-al.-2021-Beliefs-About-Self-Compassion-Implications-for-Co.pdf)). Briefly affirming a core value before hearing threatening information gives small, reliable benefits in health studies (d = .17–.32) ([Epton et al. 2015](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/the-impact-of-self-affirmation-on-health-behavior-change-a-meta-a/)), but it failed a large, well-run school replication ([Hanselman et al. 2017](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/new-evidence-on-self-affirmation-effects-and-theorized-sources-of/)), so it is a garnish rather than a meal.

The line between helpful reflection and harmful brooding is clear. Students who reflected on themselves a lot reported *more* anxiety and stress, while those with clear insight reported less distress and better self-control, and the two were largely unrelated ([Grant, Franklin & Langford 2002](https://new.sbp-journal.com/index.php/sbp/article/view/1219)). Abstract, judging thoughts about causes and meanings ("why does this always happen to me?") slow recovery, while concrete thinking about what happened and what to do is far less harmful, and harm concentrates in people who are already low ([Watkins 2008](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/10871/11162/2/watkinsconstructiveunconstructiveRT.pdf)). Looking back on a hard moment "as a fly on the wall" reduced distress and later brooding more than reliving it, and the benefit grew with depressive symptoms ([Ayduk & Kross 2010](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/ayduk_kross_compass_2010.pdf)). Writing about feelings on its own has a real but tiny effect: r = .075 across 146 randomised studies ([Frattaroli 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073523/)).

Insight becomes change through specific if-then plans ("when X happens, I will do Y"), which improved goal attainment by **d = .65 across 94 tests** ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)). New habits typically take about two months (a median of 59–66 days), but the range runs from 4 to 335 days, and habits people choose for themselves grow stronger ([Singh et al. 2024](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006)). Missing a single day does not derail the process ([UCL on Lally et al.](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)). How people read difficulty decides whether they persist: when a goal feels like "me", difficulty signals that it matters; when it does not, difficulty signals "not for people like me" ([Oyserman 2015](https://dornsife.usc.edu/daphna-oyserman/wp-content/uploads/sites/232/2023/11/oyserman2015ibm.pdf)). A clear sense of who you are cushioned stress in three small studies ([Ritchie et al. 2011](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/self-concept-clarity-mediates-the-relation-between-stress-and-sub/)), but a Korean study found a *steady* sense of worth mattered more for anxiety and low mood than clarity itself ([Min 2012](https://accesson.kr/kpageneral/v.31/3/825/15854?lang=en)). Japanese participants score lower than Canadians on self-clarity, and the link with self-esteem is weaker in Japan ([UNT measurement study](https://psychology.unt.edu/system/files/MI%20of%20SCCS.pdf)); in interdependent cultures a self that shifts with the situation is normal, not a flaw.

## Six goals, six different routes

**Each of the six goals has its own lever, and self-knowledge does different work for each: it calibrates money habits, tests social fears, anchors a sense of safety, builds a record for hope, corrects pessimism about other people and redirects the chase for happiness.**

### Economic freedom comes from habits and honest self-knowledge, not money lessons

**Money freedom grows from everyday habits and confidence, from deciding before the tempting moment, and from loosening the link between money and self-worth. Knowing facts about money matters little, and no amount of self-knowledge substitutes for income.** In a nationally representative US survey of 6,394 people, financial skill (a standardised link of .40) and confidence in reaching money goals (.32) were strongly tied to good money habits such as paying bills on time and staying within budget, while financial knowledge added almost nothing (.04). Income, though, had the largest single link with people's actual financial situation (.40) ([CFPB 2018](https://gflec.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/bcfp_financial-well-being_pathways-role-financial-capability_research-brief.pdf?x98192)). In a New Zealand cohort of 1,037 people, childhood self-control predicted adult savings, home ownership and fewer money problems even after accounting for IQ and family class, and children whose self-control improved did better than their early scores predicted ([Moffitt et al. 2011](https://moffittcaspi.trinity.duke.edu/sites/moffittcaspi.trinity.duke.edu/files/Moffitt_PNAS_factsheet.pdf)). Preferring "now" over "later" went with higher credit-card debt among 541 low- and moderate-income Americans ([Meier & Sprenger 2010](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/banking/MeierSlides_NYU.pdf)).

Teaching people about money has small effects. One meta-analysis of 201 studies found that financial education explained just 0.1% of differences in behaviour, with effects fading to almost nothing within two years ([Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer 2014](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2333898)). A later review of 76 randomised trials with over 160,000 people found effects that were positive and about three times larger, but still small ([Kaiser et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27057)). Changing the moment of choice works better. In one company, 78% of employees who had refused advice to save more agreed to raise their savings with each future pay rise, and average saving rose from **3.5% to 11.6% of pay over 28 months** ([Thaler & Benartzi](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/faculty/shlomo.benartzi/savemore.htm)). In the Philippines, bank clients randomly offered an account that locked savings until a self-chosen goal or date held 81 percentage points more in savings after a year ([Ashraf, Karlan & Yin 2006](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps06-009)). Reminders that mentioned people's own savings goal raised goal completion in Bolivia, Peru and the Philippines ([Karlan et al. 2016](https://poverty-action.org/node/13096/pdf)).

This is where self-knowledge becomes decisive, for good or ill. In another Philippine trial, about **55% of people who signed up for a commitment savings plan defaulted** and lost money because the penalties they chose were too weak. People who accurately knew their own impatience took up the plan less, chose stronger penalties and defaulted less ([John 2020](https://poverty-action.org/publication/when-commitment-fails-%E2%80%93-evidence-field-experiment)). Partial self-knowledge plus a costly commitment can be worse than none, which argues for small, reversible, penalty-free steps. Inner economic freedom has its own enemy: staking self-worth on money. It appears at every income level and goes with more stress, more comparison and less felt freedom. In one study, a short reflection on another important value removed the dip in felt freedom after a money threat ([Park, Ward & Naragon-Gainey 2017](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/2017_Park_etal_PSPB.pdf)), and five follow-up studies linked it to less time with loved ones and more loneliness ([University at Buffalo 2020](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2020/04/014.html)). People also look away when money is tight. Icelanders checked their bank app less often when in debt ([Olafsson & Pagel 2017](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23945)), and people in debt have more than three times the odds of a mental-health problem, with the link probably running both ways ([University of Southampton 2013](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/psychology/news/2013/09/23_debt_linked_to_mental_health_problems.page)).

The ceiling must be stated plainly. Worldwide, only 56% of adults could reliably find extra money in an emergency ([World Bank Global Findex 2025](https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/globalfindex)). Poverty itself raises stress and tilts choices towards the short term; the leading review stresses that the driver is environment, not character, while calling the step from stress to choices its "weakest link" ([Haushofer & Fehr 2014](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf)). The popular "scarcity mindset" research replicated unevenly in a 20-study audit ([O'Donnell et al. 2021](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/publications/empirical-audit-and-review-and-an-assessment-of-evidentiary-value/)). Cash improves well-being modestly (d = 0.13 across 45 studies in low- and middle-income countries) ([McGuire, Kaiser & Bach-Mortensen 2022](https://www.inet.ox.ac.uk/publications/a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-the-impact-of-cash-transfers-on-subjective-well-being-and-mental-health-in-low-and-middle-income-countries)), and in the US higher income keeps adding a little everyday happiness, except among people who are unhappy for other reasons, whose gains stop at about $100,000 a year ([ScienceDaily on Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers 2023](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/03/230306163006.htm)). No trial has yet shown that reflection alone improves money outcomes.

### Social freedom grows when people test what they fear others think

**People gain social freedom by discovering that others notice, judge and resent them far less than they fear, and by recognising that freedom can mean freely chosen duty as much as standing apart.** Students wearing an embarrassing T-shirt guessed that about 46% of their peers noticed it; about 23% did ([Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707330/)). Across five experiments with more than 2,000 people, those who turned down an invitation overestimated how angry and disappointed the inviter would be and how much it would cost them future invitations; in a pilot, 77% admitted accepting invitations they did not want ([APA on Givi & Kirk 2023](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2023/12/say-no-invitation)). The lead author still advises against declining everything. After a first conversation, people underestimate how much the other person liked them, and the gap is biggest for shy people ([Boothby et al. 2018](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/23025/1/The%20Liking%20Gap%20in%20Conversations.pdf)).

Being yourself goes with feeling well: across 75 studies and 36,533 people, authenticity correlated r = .40 with well-being. The link varied with how individualist or collectivist the setting was, and most measures were built in Western cultures ([Sutton 2020](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/bafa6bd6-2e91-4e9a-9fb6-6d657d3faa7a)). Going along with a group runs higher in collectivist societies ([summary of Bond & Smith 1996](https://www.themantic-education.com/ibpsych/2016/10/04/key-study-bond-and-smith-1996/)), so "not caring what others think" is not a universal ideal. Feeling free to choose what you do with your life is one of six factors that together explain more than three-quarters of the differences in life ratings between countries ([World Happiness Report 2023](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2023/world-happiness-trust-and-social-connections-in-times-of-crisis/)). Online, a week of posting authentically lifted mood more than a week of posting a polished self in a 90-person experiment, and among 10,560 Facebook users, the closer people's online persona was to how they saw themselves, the higher their life satisfaction ([Bailey et al. 2020](https://doaj.org/article/f0311e5d5abd44b8833222bc3580802f)). For a minority the fear is clinical: social anxiety disorder affects about 4% of people over a lifetime across 28 countries ([Stein et al. 2017](https://bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12916-017-0889-2)), which is a reason to point those people towards support rather than coax them.

### Feeling safe with a new truth about yourself needs privacy, kindness and anchors

**People feel safe with a new truth about themselves when there is no risk of rejection or comparison, when the truth describes behaviour rather than character, when it comes with warmth and confidence that they can work with it, and when they have anchors (people, groups, routine, place and a steady sense of worth) that do not depend on the revelation.** The first three conditions come from the feedback research above ([Kwang & Swann 2010](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/10/kwangSwann2010.pdf); [Yeager et al. 2014](https://sparq.stanford.edu/solutions/wise-critiques-help-students-succeed)). Teaching teenagers that personalities can change lowered their sense of threat and their stress hormones during a social stress task, and improved coping in a preregistered follow-up, though effects were modest and US-only ([APS on Yeager, Lee & Jamieson 2016](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/for-teens-learning-that-personalities-change-buffers-against-stress.html)). That supports describing patterns as "lately" rather than "always".

Anchors are the fourth condition. Among English retirees who had belonged to two groups before retiring, the estimated risk of death over six years was **2% if they kept both, 5% if they lost one and 12% if they lost both**, a link comparable in size to that of exercise, although poor health may drive group loss as well as the reverse ([Steffens et al. 2016](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/2/e010164)). For people already low, joining three groups went with a 63% lower risk of relapse ([Cruwys et al. 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/socmed/v98y2013icp179-186.html)). Among unemployed people, coping resources such as a daily routine and social ties mattered more to mental health than education or demographics ([McKee-Ryan et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15641890/)). Places act like people: briefly recalling a place one feels attached to raised a sense of meaning, belonging and self-esteem in experiments ([Scannell 2013](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/44cfd21e-60da-47ff-87a2-0bd4ecf6149b/full); [Krause 2023](https://www.issep.org/features/place-attachment)). How people hold pride matters too. In Polish studies, pride in one's group that demands recognition from others predicted seeing outsiders as hostile, while calm satisfaction with one's group predicted more solidarity ([Dyduch-Hazar et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01901/text); [Federico, Golec de Zavala & Baran 2021](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/29216)).

Two warnings follow. Being "in between" in belief goes with lower well-being on average than firm belief or firm non-belief ([ISSEP feature](https://www.issep.org/features/nonreligious-worldviews-and-existential-certainty)), but that is often a passage rather than a fault, so security must never be bought by nudging people towards certainty. And for people facing stigma, hiding an identity can be a rational safety choice even though it carries a cost to well-being ([Bränström & Pachankis 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429389)). Material security also sets limits: the harm of losing work is larger where safety nets are weaker ([Paul & Moser 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)).

### Grounded hope rests on the person's own record, not on promises

**Grounded hope comes from seeing that you can act and can find a way, from naming what stands in the way and planning for it, and from renewing that view at natural fresh starts. An honest app can help with all three, but it cannot promise that everything will be all right.** Psychologists define hope as goals plus agency ("I can act") plus pathways ("I can see a way"), which is sturdier than optimism's general expectation that things will turn out well ([Lybbert & Wydick](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c13832.pdf)). Programmes designed to raise hope have small effects: d = .22 on hope, .16 on life satisfaction and **no measurable effect on distress (d = .04)** across 27 studies, with published studies looking better than unpublished ones ([Weis & Speridakos 2011](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/2211-1522-1-5)). Optimism goes with somewhat better health (an average effect of 0.17 across 83 studies) and about 35% fewer cardiovascular events across about 230,000 people, but these are associations, and no one knows whether trained optimism brings the same benefit ([Rasmussen, Scheier & Greenhouse 2009](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/3408d811-f9c3-4069-97ff-26a20cedf277); [Mount Sinai on Rozanski et al. 2019](https://www.mountsinai.org/about/newsroom/2019/optimism-linked-to-lower-risk-of-cardiovascular-events-and-death)).

Positive fantasy on its own backfires. In four studies of students and children, dreaming about a rosy future went with fewer depressive symptoms at the time but **predicted more symptoms and less effort months later** ([Oettingen, Mayer & Portnow 2016](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/oettingen-mayer-portnow-2016-published.pdf)). Pairing the wish with its obstacle and an if-then plan, the method known as WOOP, improved goal attainment by g ≈ .24–.34 across 15,907 people, and guided delivery beat written prompts ([Wang, Wang & Gai 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full)). Imagining your "best possible self" lifts mood and optimism, but the effect largely vanished within a week in the strictest review, and the online version had no effect on positive mood ([Heekerens 2020](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/handle/fub188/27309?show=full)); an earlier review was more optimistic ([Carrillo et al. 2019](https://repositori.uji.es/items/b1903fd5-5ff4-4010-8593-ef1f46e34e66/full)). Repeating "I'm a lovable person" made people with low self-esteem feel *worse* ([Wood, Perunovic & Lee 2009](https://contextualscience.org/publications/wood_perunovic_lee_2009)), while habitually accepting rather than fighting negative feelings predicted better mental health six months later ([Ford et al. 2018](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2jb8x65t)).

Hope responds to evidence from a person's own life and to timing. In psychotherapy, clients' life stories became more about their own agency, and **that shift came one to two sessions before their mental health improved**, not after ([Adler 2012](https://cdn2.psychologytoday.com/assets/2023-10/Adler%20%282012%29%20JPSP.pdf)). New beginnings prompt fresh starts: US university gym visits rose 33% at the start of a week and 47% at the start of a semester, and searches for "diet" rose 82% at the new year, though the boost fades ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)). Telling people that abilities can grow has small and contested effects on grades: d = 0.08 overall in one review ([Sisk et al. 2018](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20%282018%29%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf)) and d = 0.02 in the best-designed studies by the critics' count ([Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/2023-90931-004.pdf)), though a 2023 review found larger effects on mental health (d = 0.32) ([IFP summary of Psychological Bulletin 2023](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2023/meta-analyses-systematic-reviews/bul0000368/)). Hardship does not guarantee growth. When people were measured before and after events, real gains in self-esteem, relationships and mastery appeared after positive events as well as negative ones, meaning and spirituality did not grow, and most studies with comparison groups found no difference from people who had no such event ([Mangelsdorf, Eid & Luhmann 2018](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2019/01/Mangelsforf-Eid-Luhmann-2018.pdf)). In poorer countries, hope work succeeds where real opportunities exist. Ethiopian households shown documentaries about local people who had escaped poverty had higher aspirations and more investment in farming and their children's schooling five years later ([Oxford Economics on Bernard et al.](https://www.economics.ox.ac.uk/node/3545781)), but "where external constraints bind, psychological interventions may have little impact" ([Lybbert & Wydick](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c13832.pdf)).

Hopelessness demands a different response. Suicide kills more than 720,000 people a year, about **73% of them in low- and middle-income countries**, and it is the third leading cause of death among 15–29-year-olds ([WHO 2026](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/suicide)). Hopelessness roughly doubles the odds of suicidal thoughts, attempts and death, which is strong compared with other risk factors but weak in absolute terms, so it cannot be used to spot who is at risk ([Ribeiro et al. 2018](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/product/44413C7251A6471522724814003D813A/core-reader)). Asking people directly about suicide did not raise suicidal thinking in any of 13 studies ([Dazzi et al. 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FCAEE9E5BC840D76CF10AEBECD921AC9)). Safety-planning approaches cut suicidal behaviour by about 43%, one person helped for every 16 reached, even though the thoughts persisted ([Nuij et al. 2021](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/238354)). Self-guided digital tools reduced suicidal thinking only briefly, with no effect left at follow-up ([Torok et al. 2020](https://www.suicideinfo.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Suicide-prevention-using-self-guided-digital-interventions.pdf)).

### Faith in people returns through small tests, because pessimism never gets checked

**Faith in people is restored less by argument than by small tests, because people are systematically too gloomy about others' honesty, kindness and liking, and distrust that is never tested is never corrected.** In a field experiment in **355 cities across 40 countries**, 17,303 "lost" wallets were handed to staff at banks, museums and post offices. 40% came back when they held no money, 51% with a small sum and **72% with about $94**, and more money meant more returns in 38 of the 40 countries, the opposite of what 279 economists and experts predicted ([University of Michigan on Cohn et al. 2019](https://news.umich.edu/moral-concerns-override-desire-to-profit-from-finding-a-lost-wallet)). Across studies, actual wallet return ran at about **1.8 times** what people expected; in Toronto, people expected 23% to come back and over 80% did ([World Happiness Report 2025](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf)). Pessimism persists because of lopsided feedback: when you trust, you learn whether you were right, but when you withhold trust you never find out. Giving people feedback either way made them less cynical ([Fetchenhauer & Dunning 2010](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797609358586/)). People also overestimate how much self-interest drives others, by a medium-to-large margin in preregistered UK and US replications ([Brick et al. 2021](https://dare.uva.nl/id/6af3fc3c-0445-48ce-99bc-2a6724b0f0d6)).

People undervalue their own goodwill too. Writers of thank-you letters underestimated how happy recipients would feel and overestimated how awkward they would feel ([APS on Kumar & Epley 2018](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/uncategorized/people-underestimate-the-power-of-saying-thanks.html)). Givers of small kindnesses underestimated recipients' delight, and recipients then gave more to a stranger ($48.02 against $41.20 out of $100) ([Neuroscience News on Kumar & Epley 2022](https://neurosciencenews.com/kindness-outcome-21263/)). People giving compliments worried about their wording while recipients cared about the warmth ([Psychology Today on Zhao & Epley 2021](https://www.psychologytoday.com/za/blog/ulterior-motives/202110/you-probably-don-t-compliment-other-people-often-enough)). These experiments are mostly American. Kindness also rewards the giver: a small-to-medium well-being boost across 27 studies (δ = .28) ([Curry et al. 2018](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:4701365b-5760-4ece-a390-857a5f7b3c0c)), a link that is stronger for informal help, such as carrying a neighbour's shopping, than for formal volunteering across 201 studies and 198,213 people ([APA on Hui et al. 2020](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2020/09/doing-good-boosts-health)), and a link between spending on others and well-being in **120 of 136 countries** ([APA on Aknin et al. 2013](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2013/02/people-giving)). Helping strangers in 2024 stayed above pre-pandemic levels in every region, by 18% on average ([World Happiness Report 2025](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf)).

Cynicism is costly where it is unwarranted. Cynical beliefs about human nature predicted lower income nine years later in US and German panels, but the penalty shrank in countries where cynicism was realistic, with little prosocial behaviour or high homicide rates ([Stavrova & Ehlebracht 2016](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/aa10b12e-a926-4fe6-8e93-b6be81684b60)). Across about 200,000 people in 30 countries, cynics generally scored *worse* on cognitive tests, not better ([Stavrova & Ehlebracht 2019](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/098985fd-e45e-4389-9cde-6646333d3b26)). Trust is a feature of places as well as personalities. The share of people saying "most people can be trusted" ranges from **74% in Denmark to 2% in Zimbabwe**, with the UK at 43%, the US at 37% and India at 17% ([Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/self-reported-trust-attitudes?tab=table)), yet Indonesia, at under 5%, ranks first in the world for both donating and volunteering ([World Happiness Report 2025](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR25_Ch02.pdf)). Trusting strangers in the abstract and being kind in practice are different things. Expecting a lost wallet to come back goes with life ratings more than three-quarters of a point higher on a 0–10 scale, which the authors put above the effect of doubling income, and people who see others as fair and kind lose less well-being when unemployment, illness or discrimination strike (same source).

After a betrayal, the kind of breach matters. In experiments, apologies repaired trust after mistakes of competence better than after breaches of integrity, which were harder to mend ([WashU on Kim et al. 2004](https://source.washu.edu/2004/09/removing-the-shadow-of-suspicion/)). Structured forgiveness therapy increased forgiveness (SMD 0.55) and reduced depression (SMD 0.37) in randomised trials ([Akhtar & Barlow 2016](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:c4f1e260-c0e9-4ff1-b858-030826eeefbd)), although forgiving is not the same as reconciling or trusting again. Politics shows the same misjudgement. Americans believed the other party disliked and dehumanised them about twice as much as it did ([Moore-Berg et al. 2020](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/exaggerated-meta-perceptions-predict-intergroup-hostility-between/)); people in 26 countries overestimated how negatively the other side saw them ([Ruggeri et al. 2021](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-general-fault-in-our-fault-lines/)); and Americans overestimated the share of stereotyped groups in the other party by about 342%, though correcting that warmed feelings only modestly, by 6.4 points out of 100 ([Ahler & Sood](https://gsood.com/research/papers/partisanComposition.pdf)). Loneliness is the soil in which distrust grows. About 1 in 6 people worldwide are lonely, linked to an estimated **871,000 deaths a year**, with rates twice as high in low-income as in high-income countries ([WHO 2025](https://who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death)), and half of people across 142 countries had no interaction with a stranger in the previous week ([Gallup & Meta 2023](https://gallup.com/file/analytics/513347/Gallup-Meta-Global%20State%20of%20Social%20Connections%20Report-2023.pdf)).

### Contentment comes from people and 'good enough', not from chasing happiness

**Lasting contentment comes chiefly from relationships, from settling for good enough rather than the best, and from pursuing connection rather than happiness itself. The practices sold as happiness boosters give small lifts that fade unless they are kept up.** In the Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed Harvard students and young Boston adults since 1938, satisfaction with relationships in midlife predicted who was healthy and happy at 80 better than cholesterol levels did ([Harvard Gazette 2023](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you)). Happiness is not fixed at a "set point": on average people do not fully return to their earlier level after divorce, unemployment or disability, while the lift from marriage fades within a couple of years ([APS on Lucas](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/new-study-reports-on-the-state-of-human-happiness.html)). Treating happiness as a target backfires in some cultures. American women who prized happiness felt worse after a happy film, through disappointment with their own feelings ([Mauss et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3160511/)). In a preregistered German study of 1,178 people, those whose plans for becoming happier were social, such as more time with friends and family, were more satisfied a year later, while other active pursuits made no difference ([Rohrer et al. 2018](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797618761660/)). Socially engaged ideas of happiness predicted well-being in the US, Germany, Russia and East Asia alike ([Ford et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658246)).

Positive psychology exercises help a little. Across 39 randomised trials, they lifted well-being by d = .34 straight afterwards and .22 three to six months later ([Bolier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3599475)); correcting for small-study bias cut the effect to about r = .10, with no reliable effect on depression ([White, Uttl & Holder 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216588)). A broader review of 347 studies in 41 countries found g = .39, but it included longer, clinical programmes ([Carr et al. 2021](https://stafforini.com/works/carr-2021-effectiveness-of-positive/)). Gratitude exercises reduced depression and anxiety only slightly (g = −.29) and were not much better than neutral activities such as writing about a class timetable ([Ohio State News on Cregg & Cheavens](https://news.osu.edu/gratitude-interventions-dont-help-with-depression-anxiety/)). Savouring, deliberately taking in good moments, showed a moderate effect (g = .51) across 20 trials in one new review, with no differences by culture, though that estimate is probably inflated ([Chen et al. 2026](https://www.synapsesocial.com/papers/69b258a396eeacc4fcec880f)). In the same early research on fit, mildly depressed people benefited more from simple pleasant activities than from reflective ones ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)). Meaning and happiness overlap only about half: giving, effort and worry for others raise meaning while sometimes lowering momentary happiness ([Baumeister et al. 2013](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2013-meaning-vs-happiness.pdf)), so a content life need not feel cheerful from moment to moment. Interventions built on growth and purpose raise a sense of mastery, growth and self-acceptance most, and autonomy and relationships least ([van Dierendonck & Lam 2023](https://scholars.ln.edu.hk/en/publications/interventions-to-enhance-eudaemonic-psychological-well-being-a-me/)).

The 2026 World Happiness Report, devoted to social media, sharpens the picture. Among more than 270,000 15-year-olds in 47 countries, heavy users (seven or more hours a day) reported lower well-being than light users, a gap of almost a full point out of 10 for girls in Western Europe and roughly zero for boys outside Western Europe. Communicating, learning and creating went with higher satisfaction, while scrolling feeds went with lower. **Moving from low to high belonging at school mattered about four to six times more than cutting social media from high to low** ([World Happiness Report 2026](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)). The much-discussed decline in young people's happiness is regional: under-25s are happier now than in 2006–2010 in 85 of 136 countries, with the falls concentrated in North America, Australia, New Zealand and Western Europe ([Gallup on the World Happiness Report 2026](https://www.gallup.com/analytics/349487/world-happiness-report.aspx)).

## Across twenty identities, the same thing gives meaning and applies pressure

**Every part of life people identify with feeds the same core cravings and can also trap them, and four questions recur everywhere: how many ties a person has and whether they survive change, whether a role is chosen or imposed, whether worth depends on other people's recognition, and how accurately the person pictures outsiders.** The evidence for each area varies a great deal in strength and in how global it is, so each paragraph below starts with the plain answer and then shows what supports it.

### Roots: culture, heritage, language, nationality, age and generation

**Culture and heritage** give pride, meaning and belonging, and the main threat is being treated unfairly for them again and again. Across 184 studies, feeling positively connected to one's ethnic heritage went with modestly higher well-being (r = .17), about twice as strongly for self-esteem and positive feelings as for reducing distress ([Smith & Silva 2011](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/88)), and among minority youth positive feelings about their group went with fewer depressive symptoms and better schooling across 46 studies ([Rivas-Drake et al. 2014](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/feeling-good-happy-and-proud-a-meta-analysis-of-positive-ethnic-r/)). Perceived discrimination went with lower well-being across 144,246 people (r = −.23), and experiments showed that what harms is feeling that unfair treatment is *pervasive*; attributing a single bad event to discrimination did not ([Schmitt et al. 2014](https://openworks.wooster.edu/facpub/344)). Culture also changes what freedom looks like. A test across 55 cultural groups in 33 nations found no simple East-versus-West split, but many ways of being both independent and interdependent ([Vignoles et al. 2016](https://eprints.ucm.es/id/eprint/47790/)), and cultural gaps are often smaller than assumed ([Oyserman et al. 2002](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/89926/1/oysermancoonkemmelmeier2002.pdf)). Self-knowledge here means noticing what one's heritage gives, never being told one's culture explains one's choices.

**Language** keeps people close to family and to identity, and its power lies in relationships rather than drills. Among Mexican-origin US teenagers followed for six years, almost half kept high Spanish skills; warmth with their mothers predicted keeping the language more than how much Spanish was spoken at home, and high skill went with stronger identity, resilience and meaning ([UT Austin brief on Wang et al. 2022](https://liberalarts.utexas.edu/prc/our-research/prc-brief-series/family-demography-and-human-development/heritage-language-development-and-well-being.html)). For people living between cultures, "both/and" works best: across 5,366 immigrant youth in 13 countries, those who kept their heritage *and* built ties to the new society adapted best, and those weakly tied to both fared worst ([Berry et al. 2006](https://research-portal.najah.edu/migrant/20477/)), a pattern confirmed across 83 studies of about 23,000 bicultural people ([Nguyen & Benet-Martínez 2013](https://psychology.fullerton.edu/amnguyen/pdf/jccp2013.pdf)). Switching between cultural worlds is a skill, not a symptom.

**Nationality** gives belonging and pride, and the trap is pride that needs others to acknowledge it. Polish studies distinguish calm satisfaction with one's nation from "collective narcissism", the belief that one's group is exceptional but not recognised enough; the two overlap but predict opposite things, with the second predicting nationalism and hostile assumptions about outsiders ([Federico, Golec de Zavala & Bu 2022](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/31995/1/Political%20Psychology%20-%202022%20-%20Federico%20-%20Collective%20Narcissism%20as%20a%20Basis%20for%20Nationalism.pdf)). This evidence is from one country and never measured personal happiness, so it supports a neutral question about *how* pride is held in any group, not a claim that one kind of patriot is happier.

**Age** brings perspective and calmer priorities, and seeing ageing only as loss is the trap. Feeling younger than one's years went with fewer depressive symptoms and higher well-being across 45,329 older adults (r = .18), more strongly in collectivist samples ([Alonso Debreczeni & Bailey 2020](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/153397/2/AlonsoDebreczeniandBaileyaccepted.pdf)), and positive views of one's own ageing predicted slightly better later health across 107 long-term studies ([Westerhof et al. 2023](https://ifp.nyu.edu/?p=1671294)). Both links are small and partly run backwards, since healthier people feel younger. Staking self-worth on money fades with age ([Park et al. 2017](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/2017_Park_etal_PSPB.pdf)). About one in two people across 57 countries hold ageist views ([WHO 2021](https://www.who.int/news/item/18-03-2021-ageism-is-a-global-challenge-un)).

**Generation** predicts far less than headlines suggest. Across 20 studies of 19,961 workers, generational differences in work attitudes were "small or near zero in virtually all cases", and age, era and birth cohort are nearly impossible to separate ([Neoacademic on Costanza et al. 2012](https://neoacademic.com/2012/12/05/millenial-workers-no-different-from-anyone-else-a-meta-analysis/)). One exception deserves watching: in European data, internet use was linked strongly negatively to well-being for Gen Z, near zero for Gen X and slightly positively for Baby Boomers ([World Happiness Report 2026](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)). The honest stance is to infer nothing about a person from their generation.

### Livelihood: work, money, social class and education

**Work** supplies meaning, structure, status and belonging, which is exactly why compulsive work and a self resting on one role are dangerous. Feeling one's work is meaningful correlated at r = .70 or more with engagement and job satisfaction across 23,144 people ([Allan et al. 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jomstd/v56y2019i3p500-528.html)), while pay is only weakly linked to job satisfaction (r ≈ .15) ([Judge et al. 2010](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-relationship-between-pay-and-job-satisfaction-a-meta-analysis/)). People who reshape their jobs by adding skills, support or autonomy are markedly more engaged (ρ ≈ .59), whereas coping mainly by cutting things out goes with *more* strain and more thoughts of quitting ([Rudolph et al. 2017](https://www.mentorcoach.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Rudolph-Katz-Lavigne-Zacher-2017-job-crafting-meta-analysis-due-by-week-5.pdf)). Compulsive work goes with stress, burnout and poor health but **not with better performance** ([QIC-WD summary of Clark et al. 2016](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Workaholism%20101921.pdf)), and working 55 or more hours a week carries 35% higher stroke risk ([WHO & ILO 2021](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo)). Losing work causes harm: 34% of unemployed people had psychological problems against 16% of employed people, worst where safety nets are weak ([Paul & Moser 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)). Retirement is no simple relief or loss: across 19 studies, about a third found higher life satisfaction, nearly half lower and a fifth no link ([Ejike, Kenni & Sunday 2024](https://www.mentalhealthdataprizeafrica.aphrc.org/repository/paper/35314/paradox-of-life-after-work-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-on-retirement-anxiety-and-life-satisfaction)). Most of the world's workers, more than 61% and 85.8% in Africa, are informal, without contracts or protection ([ILO 2018](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/more-60-cent-worlds-employed-population-are-informal-economy)), so "your boss" and "your career" are the wrong words for most users.

**Money** as identity is covered above under economic freedom; the identity point is that the *relationship* to money matters more than the amount. People whose spending matched their personality were more satisfied with life, a stronger link than total income or spending ([Matz et al. 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797616635200)), and people told to spend on someone else felt happier that evening than those who spent on themselves ([Dunn, Aknin & Norton 2008](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/money-can-buy-happiness-if-you-spend-it-on-other-people)). Life satisfaction tracks income *rank* among similar people more than its amount ([Boyce et al. 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797610362671)), and learning that colleagues earn more made lower-paid staff less satisfied without making higher-paid staff happier ([Card et al. 2012](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16396)).

**Social class** works like a culture, and its strengths can become blind spots. Fewer resources and lower rank tend to orient people outwards, towards reading others, seeing outside constraints and relying on one another, while abundance orients people inwards, towards personal goals and control ([Kraus et al. 2012](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/social_class_solipsism_contextualism.pdf)). Working-class children were more likely to stay quiet than "bother" a teacher, and first-generation students did worse when university was framed as "pave your own path" but as well as their peers when it was framed around family and collaboration ([APA Monitor 2015](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/02/class-differences)). A one-hour panel in which older students linked their stories to their class backgrounds shrank the first-year grade gap by 63%, mainly by making first-generation students more willing to seek help ([Stephens, Hamedani & Destin 2014](https://edpolicy.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/publications/closing-social-class-achievement-gap.pdf)); an independent review rated it as meeting its standards without reservations ([What Works Clearinghouse 2026](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/study/78652)). A belonging exercise for 26,911 students at 22 institutions raised first-year completion by about 2 percentage points, **but only where the institution actually offered chances to belong** ([Stanford News on Walton et al. 2023](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2023/05/improving-student-success-social-belonging)). Claims that richer people are less generous have not held up in large surveys ([Korndörfer et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0133193)).

**Education** feeds competence and hope, but people judge their ability against whoever is around them. Across 1.28 million students worldwide, being in a higher-achieving class or school went with feeling *less* able (the "big fish, little pond" effect) ([Fang et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01569/full)). People who believed passions are found rather than developed lost interest faster when things got hard ([O'Keefe, Dweck & Walton 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797618780643)). Adult learning is unequal: 87% of US graduates versus 60% of those with high school or less had done personal learning in the past year ([Pew 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2016/03/22/lifelong-learning-and-technology/)). Whether adult learning raises well-being was not established in the evidence gathered, so the app should not claim it.

### Close ties and the body: family roles, relationships, gender, body and sexuality

**Family roles** reliably add meaning, not reliably happiness, and duty turns heavy mainly when it comes without any sense of control. Parents consistently report more meaning in life, but whether they are happier than non-parents depends on age, partner status, and whether one is a mother or father ([Nelson, Kushlev & Lyubomirsky 2014](http://sonjalyubomirsky.com/files/2012/09/Nelson-Kushlev-Lyubomirsky-in-press1.pdf)), and across 22 rich countries the gap disappeared where paid leave and affordable childcare were common, with parents happier in Norway and Hungary ([Glass, Simon & Andersson 2016](https://contemporaryfamilies.utah.edu/publications/posts/2016/june/brief-parenting-happiness.php)). Among 752 teenagers of Latin American, Asian and European backgrounds, helping the family each day was generally not stressful and went with more happiness through a sense of fulfilling a valued role ([Telzer & Fuligni 2009](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/daily-family-assistance-and-the-psychological-well-being-of-adole/)). Among 250 Arab adult children caring for elderly parents in Israel, a strong sense of filial duty went with lower burden, and a sense of mastery protected against depression ([Khalaila & Litwin 2011](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/does-filial-piety-decrease-depression-among-family-caregivers/)). Across 228 studies, the hardest part of caregiving was the cared-for person's difficult behaviour ([Pinquart & Sörensen 2003](https://psychsoc.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/58/2/P112)). Extended-family households are the most common arrangement worldwide, at 38% against 11% in the US ([Pew 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/12/13/key-findings-how-living-arrangements-vary-by-religious-affiliation-around-the-world/)).

**Relationships** give love and security, and the traps are fear of being alone and losing oneself in or after a partnership. Partnered people report somewhat higher life satisfaction on average, but the link varies by country and even reverses in some; people are already happier before marrying and unhappier before divorcing, and single people thrive when they have good friendships, little fear of being single, low attachment anxiety and little family pressure, which weighs more in interdependent cultures ([Girme, Park & MacDonald 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475216)). After a break-up, people often feel less sure who they are, and that blurring goes with more sadness ([ScienceDaily on Slotter et al. 2010](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/03/100308132139.htm)). In couples, feeling that one's partner is committed and appreciative predicted relationship happiness far more than either person's personality across 43 studies of over 11,000 couples ([Joel et al. 2020](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/machine-learning-uncovers-the-most-robust-self-report-predictors--3/)).

**Gender** shapes which pressures people carry, and the most damaging pattern is one anyone can hold: "I must handle it alone". Across 78 samples and 19,453 people, conformity to self-reliance, power over women and "playboy" norms went with poorer mental health and less help-seeking, whereas putting work first did not ([Wong et al. 2017](https://culturereframed.org/library-article/meta-analyses-of-the-relationship-between-conformity-to-masculine-norms-and-mental-health-related-outcomes/)). Women do about 76% of the world's unpaid care work ([ILO 2018](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-calls-urgent-action-prevent-looming-global-care-crisis-0)), and men report higher self-esteem than women on average, with the gap largest in rich Western countries ([APA on Bleidorn et al. 2016](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2016/01/self-esteem-gender)). Across 28 European countries, transgender people in places with more stigma hid their identity more and were less satisfied with life, yet hiding also partly shielded them from discrimination ([Bränström & Pachankis 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429389)).

**The body** gives capability and comfort, and comparing appearance is the main trap. Across 240 papers, body appreciation, valuing the body regardless of how it looks, went with higher self-esteem and self-compassion and less depression, anxiety and disordered eating, even after accounting for negative body image ([Linardon et al. 2022](https://europe.as.uky.edu/bibcite/reference/29503)). Viewing idealised social-media images worsened body image moderately in experiments (g = −.61), though the long-term link with overall social-media use is very small ([de Valle et al. 2021](https://www.uloyola.es/en/research-transfer/publications/social-media-body-image-and-the-question-of-causation-meta-analyses-of-experimental-and-longitudinal-evidence-article)). Writing about what the body can do improved body appreciation in randomised trials ([Alleva et al. 2015](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/expand-your-horizon-a-programme-that-improves-body-image-and-redu)), including **a single 15-minute session that lifted mothers' body appreciation from 50.5 to 70.9 out of 100**, against 47.7 to 55.3 in a comparison group, with the largest gains among the least self-compassionate ([Granfield et al. 2023](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/48199/1/1-s2.0-S1740144523001912-main.pdf)).

**Sexuality** links closeness and self-esteem, and stigma and hiding are the traps. Among more than 11,000 Germans followed for 12 years, self-esteem and sexual satisfaction rose and fell together in both directions, as strongly for single people as for partnered ones ([University of Zurich on Weber et al. 2024](https://news.uzh.ch/en/articles/media/2024/Self-esteem.html)), and more open communication about sex went with better sexual well-being across 48 studies ([PsyPost on Mallory et al. 2019](https://www.psypost.org/couples-who-communicate-more-about-sex-tend-to-have-better-sex-study-finds)). The minority-stress framework places much of the higher rate of mental disorder among lesbian, gay and bisexual people in a hostile social environment rather than in the person ([Meyer 2003](https://www.hhri.org/?p=35900)), and among 174,209 men who have sex with men in 38 European countries, more stigma meant labels, attraction and behaviour lined up less often ([Pachankis et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5346459)). The labels people use are shaped by safety, so nothing should ever be inferred from answers.

### Belief and public life: faith and politics

**Faith and belief** give meaning, community and hope, and their benefit depends on fit with one's surroundings and on community rather than on the content of the belief. Across 65 countries and 2.2 million people, being religious boosted self-esteem in religious cultures but did little in secular ones ([Gebauer et al. 2016](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/393348)). In 24 countries, highly religious people, non-religious people and atheists did not differ in life satisfaction once fit with their country's norms was accounted for; weakly religious people were the least satisfied, and atheists fared worse only in religious societies ([Pöhls, Schlösser & Fetchenhauer 2020](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/nonreligious-identities-and-life-satisfaction-questioning-the-uni/)). Across 26 countries, actively religious people were more often very happy, but inactive members were no happier than the unaffiliated in 24 of 26 ([Pew 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2019/01/31/religions-relationship-to-happiness-civic-engagement-and-health-around-the-world/)); the religious advantage is largest where life is hard and religion is the norm ([University of Mannheim on Diener, Tay & Myers 2011](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/forschung-erleben/artikel/du-hast-doch-alles-brauchst-du-dann-noch-einen-gott/)). Leaving one's childhood religion went with lower US well-being, fully explained by lost attendance ([Fenelon & Danielsen 2016](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/leaving-my-religion-understanding-the-relationship-between-religi/)). Drawing comfort from faith went with better adjustment, while spiritual struggle, feeling punished or abandoned, went with more distress ([Ano & Vasconcelles 2005](https://www.uned.es/universidad/inicio/dam/jcr:fd37f522-5523-47c7-9f9f-821180678483/ANOYVA~1.PDF)), a link repeated across 24 countries in the pandemic (r = .29) ([Cheng & Ying 2023](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1097598/text)).

**Politics** gives belonging and purpose, and its main cost comes from an imagined other side. Partisanship increasingly works as a social identity that spills into friendships and family ([Iyengar et al. 2019](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-polisci-051117-073034)), although over four decades hostility to the other side rose most in the US and fell in six of 12 rich countries ([Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v106y2024i2p557-565.html)). In a US survey, nearly 40% said politics stressed them out and about 20% said it had damaged friendships ([University of Nebraska on Smith, Hibbing & Hibbing 2019](https://news.unl.edu/article/stressed-out-americans-making-themselves-sick-over-politics)). Across 48 markets, 40% now sometimes or often avoid the news, up from 29% in 2017, citing powerlessness and toxic arguments among their reasons ([Reuters Institute 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary)). More contact with other groups goes with less prejudice across 515 studies in 38 nations ([Pettigrew & Tropp 2008](https://courses.washington.edu/pbafhall/563/Readings/pettigrew.pdf)), and non-judgemental exchanges of personal stories reduced prejudice for months ([Kalla & Broockman 2020](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps20-01)), while cutting like-minded Facebook content by a third did not reduce polarisation ([Syracuse on Nyhan et al. 2023](https://www.maxwell.syr.edu/research/article/like-minded-sources-on-facebook-are-prevalent-but-not-polarizing)). A person's news rhythm and their picture of the other side are neutral, side-free things to reflect on.

### Where and how people spend themselves: place and home, nature, hobbies, sport and online life

**Place and home** work like an attachment to a person, offering safety, belonging and meaning, and grief when the place is lost or changes. People described 13 kinds of benefit from places they feel attached to, from memories and belonging to comfort, security and freedom, and recalling such a place after a threat came faster to mind, suggesting a security function ([Scannell 2013](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/44cfd21e-60da-47ff-87a2-0bd4ecf6149b/full)). In experiments with 200, 274 and 472 people, recalling an attached place raised the sense that life makes sense and has meaning ([Krause 2023](https://www.issep.org/features/place-attachment)). A small student review found attachment mostly helpful but deepening distress after disasters or upheaval, with its benefit carried by social ties ([Beautell Sánchez 2025](https://riull.ull.es/xmlui/handle/915/42464?show=full)). Older Americans who saw their neighbourhood as close-knit were less likely to have a heart attack over four years ([Kim, Hawes & Smith 2014](https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/bibcite/reference/12901)). The evidence here is early and mostly North American, and nothing reliable was found on displacement.

**Nature** gives vitality and calm with few traps beyond unequal access. Feeling that nature is part of who you are went with modestly higher happiness across 8,523 people (r = .19), most strongly for vitality (.24) and for the measure closest to identity (.27) ([Capaldi, Dopko & Zelenski 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00976/full)), and people spending at least two hours a week in nature were more likely to report good health and well-being ([White et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-44097-3)). Most samples were Canadian and American.

**Hobbies** give mastery, joy and company, and the trap is turning enjoyment into duty. Among 93,263 adults aged 65 and over in 16 countries including China and Japan, having a hobby went with fewer depressive symptoms and more happiness and life satisfaction, small effects that held across countries ([Mak et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-023-02506-1)). Among 447 Chinese marathon runners, committed leisure correlated with life satisfaction (r = .46), entirely through satisfaction with the activity itself ([Tian et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.581908/full)), and people who exercise because they enjoy it keep it up longer across 66 studies ([Teixeira et al. 2012](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8677w/exercise-physical-activity-and-self-determination-theory-a-systematic-review)). A WHO review of over 3,000 studies found arts participation supports health and well-being ([Fancourt & Finn 2019](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789289054553)), and games draw people in when they make players feel capable, free and connected ([Przybylski et al. 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1037/a0019440)).

**Sport** gives belonging and borrowed success, and moods tied to results and bias against rivals are the traps. Young people who play sport, especially team sport, have higher self-esteem, better social skills and less low mood across 30 studies ([Eime et al. 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1479-5868-10-98)). Identifying with a team went with feeling socially connected (r = .33 to .54), whether or not the team was local ([Schellenberg & Gaudreau 2021](https://scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2628)). Highly identified fans who watched their team win rated their own self-esteem and future performance higher, much as after a personal success, and fans leaving a game judged rival fans' behaviour more harshly than their own side's ([APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/sports-complex-the-science-behind-fanatic-behavior)). Sport is a low-stakes way to reflect on how any group identity both connects and divides.

**Online life** gives connection and expression, and passive comparison and a polished self are the traps. Screen time on its own explained at most 0.4% of the differences in teenagers' well-being ([Orben & Przybylski 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1)), but how people use it matters, with scrolling worse than communicating or creating ([World Happiness Report 2026](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/executive-summary-happiness-and-social-media/)). Americans paid to stop using Facebook for four weeks became slightly happier and spent more time with family and friends, but knew less news ([Allcott et al. 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20190658)). People who underestimate how often others feel bad, easy to do when feeds are curated, feel lonelier and less satisfied ([Jordan et al. 2011](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167210390822)), while posting closer to one's real self goes with better mood ([Bailey et al. 2020](https://doaj.org/article/f0311e5d5abd44b8833222bc3580802f)).

## What this means for the app

**The evidence supports an app that asks about ordinary moments, reflects back what the person did and what it suggests they were reaching for, names downsides kindly and rarely, offers one small chosen step at a time, and points people outwards to other people, while promising noticing and small shifts rather than transformation.** Its rules against comparison, diagnosis and taking sides are not just ethics: they are the conditions under which people trust and use what they learn about themselves.

### Ask what people did, and make every option respectable

The questions that work best ask about behaviour in a concrete moment, because concrete thinking helps and abstract "why" thinking harms ([Watkins 2008](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/10871/11162/2/watkinsconstructiveunconstructiveRT.pdf)), and because behaviour is what the app can honestly describe. Every option should be one a self-respecting person could choose, so that duty and independence, calm and excitement, belief and unbelief are equally valid answers ([Chirkov et al. 2003](https://scholarship.miami.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Differentiating-Autonomy-From-Individualism-and-Independence/991031598656802976); [Tsai et al. 2025](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/entities/publication/d4fbbc23-48dc-4d47-8232-efe6fe572280)). Questions should include honest low options ("nothing went right today") rather than designing out the bad days, every identity question needs a "doesn't apply" or "I'd rather not say", and the app should never ask which religion, party, ethnicity or nationality someone holds. A short follow-up on *why* a choice was made ("because I wanted to", "I'd feel guilty otherwise", "it was expected") separates freely chosen duty from pressure, which is the heart of freedom. Any question about a hoped-for future should be followed by a question about what stands in the way, because wishing alone predicts less effort ([Oettingen, Mayer & Portnow 2016](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/oettingen-mayer-portnow-2016-published.pdf)). The examples below are design suggestions built on the findings cited in this report; none has itself been tested as a measure.

| What it reveals | Situation | Options |
|---|---|---|
| Freedom as felt choice | Who decided how you spent your free evening? | I did / someone else, and I was fine with it / someone else, and I wasn't / no one, it just happened |
| Security and predictability | A plan you counted on changes an hour before. | Re-plan at once / go with it / feel a bit lost and wait / feel relieved |

### Let insights describe moments and patterns, never verdicts

**An insight after one answer can honestly describe that moment; a weekly or monthly mirror can honestly describe counts and contrasts in the person's own answers; neither can honestly assign a trait, rank the person or promise an outcome.** The safest structure runs from situation to choice to tendency ("When the plan changed, you went along with it; in moments like that, keeping the peace seems to come first for you"), because verdicts on the person are where feedback backfires ([summary of Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/kluger-denisi-1996/)) and labels can become self-fulfilling ([Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf)). Insights should name the route as well as the craving ("you often meet the wish to be respected by doing things well rather than by being noticed"). General research should be cited as what happens "in studies of many people", in hedged words ("tends to", "often"), and never as a comparison with others. Downsides should be rationed, anchored in something the person's own answers show they value, explained ("we mention this because it seems to matter to you") and framed as workable, which is the formula that turned 0% revision into 82% among low-trust students ([Yeager et al. 2014](https://sparq.stanford.edu/solutions/wise-critiques-help-students-succeed)). For the hardest truths, a step-back frame ("if you watched this week from the outside…") reduces sting ([Ayduk & Kross 2010](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/ayduk_kross_compass_2010.pdf)).

A good insight is one the person could reasonably mark "not me". Positive and negative statements should meet the same evidence threshold, low-confidence statements should be dropped because one bad line spoils a set ([CORE thesis record](https://core.ac.uk/works/43440345)), and insights should stick to how situations feel and what the person tends to do, not how clever, kind or likeable they are ([Vazire 2010](https://source.washu.edu/2010/03/others-may-know-us-better-than-we-know-ourselves-study-finds/)). Any "that's me" rating the app collects will be inflated by flattery and personal labelling, so it proves little; the real test of specificity is whether people can pick out insights drawn from their own answers when they are mixed with insights drawn from someone else's. Mirrors should fall on fresh starts, such as Monday, the first of the month and optionally the person's own cultural new years, should tell the story of moments the person chose, tried, adapted or asked for help ([Adler 2012](https://cdn2.psychologytoday.com/assets/2023-10/Adler%20%282012%29%20JPSP.pdf)), should show change only against the person's own past, and should close themes rather than reopen them, because more self-focus is not more insight ([Grant, Franklin & Langford 2002](https://new.sbp-journal.com/index.php/sbp/article/view/1219)). A hard month should be called hard, with one thing the person did, not turned into a growth story. Mirrors should never score how happy someone was ([Mauss et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3160511/)).

|---|---|

Some craving patterns deserve a question rather than a statement. If someone keeps choosing the money or safety option, the app can ask whether something else feels uncertain right now, since such cravings can stand in for frustrated core needs ([Vansteenkiste, Ryan & Soenens 2020](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/2020_VansteenkisteRyanSoenens_BPNSIntro_MOEM.pdf)), but it should never assert this. Insights about money trouble, unfair treatment, unemployment or not fitting in should say plainly that this is hard for most people in that situation, because the causes are largely structural ([Haushofer & Fehr 2014](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Fehr_Science_2014.pdf); [Schmitt et al. 2014](https://openworks.wooster.edu/facpub/344); [Paul & Moser 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)).

### Offer one small step the person chooses and does

**Steps work when there is only one at a time, when the person picks it, when it is phrased as "when this moment comes, I'll do this", when it is built from the cue in the question they just answered, and when the app counts it as done only when it is done.** If-then plans have the strongest evidence of any step format ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)); self-chosen habits grow stronger ([Singh et al. 2024](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006)); and accepting a step without doing it predicted change in the wrong direction ([Hudson et al.](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2018/11/Hudson-et-al.-2018.pdf)). Steps must carry no penalties or streak pressure, since self-chosen penalties left most defaulters worse off ([John 2020](https://poverty-action.org/publication/when-commitment-fails-%E2%80%93-evidence-field-experiment)), and the app should say honestly that habits usually take a couple of months and a missed day undoes nothing. Steps must be possible without money, time or privileges the person may lack. When answers suggest a low patch, simple pleasant steps beat reflective ones ([Lyubomirsky & Layous 2013](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt079475fr/qt079475fr.pdf)), and a "best possible self" prompt should never appear after a heavy answer and never without an obstacle-and-plan follow-up. If a step feels hard, the app can say that is usually a sign it matters ([Oyserman 2015](https://dornsife.usc.edu/daphna-oyserman/wp-content/uploads/sites/232/2023/11/oyserman2015ibm.pdf)).

### Treat every heavy answer as a reason to point to people

**When an answer is heavy (hopeless, rejected, controlled, worthless, frightened), the app should stop offering clever insights, acknowledge the feeling plainly, and point calmly to human help in the person's own country and language, every time and without trying to score risk.** Hopelessness is too weak a predictor to sort people safely ([Ribeiro et al. 2018](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/product/44413C7251A6471522724814003D813A/core-reader)), so silence below a threshold would miss people; asking directly and gently about suicidal thoughts does not raise them ([Dazzi et al. 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FCAEE9E5BC840D76CF10AEBECD921AC9)); and the elements of a safety plan (warning signs, what helps, who to contact) reduce suicidal behaviour even when the thoughts persist ([Nuij et al. 2021](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/238354)). Positive-future content, fresh-start encouragement and "things will get better" lines should pause after such answers. Because self-guided digital tools have only brief effects ([Torok et al. 2020](https://www.suicideinfo.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Suicide-prevention-using-self-guided-digital-interventions.pdf)), the app should present itself as a bridge to people, not as the help. With most suicides occurring in low- and middle-income countries ([WHO 2026](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/suicide)), a single US or UK helpline fails most users. Repeated answers about rejection deserve particular care, since feeling left out was the strongest correlate of low mood across four countries ([Chen et al. 2015](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/2014_Chen-et-al._need-satisfaction.pdf)), as do answers about debt and job loss, which are linked to suicide risk and doubled rates of psychological problems ([University of Southampton 2013](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/psychology/news/2013/09/23_debt_linked_to_mental_health_problems.page); [Paul & Moser 2009](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117729304)). If an answer suggests abuse, coercion or danger, the app must never offer "trust more" or "forgive" steps.

### What to avoid, and why

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| Trait labels, type names and anything diagnostic: "people-pleaser", "avoider", "anxious attachment", "scarcity mindset", "burnout", "narcissist" | Verdicts on the person make feedback backfire, labels can become self-fulfilling, and the app is not therapy |
| Any comparison with other users, averages, percentiles or rankings | Comparison is a documented cause of feedback backfiring; status and satisfaction depend on local rank ([Boyce et al. 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797610362671)) |
| Vague or flattering insights that would fit almost anyone | People accept them without their being true, and one bad line spoils trust in the rest |
| Making happiness a target, or scoring how happy someone was | Prizing happiness backfires in some cultures; maximum happiness is a WEIRD ideal ([Krys et al. 2025](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/happiness-maximization-is-a-weird-way-of-living/)) |
| Forced cheer: affirmations, silver linings, "everything happens for a reason", growth-from-suffering stories | Affirmations hurt people with low self-esteem; accepting feelings protects; growth does not require suffering |
| Promises: "this will change your life", "40% of happiness is in your control", health benefits from positive thinking | Every effect here is small; the 40% figure is disowned by its authors |
| Abstract "why am I like this?" prompts, especially in the evening, and prompts that reopen settled themes | Abstract, judging self-focus is the brooding pattern that harms people who are already low |
| Stacking steps, streaks, penalties or "all or nothing" challenges | Accepting without doing predicts change in the wrong direction; harsh commitments leave most defaulters worse off |
| Treating money trouble, unemployment, discrimination or not fitting in as personal failings | Causes are largely structural; belonging work only helps where belonging is possible ([Stanford News on Walton et al. 2023](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2023/05/improving-student-success-social-belonging)) |
| Western defaults: independence over duty, excitement over calm, a nuclear family, a partner, a formal job with a boss and a salary | Most of the world lives in extended families and works informally; culture shapes ideals |
| Inferring or storing ethnicity, sexuality, gender identity, religion, party, class or generation | Labels are shaped by safety; generation predicts little; such data is highly sensitive in many jurisdictions and needs legal review |
| Pushing openness, forgiveness, trust or reconciliation | Hiding can be a rational safety choice; caution is rational where exploitation is common; forgiving is not reconciling |
| Taking sides on religion or politics, or treating doubt as a problem to fix | Benefits depend on fit and community, not content; being "in between" is often a passage |
| Promising that everything will be all right | Grounded hope must rest on the person's own record and real opportunities |
| Becoming another passive feed | Scrolling goes with lower satisfaction; belonging matters four to six times more than cutting screen time |

## Conclusion

The four cravings the app's owner started with turn out to be four different kinds of thing, and that difference is the most useful single idea for designing it. Freedom and belonging are needs that everyone has whether or not they feel them loudly; security is a real need when it is missing but often a stand-in when freedom, competence or connection are frustrated; success is two opposite things wearing one name; and hope is less a feeling to be cheered into people than a skill built from evidence of their own agency. That means the app's most honest job is not to tell people what they crave, but to help them see which craving is driving a choice and whether the route they are taking actually reaches it, for example whether a hunger for more money is really a hunger for safety or respect.

The deeper opportunity is that the most reliable self-discoveries in this evidence are not hidden traits but predictable misjudgements that almost everyone makes: we think we are watched about twice as much as we are, that a refusal will land harder than it does, that strangers are less honest and the other side more hostile than they are, and that our thanks and compliments will be awkward rather than welcome. These are universal, require no comparison with anyone, take no side, and can be checked by the person's own small experiments, which is exactly the lopsided feedback that keeps pessimism alive and that an app can reverse. Combined with the finding that privacy and kindness are what make hard truths bearable, this points to a product whose effects will be modest and slow but real, provided it tests its own insights for specificity, checks its questions across cultures (since most of this evidence is Western and none tests the app's format), and measures what people do afterwards rather than how much they agree with what it says.
